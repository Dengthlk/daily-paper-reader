## ABSTRACT 

Long-horizon tasks require large language model (LLM) agents to coordinate interdependent actions, a capability central to automating complex workflows. Stepwise group-based reinforcement learning provides finer credit than trajectory-level supervision, but reliable comparisons must account for differences in in-teraction history. Enforcing this consistency within the limited rollouts of each task can leave insufficient support for estimating action preferences. We propose Beyond-Local Policy Optimization (BLPO), which estimates task-progress ad-vantages from experience shared across tasks and training updates. We summa-rize trajectory prefixes into task-progress states and abstract actions by their func-tion, allowing corresponding decisions from different tasks to contribute to shared estimates. To control bias and noise from heterogeneous tasks, we accumulate context-relative returns in a global memory with balanced aggregation and reli-ability weighting. These global advantages complement local comparisons over normalized actions and trajectory-level credit without a learned critic or additional rollouts. Experiments on ALFWorld and WebShop with 1.5B and 7B policies show improved task success over the evaluated baselines under matched training budgets. Further analyses show faster learning and shorter trajectories with little computational overhead. Code is available at github.com/Tdadada/BLPO .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language model (LLM) agents extend automated assistance from answering individual queries to executing tasks in external environments (Yao et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024a). In applications such as travel planning and computational research, they offer the prospect of delegating entire workflows that otherwise require substantial human coordination (Shen et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b). Realizing this potential requires solving long-horizon tasks, where earlier actions establish prereq-uisites for later steps and environmental feedback informs subsequent decisions (Zhu et al., 2026). Agents must therefore coordinate their actions over extended interactions, making the ability to learn policies that sustain progress toward task completion central to their practical utility. Recent efforts to improve agents’ performance on long-horizon tasks have increasingly explored learning from interaction, particularly through reinforcement learning (RL) (Zhou et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025; He et al., 2026b). Among these approaches, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) compares trajectory returns to optimize the policy without a separate critic (Shao et al., 2024). However, assigning one advantage to all actions can reinforce errors in successful trajectories and suppress useful steps in failed ones. When all rollouts receive the same failure reward, their relative advantages vanish entirely (Yang et al., 2026). To distinguish individual contributions, GiGPO compares actions taken from matching observed states across rollouts (Feng et al., 2025). Such comparisons can nevertheless mix different interaction histories, motivating HGPO to construct groups with varying degrees of historical consistency and adaptively combine their advantages (He et al., 2026a). 1arXiv:2611.03005v1 [cs.AI] 2 Nov 2026 Preprint Despite these refinements, requiring greater historical consistency can leave the limited rollouts of a task insufficient for reliable comparisons (He et al., 2026a). ECPO improves the reliability of local action estimates (Li et al., 2026a), while BiPACE expands comparison groups by aggregating similar states (Wang et al., 2026); both still estimate credit within sampled rollout groups. Experience from related tasks offers additional support. Histories involving different entities may represent equivalent task progress, while different concrete actions may serve the same function. Grouping decisions on this basis allows comparisons to accumulate across tasks and training updates. Such sharing, how-ever, must separate action effects from differences in task difficulty and prevent frequently visited contexts from dominating the estimates. Reliable global credit therefore requires correcting these biases while controlling how strongly uncertain comparisons influence policy updates. Building on this perspective, we propose Beyond-Local Policy Optimization (BLPO), which esti-mates task-progress advantages by pooling experience across tasks and training updates. To con-struct these comparisons, predefined environment-specific rules summarize task progress from prior interactions and map concrete actions to their goal-relative functions. Applied automatically across task instances, these rules define comparison groups, while their action preferences are estimated from rollout returns. To control the heterogeneity introduced by sharing, we center returns within concrete contexts and balance their contributions to a decaying statistical memory. Preferences de-rived from this memory are weighted by their support and uncertainty, limiting the influence of unreliable estimates. The resulting task-progress advantages complement local comparisons over normalized actions, while trajectory advantages preserve supervision of overall task completion. All three signals enter a unified policy objective without a learned critic or additional rollouts. Ex-periments on ALFWorld and WebShop show improved task success with 1.5B and 7B policies, including a 2.48 percentage point gain over HGPO on WebShop with the 1.5B backbone. Further analyses support cross-task experience sharing and show faster learning with little computational overhead. Our contributions are threefold: • We propose BLPO, a critic-free policy optimization method that reuses task-progress-aligned experience across tasks and training updates for step-level credit assignment, with-out additional rollouts. • We develop a context-balanced global advantage estimator that reduces bias from context difficulty and repeated visits. Support and uncertainty scale the resulting credit to limit the influence of unreliable comparisons. • Experiments on ALFWorld and WebShop show improved task success with both 1.5B and 7B policies. Controlled comparisons under the same abstraction support the benefits of cross-task sharing, while learning and cost analyses show faster ALFWorld learning with little additional computation. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Reinforcement Learning for LLM Agents. Research on agentic RL increasingly treats sustained interaction as the basis for policy learning. ArCHer combines utterance-level value estimation with token-level optimization (Zhou et al., 2024b), while RAGEN and Search-R1 develop training around multi-turn trajectories (Wang et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025). As training extends to longer interac-tions, AgentGym-RL introduces a curriculum that progressively increases interaction length (Xi et al., 2026). Complementing these algorithmic developments, Agent Lightning v1.0 addresses the systems integration of RL with existing agents while preserving the execution harness’s control of the interaction loop (He et al., 2026b). 

Credit Assignment in Agentic RL. Within these training frameworks, a central question is how task outcomes should supervise individual decisions. RLOO and GRPO offer relative estimates without a learned critic, but their direct trajectory-level application does not distinguish interme-diate actions (Ahmadian et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024). GiGPO makes these comparisons more granular by grouping decisions with matching observations (Feng et al., 2025). HGPO further condi-tions groups on interaction history, addressing inconsistencies hidden by observation matching (He et al., 2026a). Such finer comparisons also raise questions of statistical support and reliability. 2Preprint 

Figure 1: Overview of BLPO. (1) Trajectory histories and concrete actions from different tasks are mapped to shared task-progress states and action functions. (2) Within each concrete context, returns are centered and averaged per abstract state–action pair to update a global memory across training iterations. (3) Action contrasts estimated from this memory are scaled by effective support and uncertainty. The resulting task-progress advantages are combined with trajectory-relative ad-vantages and local advantages over normalized actions to optimize the policy. BiPACE increases support by grouping states in actor representation space (Wang et al., 2026), whereas ECPO calibrates comparisons over equivalent actions using support and variability (Li et al., 2026a). Alongside these group estimators, GraphGPO derives credit from goal distances in trajectory graphs (Cheng et al., 2026), while HCAPO refines action values through hindsight reasoning (Tan et al., 2026). 

Experience Reuse for Agent Learning. The experience available for learning can also extend beyond current rollouts. One approach supplies earlier experience as policy context through re-trieved step traces in RAPO and evolving skill documents in SkillRise (Zhang et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2026). Rather than exposing history through the policy input, TIGPO retains it in persistent transition graphs, combining past and current transitions for credit estimation (Gan, 2026). This form of temporal reuse remains confined to individual task instances. BLPO instead matches task progress and action function to pool comparisons across tasks and policy updates. To manage the resulting heterogeneity, we adjust returns within concrete contexts, balance their contributions, and attenuate uncertain comparisons.

BLPO achieves the best overall performance. Table 1 reports the highest mean success for BLPO on both benchmarks at both scales. With Qwen2.5-1.5B, ALFWorld Out-Success rises from HGPO’s 90.16% to 91.54%, and WebShop Task Success from 71.54% to 74.02%. Under matched rollout and update budgets, the 1.5B policy also surpasses 7B GRPO on all four metrics. 

Global experience complements local credit assignment. GiGPO distinguishes decisions within trajectories; HGPO further conditions comparisons on history. BLPO pools decisions with shared task progress and action function across tasks and updates. Its gains over HGPO suggest that broader support, with context adjustment and reliability weighting, complements history conditioning within individual tasks. 

Scaling and generalization. With the 7B backbone, BLPO reaches 97.14% In-Success and 92.97% Out-Success on ALFWorld, exceeding both GiGPO and HGPO. On WebShop, it attains a Task Score of 88.90, comparable to HGPO’s 88.96, while increasing Task Success from 78.51% to 79.43%. Although the WebShop tra-jectory ultimately fails, selecting the requested size still resolves a necessary constraint, separating this useful step from the final outcome. Evidence from these groups turns the mixed step advantage positive in both cases, aligning credit with the actions’ con-tribution to task progress. The global preferences remain positive after excluding the current task’s contributions, confirming support from other tasks. 9Preprint 

## 6 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Our results support global credit estimation that controls context difficulty and uncertainty, rather than increasing comparison support alone. The current instantiation uses environment-specific rules to align task progress and action functions, while sharing the statistical estimator across environ-ments. Applying BLPO to new domains calls for suitable comparison groups; learning transferable abstractions is a promising direction for broadening its applicability. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We introduced BLPO to estimate task-progress advantages from global experience while controlling context bias and uncertainty. Combined with trajectory and local credit, these advantages improve task success on ALFWorld and WebShop at both model scales. Faster learning and shorter ALF-World trajectories further support global experience as a useful source of decision-level supervision.

Construction principle. The rules follow a common construction principle across the two envi-ronments. We begin with task completion conditions and identify observable facts that track whether their prerequisites have been met. Task descriptions define goal-relative roles, while interaction his-tory and environment feedback update the relevant facts before each decision. States summarize task progress, and actions are mapped to their functions relative to the goal and current progress. This retains distinctions such as acquiring an unplaced target versus retaking a placed one, while allow-ing object identities to differ. The environment-specific rules are reused across task instances and executed automatically, without annotating individual trajectories. They define comparison groups rather than numerical advantages or additional progress rewards; action preferences are estimated from rollout returns. State construction uses only pre-action information and does not extend the policy input. 

ALFWorld history and progress states. Task metadata identifies the task family, target object, destination, and required tool. Placement tasks define a destination relation; Heat, Cool, and Clean add a required property; Pick2 requires two placements; Look requires inspection under light. We reconstruct the relevant facts by replaying accepted actions, tracking possession, object locations, completed transformations, placement counts, encountered entities, and container or tool states. Rejected actions leave these facts unchanged, and removing a previously placed instance decreases the placement count. The task-progress state combines the task family, the types of goal conditions, their progress repre-sentations, and their joint completion pattern. Each condition retains goal-relative relations, a coarse progress stage, and relevant facts such as whether the target is held or the required tool is accessible. Transformation stages distinguish an undiscovered target, discovery or possession, possession with a seen or accessible tool, placement in the tool, and completed transformation. Placement conditions separately retain whether delivery remains unfinished; Pick2 additionally records placement count, while Look retains target and lighting conditions. These stages are categorical grouping features and do not provide additional rewards. 

ALFWorld action functions. Commands are parsed into operations and arguments, with entities mapped to task roles such as target, destination, and tool. Functions distinguish navigation, acqui-13 Preprint sition, transformation, placement, inspection, and exploration. For Pick2, taking a target from the destination is classified as a retake rather than acquisition, separating actions that can undo com-pleted placement. 

WebShop decision frontiers. Fixed patterns extract options and price limits; normalized tokens represent the requested product. These constraints are compared with the observation and public page state, including query, selected options, clickable elements, and prior interaction counts. The task-progress state retains only the page type and active decision frontier, omitting concrete query and product identities. Frontier rules are evaluated in order. On a results page, an affordable candidate covering at least two-thirds of the target product tokens marks readiness to inspect a candidate. Otherwise, query coverage below two-thirds indicates a need for query refinement. If coverage is sufficient and another results page exists, the frontier instead indicates further result exploration. On an item page, excessive price or zero product-token overlap first marks the item as unsuitable. Missing or conflicting options then indicate a need for option selection when a requested choice is available; otherwise, the item is unsuitable. Once required options are satisfied, sufficient token coverage and an acceptable price mark readiness to purchase. Remaining cases indicate a need for information only if product details have not yet been inspected; otherwise, the frontier remains unresolved. An absent price limit imposes no price restriction. These checks use current public information, not a hidden purchase-success label. 

WebShop action functions. Action functions distinguish opening a strongly matching, affordable candidate from selecting a requested option that remains unmet. Conflicting or repeated selections are separated, as are inspection, purchase, backtracking, and result exploration.
