## SGR-OPSD: Learning from Subgoal Plans with Graph-Regularized Credit Assignment for Agentic Reinforcement Learning 

Shu Liu 1,2 * , Yang Chong 2, WenShi Chen 2, Man Lan 1†1School of Computer Science and Technology, East China Normal University

Abstract 

OPSD provides dense supervision for agentic reinforcement learning through privileged in-formation feedback. However, existing priv-ileged information often lacks explicit task-progression planning, while teacher–student log-probability gaps reflect shifts in model preference rather than true task progress. To address these limitations, we propose Subgoal-Guided Graph-Regularized OPSD (SGR-OPSD ), a framework that combines sub-goal supervision with interaction-derived evi-dence for turn-level credit assignment. Specif-ically, SGR-OPSD uses LLM-generated sub-goal plans to provide dense action-level guid-ance, converting them into advantage weights via a constrained, KL-regularized objective. To ground credit in observed outcomes, we construct a Subgoal Progress Graph that es-timates empirical returns from sampled tran-sition frequencies. The graph compares em-pirical action returns against the state aver-age to derive preferences that calibrate the teacher’s credit weights. Evaluated on ALF-World, WebShop, and Search-QA across two model scales (Qwen2.5-3B and 7B), SGR-OPSD consistently outperforms GRPO and strong self-distillation baselines—improving success rates over GRPO by up to 7.4 percent-age points and reaching 85.8% on ALFWorld with Qwen2.5-7B. Ablation studies confirm that subgoal guidance and graph-based weight calibration are key to these performance gains.

On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026a) provides dense feedback by conditioning a teacher model on privileged training-time information to score student-sampled actions (Lv et al., 2026; Lan et al., 2026). This feedback complements the sparse out-come rewards used in methods like Group Rel-ative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024), enabling the student to internalize inference-unavailable guidance. OPSD thus offers a promis-ing framework for dense supervision in agentic RL. Existing methods supply privileged informa-tion through trajectory-derived skills or reference answers, integrating teacher feedback via advan-tage reweighting or auxiliary distillation objec-tives (Wang et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026a; Lu et al., 2026). AgentOPSD further accumulates likelihood evidence across turns, while TASPO maps action-level support into bounded advantage weights (Wang et al., 2026b; Yang et al., 2026b). However, two key gaps remain between such su-pervision and true task progress. First, privileged guidance often lacks an explicit, ordered plan for the task at hand. For “cool bread and place it on the countertop” , a structured plan—locate the bread, cool it, then place it—makes the required progression explicit. Second, teacher–student log-arXiv:2611.02414v1 [cs.AI] 2 Nov 2026 probability gaps measure shifts in model preference rather than progress made through real interactions; even plan-conditioned feedback can incorrectly fa-vor actions that stray from successful continuations. Thus, effective credit assignment requires both task-specific planning information and a mechanism that grounds its influence in execution evidence. To bridge these gaps, our approach uses subgoal plans to structure teacher guidance and uses evidence from the Subgoal Progress Graph to calibrate the resulting credit. We propose SGR-OPSD , illustrated in Figure 1. An LLM generates a fixed subgoal plan from the task description alone, providing the scoring teacher with explicit task-progression guidance. We derive a constrained KL-regularized rule that converts action-level teacher–student likelihood ev-idence into bounded exponential weights on GRPO advantages. The rule accounts for advantage signs and enforces a fixed token-weighted budget within each trajectory, establishing a teacher reference for turn-level credit. To calibrate these weights, we construct a Subgoal Progress Graph from student rollouts and derive action preferences from empiri-cal Q − V : each action’s discounted return relative to the visit-weighted state average. A joint objec-tive incorporates these preferences while regulariz-ing the allocation toward the teacher reference, con-necting plan-guided supervision with evidence of task progress. The trained student requires neither the plan nor the graph at inference. We evaluate 

SGR-OPSD on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Search-QA with Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B. On the 7B model, it reaches 85.8% success on ALFWorld and 

78.2% on WebShop, improving over GRPO by 7.4 and 7.6 percentage points, respectively. Our contributions are as follows: • We propose leveraging subgoal plans as privi-leged supervision and derive a constrained KL-regularized formulation to allocate turn-level credit via bounded exponential weights. • We develop a Subgoal Progress Graph that de-rives action preferences from empirical return estimates to calibrate teacher-reference weights, while preserving advantage signs and a fixed token-weighted budget per trajectory. • Extensive experiments across three environments and two model scales demonstrate the superiority of SGR-OPSD , outperforming GRPO by up to 7.6 percentage points and achieving an 85.8% 

success rate on ALFWorld. 

2 Related Works 

2.1 On-Policy Self-Distillation for LLMs 

Self-distillation derives the teacher from the stu-dent and conditions it on privileged training-time information (Wang et al., 2026a,b). Existing works integrate diverse privileged signals via distinct mechanisms: Skill-SD leverages trajectory-derived skills (Wang et al., 2026a), RLSD scales token updates using log-probability gaps between an answer-conditioned teacher and the student (Yang et al., 2026a), and SDAR gates SkillBank guidance alongside RL (Lu et al., 2026). However, these priv-ileged signals often contain irrelevant noise and lack explicit task-progress planning. To address this, SGR-OPSD uses a fixed, LLM-generated sub-goal plan derived solely from the task description as privileged information and applies a constrained KL-regularized objective to transform action-level evidence into bounded turn weights. 

2.2 Credit Assignment for Multi-Turn Agents 

While GRPO provides trajectory-level advantages under outcome rewards, it fails to distinguish the contributions of individual turns (Shao et al., 2024). Recent lines of work refine this coarse outcome sig-nal: AgentOPSD accumulates privileged likelihood evidence via recursive belief updates (Wang et al., 2026b), TASPO converts action-level support into bounded advantage weights (Yang et al., 2026b), and AlignOPSD aligns sibling contexts to allocate credit across spans and turns (Chen et al., 2026). Although such teacher likelihood preferences offer dense feedback, they lack explicit measurements of task progress. To bridge this gap, SGR-OPSD 

constructs a Subgoal Progress Graph from student rollouts, leveraging graph-derived action compar-isons to calibrate turn-level credit from a subgoal-conditioned teacher.

3.1 Background 

We consider multi-turn tasks where an agent inter-acts with an environment to complete an instruction 

x. Given a task x, the rollout policy πθold samples 

K trajectories {τi}Ki=1 . Each trajectory τi consists of Ni turns indexed by t, with k indexing individual tokens within a response. At turn t, given context 

hSi,t , the agent generates a response yi,t containing action ai,t and receives the next observation. Outcome-based policy optimization assigns each turn a base advantage Ai,t , where positive values reinforce the sampled turn and negative values dis-courage it. Self-distillation supplements this sparse outcome feedback by re-scoring student-sampled trajectories using privileged information. Both scoring branches share the pre-update parameters 

θold , so their likelihood difference reflects the effect of privileged information. 

3.2 Subgoal-Guided Credit Weights Subgoal-conditioned supervision. Subgoal plans make the intermediate requirements of task comple-tion explicit. 4 Experiments 

We evaluate SGR-OPSD across three interactive environments and two model scales to examine whether subgoal-guided, graph-calibrated credit improves policy learning. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Benchmarks and metrics. For ALFWorld (Shrid-har et al., 2020), we evaluate success on the 140-task seen split across six household task categories: Pick, Look, Clean, Heat, Cool, and Pick2. For WebShop (Yao et al., 2022), we evaluate product search and selection on 500 goals, reporting task score and full-task success rate. SearchQA fol-lows Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025); we report exact-match accuracy on 51,713 questions across NQ, TriviaQA, PopQA, HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, MuSiQue, and Bamboogle. Together, these envi-ronments test sequential state changes, constraint satisfaction, and evidence gathering. 

Baselines. We compare with Vanilla and Skill-Prompt as training-free references, GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as the outcome-based learner, and OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026b), GRPO+OPSD, Skill-SD (Wang et al., 2026a), RLSD (Yang et al., 2026a), SDAR (Lu et al., 2026), and Agen-tOPSD (Wang et al., 2026b) as self-distillation baselines. Qwen3.5-Plus and DeepSeek-V3.1 pro-vide additional closed-source model references. 

Training configuration. We use the Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct backbones and carry out three independent training runs with seeds {0, 42 , 2026 }. We train SGR-OPSD on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs for 150 policy updates, with eight rollouts per task and a learning rate of 10 −6.The maximum interaction lengths are 50, 15, and 4 turns for ALFWorld, WebShop, and SearchQA, respectively. We set ϵ = 0 .1, T = TG = 1 ,

β = 2 , μ = 0 .02 , and γ = 0 .1. We provide subgoal-generation prompts in Appendix A and environment-specific training and evaluation set-tings in Appendix C.

4.3 Component Ablations 

The ablations examine three components: the Sub-goal Progress Graph, teacher evidence, and task-specific subgoal plans (Tables 2 and 3). The met-rics are ALFWorld macro-average seen success, SearchQA macro-average exact-match accuracy, and WebShop success rate. 

Role of graph calibration. We introduce SGR-OPSD , which combines subgoal-conditioned teacher evidence with calibra-tion by the Subgoal Progress Graph to allocate credit across agent turns. It improves over GRPO at both 3B and 7B, reaching 85.6% ALFWorld macro-average success and 78.2% WebShop suc-cess at 7B, supporting structured credit assignment. 

Limitations 

Evaluation scope. We evaluate SGR-OPSD on three benchmarks using Qwen2.5-3B/7B-Instruct. Its applicability to other model families and open-ended environments requires further evaluation, particularly when observations and action spaces differ from those studied here. 

Fixed subgoal guidance. Subgoal plans are generated from task instructions before interaction and remain fixed across turns. They may omit requirements or fail to accommodate new informa-tion revealed by the environment. Updating plans from observed feedback is a natural extension of the current framework. 

Rollout-dependent graph evidence. The Sub-goal Progress Graph estimates empirical action val-ues from recorded transitions and outcomes; com-parison coverage depends on exploration and the availability of matched action visits. When eli-gible comparisons are absent, the method retains the teacher reference. Graph scores summarize progress within sampled rollouts rather than es-tablish true action values; broader exploration and more reliable state matching are directions for ex-tending this evidence.

B Implementation Details of Credit Allocation 

This appendix specifies the scoring, weighting, and graph conventions used by the allocation in Sec-tion 3. 

B.1 Scoring Context and Action Tokens 

The teacher prompt prepends the cached task plan to the student prompt and is left-truncated to the environment-specific context limit. Consequently, 

hTi,t and hSi,t can retain different parts of the in-teraction context. Both branches use the same pre-update actor and score the original sampled response IDs. Action-token probabilities remain conditioned on earlier reasoning tokens; Equation 1 is not a probability marginalized over reasoning. The action mask selects original response tokens that overlap the trimmed executable-action span, including tokens crossing its byte boundaries. The span is not retokenized. A turn without a score-able action receives di,t = 0 and is excluded from graph comparisons. Its outcome penalty and full-response policy update remain active. Such a turn still participates in its trajectory’s allocation budget, so its multiplier need not remain one.

D.1 Independent Progress Verification Agreement with independent outcomes. Pref-erences from the Subgoal Progress Graph agree with independent continuation returns on 49/60 matched ALFWorld action pairs (81.7%), com-pared with 30/60 (50.0%) for teacher evidence. Graph-preferred actions also achieve higher mean discounted return and continuation success (Ta-ble 10). They succeed in 1,756/1,920 continuations, compared with 1,702/1,920 for their alternatives. At the task level, success is higher for 22 pairs, lower for nine, and tied for 29. On the 31 pairs with unequal success rates, graph preferences agree with the success ordering on 22 pairs (71.0%), com-pared with 16 (51.6%) for teacher evidence. Return agreement evaluates all 60 pairs and captures both completion and its timing. 

Independent continuation protocol. The eval-uation fixes Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct at update 150, selected by training-validation success (82.03%), and constructs graphs from eight rollouts for each of 140 ALFWorld tasks. Candidate actions must be legal and share the exact simulator-state signa-ture, student-prompt hash, and remaining budget. The sampling protocol requires an absolute graph-score difference above 10 −8 and positive recorded reliability for at least one source visit. The latter requires that its state–action pair occur in at least two trajectories, have nonzero empirical advantage, and appear in at least 16 of 64 trajectory-bootstrap replicates. Each replicate resamples the eight tra-jectories with replacement. These criteria select the evaluation pool; allocation uses the objective and pair weights defined in Appendix B. The pool contains 311 action pairs across 94 tasks. Using selection seed 20261027, we choose one pair uniformly within each task, reserve eight disjoint tasks for replay checks, and select 60 for-mal tasks proportionally across the remaining task categories. The state and action manifest is frozen before continuation sampling. For each pair, we restore the recorded state and history, force either action, and sample 32 continuations per action with the remaining interaction budget. Sampling uses temperature 1, top-p 1, no top-k restriction, and at most 512 response tokens per turn. Request seeds differ between graph construction and continua-tion evaluation; the inference-engine initialization seed is 0. D.2 Credit Calibration Analysis Improved outcome alignment. Calibration us-ing Subgoal Progress Graph preferences increases agreement with independent returns from 8/21 (38.1%) to 16/21 (76.2%) on the same directly eli-gible pairs, with Wilson intervals [20.8%, 59.1%] and [54.9%, 89.4%] (Table 12). Across all 60 pairs, calibration yields eight additional correct rankings: nine corrections and one change from correct to in-correct. The paired gain is 13.3 percentage points over all 60 pairs (95% task-bootstrap interval [3.3, 23.3]). Thirty-five pairs involve zero-advantage visits and remain tied under both reference and cal-ibrated credit; four further pairs fall outside the exact direct-comparison conditions. The table re-ports correctness and ties for both the complete set and the directly eligible subset.
