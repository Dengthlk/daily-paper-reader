## ABSTRACT 

The performance of large language model (LLM) agents depends on both the un-derlying model and the harness that governs interaction with tools and the envi-ronment. Yet harness design remains largely manual, and automating it requires navigating an open-ended search space through costly end-to-end evaluations. We introduce HARNESS FLOW , a framework for optimizing complete executable har-nesses under a limited candidate-evaluation budget, with the model and task en-vironment held fixed. H ARNESS FLOW organizes candidates and their evaluation histories in a refinement tree, using a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)-based policy to balance exploitation and exploration. A coding agent refines selected harnesses using archived code, execution feedback, and a library of reusable har-ness mechanisms. With 15 refinement attempts per benchmark, H ARNESS FLOW 

achieves the highest mean test scores among the compared methods on six bench-marks spanning agentic tasks, coding, and web interaction. The optimized har-nesses achieve an average score of 69 .48% , outperforming the initial harnesses and Meta-Harness by 10 .89 and 3.95 percentage points, respectively, while incur-ring lower average per-task execution costs than hand-engineered baselines. These results suggest that structured harness search offers a promising route toward au-tomating the design of capable and efficient LLM agents. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

The performance of LLM agents depends not only on the underlying model but also on the har-ness, the executable system that governs access to information, maintains state, invokes tools, and coordinates execution (Yang et al., 2024b; Shang et al., 2025; Khattab et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025). Prior studies of context management and multi-agent systems show that these mechanisms can sub-stantially affect agent behavior (Packer et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023), highlighting harness design as a practical means of improving agent performance without modifying model weights. However, harness design remains largely manual and expertise-intensive, making effective designs difficult to adapt and reuse across tasks and domains (Zhang et al., 2025b). Recent work has sought to automate harness design and refinement to reduce reliance on manual engineering (Zhuge et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; 2025a; Yue et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2026). Despite this progress, efficient optimization remains challenging: the harness de-sign space is open-ended, and evaluating each candidate requires costly end-to-end agent execution. HARBOR (Sengupta & Wang, 2026), PRISM (Ruan et al., 2026), and Self-Harness (Zhang et al., 2026a) refine harnesses through predefined configurations or bounded edits. Meta-Harness (Lee et al., 2026) permits full-harness code refinement, while AutoSaddler (Park et al., 2026) organizes durable updates from agent execution traces. DarwinX (Zhang et al., 2026d) explores harness pop-ulations through selection and preservation checks. These approaches motivate the study of search organization under a limited evaluation budget, leading to a central question: How can we systemat-ically search over complete executable harnesses while making effective use of a limited candidate-evaluation budget?

To address this challenge, we introduce HARNESS FLOW , a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)-based framework (Browne et al., 2012) designed to systematically explore and discover optimal harnesses under a limited evaluation budget. First, it represents each harness as a complete exe-cutable codebase that a coding agent can inspect and modify in a filesystem workspace, enabling coordinated changes across the entire harness (Chen et al., 2026a; Lee et al., 2026). It also pro-vides the coding agent with a Harness Component Library of reusable mechanisms that can be adapted, composed, or extended during refinement. Second, it reformulates harness optimization as a tree-structured search problem over these executable codebases: nodes represent harness imple-mentations, and edges record code-level modifications. The tree preserves earlier designs together with their evaluation scores and execution traces, providing structured experience that the coding agent can inspect and reuse across branches. Finally, it employs an MCTS-based selection policy to determine which eligible candidate in this tree to refine next, while the coding agent determines how to modify it. The policy combines measured performance with prior expansion counts to balance exploitation of promising designs with exploration of underexplored refinement paths (Auer et al., 2002; Kocsis & Szepesvári, 2006). We conduct extensive evaluations on six established benchmarks spanning agentic tasks (TAU2-bench and AppWorld), code generation (BigCodeBench-Hard and SWE-bench Verified), and web interaction (WebShop and BrowseComp-Plus). The results demonstrate that H ARNESS FLOW is (i) high-performing , achieving the highest average test score of 69 .48% in the main comparison and improving over Vanilla by 10 .89 percentage points; (ii) cost-efficient , running at a lower average inference cost than every hand-engineered harness, at 41 .4% of Codex’s across the six benchmarks; and (iii) sample-efficient , attaining a mean best-so-far validation score of 71 .5% after three candi-date evaluations per benchmark and 74 .2% by round 15 .In summary, our key contributions are as follows: • We formulate full-harness optimization as a tree-structured search problem over complete executable codebases under a limited candidate-evaluation budget. • We introduce H ARNESS FLOW , an MCTS-based framework that uses a coding agent, tree-structured experience, and a Harness Component Library to generate full-harness refine-ments, while an explicit selection policy allocates the search budget across alternative de-signs. • We conduct extensive evaluations on six benchmarks, demonstrating that H ARNESS FLOW

## 2 PROBLEM SETTING 

Harness and Search Space. Given a fixed model m, task environment, and evaluation protocol, a 

harness h is an executable program that governs how the model interacts with tools and the environ-ment. Let H denote the space of harnesses compatible with this setting. Refinements may modify mechanisms for context construction, state management, tool use, and termination, as well as their composition and the overall execution structure. For example, adding a verification step may require retaining additional state and revising the stopping rule. The search therefore operates over complete executable harnesses rather than a predefined set of prompts, configurations, or components. 

Harness Evaluation and Optimization Objective. For a task set D = {(x, y )}, x denotes a task instance and y the corresponding reference answer or success criterion. We write Agent( m, h, x )

for the end-to-end outcome produced by running harness h with model m on x. Depending on the task, this outcome may be a final response, a generated artifact, or the terminal state of an interactive environment. A benchmark-specific metric r(·, ·) compares this outcome with y and returns a normalized score in [0 , 1] . We define harness performance as 

s(h | D ) = E(x,y )∼D [r(Agent( m, h, x ), y )] , (1) where tasks are sampled uniformly from D. In practice, this score is estimated by averaging repeated executions on the task set. It measures end-to-end harness performance rather than individual model calls. The resulting scores support candidate comparison, while execution traces provide diagnostic evidence for subsequent refinements. Given a validation set Dv, the ideal optimization objective is

Budgeted Optimization Protocol. Evaluating a harness on Dv is costly, as each task may require multiple model and tool calls. We therefore consider a sequential optimization protocol initialized from a harness h0 and constrained by a budget of N refinement attempts. At each attempt, the op-timizer draws on accumulated validation scores and execution traces to select an existing harness for refinement and propose a new candidate. The central challenge is to allocate refinement effort across alternative paths while further developing promising designs. At the end of search, we select the harness with the highest observed score on Dv among the candidates produced during optimiza-tion. The selected harness is then evaluated on a disjoint held-out test set Dt, and the resulting score is reported as its final performance. 33 METHODOLOGY 

HARNESS FLOW implements the protocol in Section 2 by separating parent selection from code refinement. An MCTS-based policy determines which harness to refine, while a coding agent de-termines how to modify it. The policy allocates search effort using validation scores and expansion counts; the coding agent uses prior execution experience and general, reusable harness mechanisms to develop new candidates. Each evaluation contributes evidence that can inform subsequent selec-tion and refinement. Code-Represented Harnesses. Each candidate is represented by a complete executable codebase. Refinement may add, remove, or jointly modify mechanisms for context construction, state manage-ment, control flow, and tool use. This allows a single proposal to coordinate changes across interact-ing mechanisms, supporting both local improvements and broader architectural changes within the search space defined in Section 2. 

Experience-Guided Code Refinement. The coding agent develops each candidate from a se-lected parent harness, guided by accumulated search experience. We denote the experience archive by A; it contains prior implementations, code modifications, validation feedback, task-level execu-tion records, and refinement relationships. The parent supplies the starting design, while the archive provides empirical evidence about the effects of earlier changes. The agent can selectively retrieve relevant code and feedback to compare designs, diagnose failures, and identify refinements, without requiring the entire search history to fit in its context. 

Harness Component Library. The Harness Component Library L offers reusable code for con-text construction, memory and state tracking, control flow, and tool interfacing (Figure 5 in the appendix). It includes observation compaction and skill catalogs; note stores, observation logs, and request tracking; loop and workflow runners; and argument-schema checks with heuristic tool-effect classification. The coding agent can inspect a read-only snapshot and is instructed to copy adopted code and local dependencies into the candidate, not import the snapshot at runtime. It may adapt or compose these implementations or write new code. The library thus offers optional implementation references rather than a fixed template or an exhaustive search space; the experience archive supplies empirical feedback for refinement. 3.2 MCTS-G UIDED TREE SEARCH 

After n refinement attempts, the search retains evaluated candidates in a rooted tree Gn = ( Vn, En).Each node ui ∈ V n represents a harness hi together with its validation score qi and execution records Ri; the root u0 represents the initial harness h0. An edge (ui, u j ) indicates that hj was generated by refining hi. Each evaluated harness and its measured score remain unchanged as the tree grows, preserving earlier designs and alternative refinement paths. Expansion counts and accumulated experience evolve with the search. H ARNESS FLOW adapts the MCTS cycle (Browne et al., 2012) through selection, expansion, evaluation, and experience backpropagation.

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks. We evaluate on six established benchmarks spanning agentic tasks, code generation, and web interaction: TAU2-bench (Barres et al., 2025), AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024), SWE-bench Verified (Jimenez et al., 2024), BigCodeBench-Hard (Zhuo et al., 2025), WebShop (Yao et al., 2022a), and BrowseComp-Plus (Chen et al., 2026b). Each benchmark is split once, with a fixed seed, into a validation set used during search and a disjoint test set used only for the final evaluation (92/186, 105/210, 100/200, 100/200, 100/200, and 100/200 tasks, respectively). The test set is never observed by the optimizer. 

Baselines. We compare against three categories of methods. (i) Vanilla , the initial harness from which search starts: the benchmark’s own reference agent implementation, which is also the root node of the search tree. (ii) Production agent harnesses that are engineered by hand and widely used: Claude Code (Anthropic, 2026), Codex (OpenAI, 2026a), OpenHands (Wang et al., 2025), and Hermes (Nous Research, 2026). We evaluate the official implementations at fixed versions, using the same backbone model, access to benchmark tools, and per-task resource limits as H ARNESS FLOW .Appendix C.2 documents the origin of every initial harness, and Appendix C.3 the exact releases and invocation of the production agents. (iii) Meta-Harness (Lee et al., 2026), a harness-optimization method that, like ours, permits full-code modification of a complete harness, but refines proposals iteratively from execution feedback rather than maintaining a persistent search tree. It uses the same initial harness, coding agent, benchmark, candidate budget, and evaluation protocol. 

Metrics. Following prior LLM agent work (Zhou et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2026a), we report each benchmark’s official metric in percent: task success rate for TAU2-bench and AppWorld; task score for WebShop; resolution rate for SWE-bench Verified; Pass@1 for BigCodeBench-Hard; and answer accuracy for BrowseComp-Plus. 

Implementation Details. Unless stated otherwise, all agents execute with GPT-5.4-mini (Ope-nAI, 2026c) at medium reasoning effort as the backbone model, and optimization uses a budget of 

N = 15 candidate attempts. Every method is executed R = 5 times over each split, during search and at test time alike, and tables report the mean of those five executions ± their sample standard deviation. For the policy in Section 3.2, we use ε = 0 .35 , α = 1 , and automatically determined 

τ . 4.3 ABLATION STUDY 

Library and Initialization. Removing the Harness Component Library lowers the three-benchmark average from 65 .03 to 63 .39 (Table 2), primarily through TAU2-bench ( 81 .83 → 77 .64 ), with small changes on AppWorld and BigCodeBench-Hard. Without the library, search still im-proves all three benchmarks over Vanilla by 8.63 points on average. Reusable components com-plement the search procedure, with the clearest gains on TAU2-bench. Starting from OpenHands likewise improves all three benchmarks, reaching an average of 62 .61 . The gains therefore extend to another agent architecture. 

Exploration–Exploitation Balance. To examine the contribution of each selection term, we vary 

ε in Equation 5 while keeping the top-K shortlisting rule ( K = 5 ), a budget of N = 15 candidate attempts, and other settings fixed. With ε = 0 , parent sampling uses only the score-based softmax distribution; with ε = 1 , it favors parents with fewer direct children. The default mixture ( ε = 0 .35 )achieves the highest mean test score on all three benchmarks (Table 3), averaging 79 .33 versus 73 .93 

for exploitation alone and 73 .65 for exploration alone, gains of 5.40 and 5.68 percentage points, respectively. Improvements are larger on TAU2-bench and SWE-bench Verified than on AppWorld. ## 5 RELATED WORK 

Automated Harness Optimization. Automatic optimization spans several levels of the agent stack. Prompt and LM-program optimizers use task feedback to improve instructions and computation-graph components (Yang et al., 2024a; Khattab et al., 2024; Yuksekgonul et al., 2024). Structural search extends the editable object to agent modules, graph connectivity, and communi-cation topology (Zhuge et al., 2024; Shang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024); evolutionary multi-agent generation and adaptive architecture or model routing further explore how agents are assem-bled and assigned computation (Yuan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Yue et al., 2025). At the code level, ADAS generates executable agent designs using an archive of prior discoveries, while AFlow combines MCTS with code-represented workflow refinement (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b). Within harness optimization, HARBOR searches configurations, PRISM targets prompts and guarded tool-boundary middleware, and Self-Harness validates failure-driven changes to an agent’s operating harness (Sengupta & Wang, 2026; Ruan et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a). Code-level methods include SICA, which edits its own implementation, and Meta-Harness, whose pro-poser inspects archived code, scores, and traces (Robeyns et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). Agentic Harness Engineering, HarnessCompass, and AutoSaddler further structure execution evidence, edit constraints, and validation to guide harness updates (Lin et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c; Park et al., 2026). Archive- and lineage-based search also appears in the Huxley–Gödel Machine and DarwinX (Wang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026d), while Hyperagents makes the improvement procedure itself editable (Zhang et al., 2026b). These studies establish both code-level refinement and explicit search over retained designs. H ARNESS FLOW builds on these directions by coupling a fixed coding-agent proposer with MCTS-guided parent selection, filesystem-grounded experience reuse, and reusable harness mechanisms to allocate a limited candidate-evaluation budget across full-harness refinement paths. Further related work on harness engineering is discussed in Appendix A.

HARNESS FLOW optimizes complete agent harnesses through MCTS-guided search and coding-agent refinement. Harness Engineering. LLM agents depend on runtime mechanisms that organize reasoning, re-tain state, coordinate model calls, and mediate environment interaction. Reasoning–acting loops, verbal feedback, and inference-time search provide complementary ways to structure task execu-tion (Yao et al., 2022b; Madaan et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023). Memory-centered architectures retain, summarize, and retrieve past experiences to support long-horizon behavior (Park et al., 2023; Packer et al., 2023). Multi-agent frameworks introduce role assignment, structured communica-tion, and coordinated workflows (Li et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024). Software-engineering agents further demonstrate the importance of tool interfaces and execution environ-ments (Yang et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2025), while HarnessX organizes composable components for adaptive harness construction (Chen et al., 2026a). Together, these studies identify runtime mechanisms and their composition as important determinants of agent behavior beyond the under-lying model. Our work takes the complete harness implementation as the unit of optimization, allowing these interacting mechanisms to be refined jointly. The closest search-based precedent is AFlow (Zhang et al., 2025b), which also applies MCTS to code-represented candidates. It differs from our setting in two ways. First, its search space is the workflow: how a fixed vocabulary of operators is wired together, which is one layer of a harness. The execution loop, state handling, tool interfaces, and environment mediation stay fixed, and it is precisely these layers that the mechanisms above, and the harnesses selected by our search, modify. Second, its candidates are produced by a single model call that rewrites the current workflow from a textual summary of past changes and scores, whereas our proposer is a coding agent that runs the harness, reads its execution evidence, and makes coordinated edits across files. AFlow is accordingly evaluated on single-turn question answering, mathematics, and code generation, whereas the interactive benchmarks studied here are dominated by mechanisms that lie outside the workflow layer.
