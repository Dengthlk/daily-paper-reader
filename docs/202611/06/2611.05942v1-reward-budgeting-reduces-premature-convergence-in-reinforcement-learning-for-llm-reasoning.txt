# Reward Budgeting Reduces Premature Convergence in Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning 

Mengni Jia 2, Mengyu Zhou †1, Xiaoxi Jiang 1 and Guanjun Jiang 1 

> 1Qwen Business Unit, Alibaba, 2University of Cambridge

RL for LLM reasoning often reaches an early performance plateau as the policy distribution becomes increasingly sharp. Recent work has studied policy entropy as both a diagnostic and a control signal for this behavior. In our experiments, however, increasing entropy alone does not reliably change the eventual plateau. We analyze RL through its implicit target policy and derive a sharpness factor 𝜅 , together with an effective counterpart 𝜅 eff , that describes how strongly reward or advantage gaps are translated into probability-ratio gaps. Motivated by this view, we introduce reward budgeting , a plug-in advantage construction that assigns a fixed total reward budget to each prompt. For binary rewards and independent on-policy rollouts, its unclipped score-function estimator follows the gradient of an exact finite-𝐺 concave objective with diminishing returns, while its advantage gap decreases as more rollouts become correct. For general rewards, a shifted log-mean interpretation holds asymptotically under the stated regularity conditions. These results motivate, rather than establish exact objective equivalence for, the clipped implementations. On Qwen3-8B and Llama3.2-3B-Instruct, reward budgeting consistently improves a range of RL baselines, with gains up to +12.50 on AMC23 and +10.00 on AIME24 and AIME25. Overall, our results support an update-dynamics view of premature convergence and show that strong gains can be obtained without explicitly preserving entropy. 

# 1. Introduction 

Policies trained with RL for LLM reasoning often improve rapidly at first and then enter a plateau where further training brings little gain. Such a plateau need not reflect an intrinsic limit of the model or data: optimization choices can change the performance reached under otherwise similar training conditions. For example, [12] report that, in their scaling study, GSPO [35] and CISPO [18] reach higher fitted asymptotic performance than DAPO [31]. This motivates us to distinguish natural convergence from algorithm-induced premature convergence : training may stop making useful progress, even when a different algorithm can reach a better solution.

This phenomenon has been commonly referred to as “en-tropy collapse”. This name arises from the observation that the policy seems to stop learning and converge prematurely once its entropy drops to a relatively low level [3]; see Fig-ure 1. Consequently, many proposed remedies focus on explicit 

entropy control, typically by encouraging higher entropy or keeping it within a prescribed range [3, 26, 2, 24, 10, 4, 7, 32, 25, 19, 28, 20, 5, 13, 33, 31, 22, 30, 32, 15, 11]; see 2 for a discussion. These studies provide substantial evidence that entropy is related to RL training dynamics. Here, we ask a narrower question: is changing entropy itself sufficient to change the eventual performance plateau? 

Our results suggest caution. First, the familiar entropy– accuracy pattern is often observed after averaging across runs, while individual methods have different entropy trajectories (§3.3). Second, in the intervention study of §3.1, two different entropy-promoting interventions

substantially change entropy during early training, yet the trajectories return toward the same baseline behavior after the interventions are removed and do not reach a better final plateau. This does not imply that entropy-based methods cannot be effective: such methods may alter optimization in useful ways beyond simply raising entropy. It does suggest that, in our setting, entropy promotion alone is not a sufficient intervention for premature convergence. Conceptually, entropy measures how dispersed the policy distribution is, but it does not indicate whether the resulting responses are correct or whether their diversity is useful. A high-entropy policy can produce diverse but unproductive outputs, while a low-entropy policy can be confidently correct. We therefore view entropy as an informative diagnostic of policy concentration rather than, by itself, an explanation of why training plateaus. For this reason, we use the term premature convergence rather than entropy collapse throughout the paper. Once we question whether entropy is the primary cause behind the plateau, a natural question follows: if we do not control entropy directly, how can we sustain meaningful learning signals so that training can continue to realize incremental gains? In fact, reduced entropy (and the resulting sharper policy distribution) is expected, since it indicates that the policy is learning and becoming more confident on the training data. We just wish to avoid improper sharpening that locks the policy into cheap pseudo-modes and leaves little room for further gains. This matters because RL is inherently mode-seeking , and once a policy collapses onto a narrow set of modes, it becomes empirically difficult to re-expand it [14]. However, simply delaying convergence does 

not guarantee stronger performance: gentler dynamics can slow training and require more data for sufficient learning. What we need is a mechanism that maintains gentler training dynamics to prevent premature sharpening, while adaptively relaxing control when genuine learning signals appear. To describe the training dynamics more directly, we analyze RL through its implicit target policy in §3. For KL-regularized RL, the optimal target takes the reward-weighted form 𝜋 ∗ (𝛾 ) ∝ 𝜋 ref (𝛾 ) exp (𝑅 (𝛾 )/ 𝛽 ). This view isolates a sharpness factor 𝜅 , which determines how strongly reward gaps are amplified into probability-ratio gaps between higher- and lower-reward responses. We further define an effective version, 𝜅 eff , for practical algorithms with normalized advantages, clipping, and other implicit constraints. In this sense, 𝜅 and 𝜅 eff 

describe the sharpening pressure induced by the update itself, rather than the resulting uncertainty of the policy. Motivated by this, we introduce reward budgeting . For each prompt, we allocate a fixed total reward budget across sampled rollouts according to their positive reward gaps. In the binary-reward setting, if 𝑘 out of 𝐺 rollouts are correct, each correct rollout receives a reward share proportional to 1 /𝑘 . As a result, the advantage gap decreases as a prompt becomes easier, producing stronger sharpening when successes are rare and gentler sharpening when successes are already common. Importantly, this behavior emerges without explicitly constraining policy entropy. We also discuss reference-free optimization in §4.2: removing a fixed KL reference avoids permanently anchoring the target policy to a static prior, while importance-ratio clipping can still provide local regularization. Theoretically, reward budgeting admits a principled interpretation rather than a heuristic tweak. First, it regulates the 𝜅 trajectory, distinguishing it from most existing methods. As more rollouts become correct, each correct rollout receives a smaller share of the reward, which implicitly reduces the sharpness factor 𝜅 

and automatically relaxes the sharpening pressure. Second, under binary rewards, which match our main RLVR experiments, the expected update of the proposed estimator is exactly the gradient of a finite-𝐺 concave objective with diminishing returns (§A.3). Thus, repeatedly solving an already-easy prompt contributes progressively less marginal benefit than under a linear expected-reward objective. Under general rewards, the gap-normalized estimator admits a shifted log-mean interpretation in the large-𝐺 limit under suitable support assumptions (§A.4). Empirically, our method substantially improves on Qwen3-8B and Llama3.2-3B-Instruct across various RL baselines (up to +12.50 on AMC 23 and +10.00 on AIME24 & AIME25). The gains do not require higher entropy; after applying reward budgeting, entropy often decreases while final performance improves. Collectively, these results suggest that regulating algorithm-induced sharpening is a practical way to mitigate premature convergence, while entropy remains a useful diagnostic rather than a necessary quantity to preserve.

# 2. Related Work 

Premature convergence in RL for LLM reasoning is often accompanied by a rapid decline in policy entropy. Recent studies have examined this behavior from different perspectives, including entropy dynamics, token selection, reward shaping, and regularization. While these approaches share an interest in improving exploration, they differ in how entropy enters the policy update. 

Entropy Dynamics and Token-Level Interventions. [3] establish an empirical relationship between policy entropy and validation performance, and analyze entropy changes through the covariance between action log-probabilities and policy updates. Their analysis motivates Clip-Cov and KL-Cov, which selectively constrain high-covariance token updates to slow entropy reduction. From another perspective, [26] find that a small fraction of high-entropy tokens act as important forks in reasoning. They show that restricting policy-gradient updates to these tokens can match or outperform full-token training. Both works highlight the importance of where and how policy updates are applied. However, they focus on different signals: covariance characterizes contributions to entropy change, while token entropy identifies uncertain decision points. Our analysis instead examines how the RL objective and advantage construction determine the sharpening pressure of policy updates. 

Entropy-Guided Credit Assignment. Other studies use entropy to improve the learning signal rather than directly regularizing the policy distribution. [2] associate high-entropy regions with exploratory reasoning behaviors, such as reflection and correction. They add a clipped, gradient-detached entropy term to token advantages, encouraging such behaviors without directly optimizing an entropy bonus. Similarly, [24] propose GTPO and GRPO-S to address coarse-grained credit assignment in GRPO. Their methods use entropy to assign greater rewards to uncertain decisions in successful responses and stronger penalties to confident errors, at the token and sequence levels, respectively. These approaches show that entropy can help identify where learning signals may be more useful. Our method also modifies reward allocation, but uses the relative rewards of rollouts within each prompt rather than their entropy. The goal is to adjust how strongly each prompt contributes to learning as its sampled success rate changes. 

Selective Entropy Regularization. Direct entropy regularization provides another way to encourage explo-ration, but increasing entropy indiscriminately can also destabilize training. [10] study this issue and propose SIREN, which combines top-𝑝 action masking, high-entropy token selection, and a self-anchored regularizer. Rather than simply maximizing entropy, SIREN maintains a more controlled level of uncertainty over selected actions and positions. This distinction is important: the effectiveness of entropy-based methods can depend on which parts of the policy are regularized and how the updates are constrained, not only on the resulting entropy level. 

Group-Relative Optimization and Reward Allocation. Our work is also related to the design of group-based RL objectives. GRPO [21] estimates advantages from relative rewards within each prompt group. Dr. GRPO [16] shows that group standard-deviation normalization can introduce difficulty-dependent weighting, and removes this normalization along with response-length normalization. DAPO [31] improves training through several changes, including dynamic sampling that excludes groups with uniformly correct or incorrect rollouts. These methods improve advantage estimation or group selection. In contrast, reward budgeting changes how rewards are allocated within each group. Instead of assigning each successful rollout an independent unit reward, it distributes a fixed budget across the group according to reward gaps. Under binary rewards, this makes the advantage gap decrease as the number of correct rollouts increases. 

Relation to Our Work. The above studies show that entropy can be useful for understanding and improving RL, but its role depends on how it is used. We examine a related question: whether increasing entropy itself is sufficient to change the final performance plateau. Our intervention experiments suggest that this is not reliable in the settings we study. We therefore focus on the sharpening pressure induced by policy updates, characterized by 𝜅 and its effective counterpart 𝜅 eff . Reward budgeting provides a simple way to adjust this pressure through advantage construction, without explicitly controlling entropy. Moreover, under binary rewards, its expected policy-gradient update corresponds to a finite-𝐺 concave objective with diminishing returns. Our results suggest that prompt-level reward allocation is another useful way to address premature convergence, alongside existing entropy-guided approaches.

# 3. Sharpness Analysis 

Premature convergence is common in RL training, but its underlying dynamics remain unclear. In this section, we examine policy sharpening as a more direct description of RL updates than entropy alone. In §3.1, we show that increasing entropy alone does not reliably improve the final performance plateau in our settings. In §3.2, we derive a sharpness factor 𝜅 from the RL objective, which characterizes how reward gaps are exponentially amplified in the implicit target policy. Finally, controlled experiments in §3.2.1 and individual-run analyses in §3.3 show how sharpening strength relates to convergence dynamics, while averaging can obscure inconsistencies in the entropy–accuracy relationship. 

3.1. Entropy 

Due to the observation that policies often plateau as their entropy drops, many remedies propose to control entropy. However, this correlation does not establish that entropy reduction causes the plateau. Before taking any action, we must first ask: is entropy truly the cause, or more like a correlate? This question is highly important, because treating the correlational signal as causal can lead to ineffective interventions. 3.3. The Averaging Effect 

When plotting entropy and accuracy, “entropy collapse” appears striking: accuracy plateaus as entropy drops. However, this observation is usually based on heavily averaged curves, such as 11 runs in [3] and 12 runs in our Figure 1. Although averaging reduces noise, it also introduces a subtle statistical risk : by smoothing away run-level heterogeneity, it can make weak or inconsistent trends appear visually robust. Therefore, averaged plots and individual runs can show markedly different trends. This distinction is particularly important for causal interpretation. The relationship between a strongly tied metric and accuracy should remain visible within individual runs, not only after averaging.

# 4. Method 

We propose a plug-and-play advantage construction method, reward budgeting , to regulate RL training dynamics. Despite its simplicity, reward budgeting has a principled theoretical effect. First, for binary rewards and independent on-policy rollouts, its unclipped score-function estimator follows the gradient of a concave finite-group objective. After subtracting a parameter-independent constant, this objective approaches the logarithm of success probability as 𝐺 → ∞ . Second, it yields a 𝜅 trajectory that is different from most existing RL methods which may better allocate training focus. Its empirical gains are further verified in §5. Reward budgeting changes how advantages are constructed and can be used with a fixed-reference KL penalty. Section 4.2 discusses the reference-free variant, which we evaluate separately in the Qwen3-8B GRPO ablation.

# 5. Experiment 

5.1. Setup Data. We train on DAPO-Math-17K [31] and evaluate on five benchmarks: AMC23 [1], AIME24 [9], AIME25 [34], MATH500 [8], and OlympiadBench (Physics) [6]. 

Models. We train (1) Llama3.2-3B-Instruct [17] and (2) Qwen3-8B [29]. 

Hyper-parameters. We sample eight rollouts per prompt and use 32 prompts per update. We train each method until its validation accuracy reaches a plateau. The benchmark comparisons in Table 2 therefore describe accuracy at the evaluated checkpoints within each model family. During evaluation, we set the maximum response length to 8192 tokens and generate 8 responses per prompt at temperature = 0.7. We report their average accuracy for Qwen3-8B and pass@8 for Llama3.2-3B-Instruct (see detailed settings in §B). 

Baselines. We apply our method to six RL methods in two types: (1) Four Representative RL Methods: GRPO [21], DrGRPO [16], GSPO [35], and DAPO [31]; (2) Two Entropy-Based RL Methods: ClipCov and KLCov [3], which are entropy-based variants of DAPO.

5.2. Key Insights More robust gains than entropy control. Table 2 includes two remedies: entropy (ClipCov and KLCov as entropy-based variants of DAPO) and our method. However, within each dataset, results of entropy-control methods are mixed. On average, on Qwen3-8B, DAPO even outperforms its variants (DAPO 61.94 vs. ClipCov 57.98 vs. KLCov 61.11); on Llama3.2-3B, KLCov marginally outperforms DAPO (25.12 vs. 23.95) while ClipCov slightly under-performs (23.26 vs. 23.95). In contrast, our method consistently improves in almost all cases. 

Impact of advantage construction on 𝜅 -dynamics. Apart from reward budgeting, the RL baselines in Table 2 construct advantages in mainly two ways: DRGRPO uses 𝐴 = 𝑟 − ¯𝑟 , while others use 𝐴 = (𝑟 − ¯𝑟 )/ 𝜎 (in fact, DAPO and its variants also includes length-based reward, but its magnitude is typically much smaller than 𝑟 , thus we omit this for simplicity). These different advantage constructions then induce different 𝜅 -trajectories (§4.1). Specifically, DRGRPO tends to exhibit slightly lower 𝜅 , thus gentler sharpening pressure. Comparing DRGRPO to the others, we observe that its performance remains in the top tier. This agrees with our earlier discussion: well-regulated 𝜅 can deliver better final convergence, though our method continues to improve even in this case.

The Qwen3-8B GRPO comparison in Table 2 retains the reference-policy KL penalty both with and without reward budgeting. Removing the KL penalty alone changes the average score from 59.87% to 60.27%, with mixed effects across benchmarks. In contrast, reward budgeting improves the average by +6.66 percentage points when KL is retained and by +5.38 percentage points when KL is removed. It also improves every evaluated benchmark under both KL settings. These results indicate that the gains are primarily attributable to reward budgeting, rather than to KL removal.

5.5. Limitations 

Our experiments concern verifiable reasoning tasks with predominantly binary rewards. The general-reward analysis requires additional assumptions and does not establish performance for noisy, learned, or open-ended reward functions. Each main configuration uses one training seed and a rollout-group size of 𝐺 = 8. We did not conduct a group-size sweep. The eight evaluation responses per prompt characterize decoding variability. The reported scores are point estimates from those training runs. The interventions and regressions describe finite-horizon behavior; they do not establish a unique causal mediator of eventual performance. Training stops when validation accuracy reaches a plateau; numerical plateau thresholds, per-run training lengths, and checkpoint-selection rules for the main benchmark table are not reported. The retrospective convergence-step measure in Appendix B.3 describes the intervention trajectories in Figure 3. 

# 6. Conclusion 

We study premature convergence in RL and suggest that aggregate entropy may not fully characterize training dynamics. Instead, RL’s implicit target 𝜋 ∗ reveals a built-in sharpening pressure, which is captured with the sharpness factor 𝜅 (and 𝜅 eff ). Motivated by this, we propose reward-budgeting to improve final performance without explicit entropy control.

A.2. Scope of the Objective Analysis 

Algorithm 1 uses a gap-normalized reward-budget estimator: it first subtracts the within-group minimum reward, normalizes the remaining positive gaps to a fixed per-prompt budget, and skips groups whose gaps are all zero. This differs from the simpler reward-normalized estimator that directly uses 𝑅 𝑖 /Í𝑗 𝑅 𝑗 .We therefore separate the objective analysis into three cases. First, in Appendix A.3, we derive the exact finite-𝐺 objective induced by Algorithm 1 in the binary-reward setting. This is the setting most closely aligned with our main RLVR experiments. Second, in Appendix A.4, we analyze an idealized non-shifted reward-normalized estimator, which gives the clean ∇ log 𝔼 [𝑅 ] interpretation. This result should be understood as an intuition for why fixed reward budgets induce diminishing returns, rather than as the exact finite-𝐺 objective of Algorithm 1. Third, in Appendix A.5, we discuss the actual gap-normalized estimator under general rewards and show that, under additional support assumptions, it asymptotically corresponds to a shifted log-mean objective.

# B. Experimental Detail 

B.1. Data Processing Training & Validation. We filter DAPO-MATH-17K [31] using the following criteria: 1. the final answer is an integer, so that we can reliably extract and compare answers from RL rollouts; 2. the prompt length is at most 1024 tokens; 3. the response length is at most 8192 tokens. After filtering, we then use the first 10k examples for training and the subsequent 1k examples for validation. 

Evaluation. For all benchmarks, we apply no filtering and keep the full dataset. 

B.2. Hyper-Parameters Training. For all RL algorithms, we use 4 nodes with 8 H100 gpus per node. We sample 8 rollouts per prompt at temperature 1.0, with a maximum response length of 8192 tokens. We use AdamW with weight decay 0 .1and a constant learning rate of 1 × 10 −6 with no warmup, and perform one update per batch of 32 prompts. For the main benchmark comparisons, we retain each baseline's KL setting when adding reward budgeting. GRPO uses a reference-policy KL coefficient of 0 .001 in both cases. 

Validation. We validate every 5 training steps by computing pass@1 accuracy on the validation set. For generation, we use greedy decoding with a maximum response length of 8192 tokens to avoid stochasticity. 

Evaluation. We evaluate each trained checkpoint on the selected reasoning benchmarks using 8 responses per prompt at temperature 0 .7, with a maximum response length of 8192 tokens. As in Table 2, we report mean accuracy across the eight generations for Qwen3-8B and pass@8 for Llama3.2-3B-Instruct. These are different metrics and should not be compared directly across model families. The table reports point estimates rather than mean ± std across independently trained models. For the integer-answer benchmarks AMC23, AIME24, and AIME25, we use exact match. For MATH500 and OlympiadBench, whose answers may be symbolic expressions, we use an LLM judge (Qwen3-235B-Instruct) to determine whether a predicted answer matches the ground truth. The judge is instructed to compare mathematical meaning rather than surface form: it should ignore differences in L ATEX, whitespace, and notation, and accept mathematically equivalent expressions. It must also enforce any required domain, units, and exactness constraints, while allowing small rounding errors when appropriate. The judge outputs only one of two labels: correct or incorrect .
