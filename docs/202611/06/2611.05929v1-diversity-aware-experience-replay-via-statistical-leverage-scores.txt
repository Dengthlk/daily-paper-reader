# Diversity-Aware Experience Replay via Statistical Leverage Scores 

Shaifalee Saxena 1,2 and Alexander Scheinker 1

Abstract — Off-policy deep reinforcement learning trains on random samples from a replay buffer of past experience. In physical robot settings, that buffer is dominated by routine: thousands of near-identical transitions from normal operation, while the rare events that define the edges of the task are rarely sampled. We propose scoring every stored transition by its 

statistical leverage , which measures how unusual it is relative to the buffer’s own data, computed from the stored numbers alone, with no labels, rewards, or value network. We draw each mini-batch from a mixture of this score and uniform sampling. First, the natural way to build the score along the directions where the data varies most turns out to be blind when actions are stored as one-hot indicators, because indicator columns always win the variance ranking; we instead select the feature columns with the heaviest tails (largest kurtosis) where small far-away populations are revealed. Second, the importance weights that prioritized replay uses to keep gradients unbiased cancel the method exactly: with full correction, training matches uniform sampling, so we drop the correction and deliberately optimize the rebalanced objective. Third, when the rare situations of a log are relational, visible only in how a stretch of behavior relates to its goal, not in any single stored coordinate—per-transition scores are blind; applying the same tail rule to short trajectory windows described relative to their goal restores the signal, and on a three-joint arm that must push an object around an obstacle it raises the rare-task success from 0.49 to 0.69 against a labeled-oracle ceiling of 0.74. 

I. INTRODUCTION Deep reinforcement learning (RL) [1] has become a prac-tical tool for synthesizing feedback controllers directly from interaction data. The modern off-policy template descends from the Deep Q-Network (DQN) [2], which revived the classical idea of experience replay [3]: transitions (s, a, r, s ′)

are stored in a large ring buffer, and mini-batches drawn from the buffer decorrelate consecutive updates and allow each interaction to be reused many times. Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) [4], followed by Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3) [5] and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) [6] carried this template to continuous control, where they remain the workhorse algorithms of robot learning [7]. Sample effi-ciency has been improved mainly by extracting more gradient signal from the same stored data: ensembling and high update-to-data ratios [8], normalization and network scaling [9], and learned state–action representations with prioritized replay [10]. These advances support real-robot manipulation

Despite this progress, uniform sampling remains the default replay rule in standard off-policy RL. Uniform sampling implicitly assumes that every stored transition is equally worth revisiting, and this assumption degrades precisely as learning succeeds. A partially con-verged policy fills the buffer with thousands of near-identical transitions, such as the gripper hovering over the object in almost the same pose, or the beam sitting at a near-optimal setpoint with very low beam loss. At the same time, rare transitions that delineate the boundaries of the task (unusual contacts, failed grasps, off-nominal beam excursions) are sampled with vanishing frequency and eventually overwrit-ten. In this work, we make the buffer’s own geometry the sampling signal. We view the buffer as a tall data matrix, one standardized transition per row, and score each transition by its statistical leverage [15]: the squared, whitened length of its projection onto a chosen score subspace, equivalently a (truncated) Mahalanobis distance from the buffer’s own empirical distribution. Mini-batches are drawn from a mix-ture placing weight β on the normalized leverage scores and 

1−β on the uniform distribution. Because N ≫ d, the scores can be refreshed efficiently with buffer-wide statistics and a vectorized pass over the stored transitions. Our contributions are: (1) a leverage-mixture replay rule using excess-kurtosis column selection to avoid variance-subspace blindness under one-hot actions, and omitting importance-sampling correction, which we show cancels the rebalancing exactly; (2) exact, numerically verified sampling-share laws on Gaussian-mixture buffers, including a rarity-independent amplification ceiling and a multi-cluster mass law; (3) offline Q-learning experiments against exact Q∗,demonstrating improved learning in an underrepresented region; and (4) a window-level extension for relational rare behaviors, where goal-relative trajectory scoring on a three-joint arm trained with TD3+BC improves over transition-level replay and approaches a labeled oracle. II. RELATED WORK Non-uniform replay is not a new idea, and its adoption is strikingly uneven. Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) [16], which samples transitions in proportion to their temporal-difference (TD) error, is a standard ingredient of value-based and distributed agents. Where modern robot-learning systems do bias the buffer, they use simple fixed composition rules rather than per-transition priorities: symmetric 50/50 sampling of online and offline data in RLPD and SERL [11], [12]. Empirically, PER’s gains transfer inconsistently from Atari to continuous control, where studies find it often matches or underperforms uniform sampling [17], [18]. Theoretically, prioritized sampling combined with its importance-sampling correction is largely equivalent to uniform sampling under a reshaped loss, so much of its effect can be replicated (or cancelled) by loss design [17]; priorities are computed only when a transition was last visited and therefore go stale; the scheme introduces sensitive hyperparameters; and TD-error-based scores systematically chase noise and outliers in stochastic environments [16], [18], [19]. Broader studies of replay find that buffer capacity, replay ratio, and retention policy often matter as much as the sampling rule itself [20], [21], [22]. When outlier sensitivity is repaired, as in the loss-adjusted prioritization used in TD7 [17], [10], prioritization becomes beneficial again in continuous control, suggesting that the obstacle is not non-uniform sampling per se but the fragility of error-based scores. This motivates a complementary signal that does not depend on the current critic at all. A smaller body of work targets buffer coverage directly. Experience-selection studies show that which transitions are retained and replayed can matter as much as how many [22]; determinantal point processes (DPPs) have been used to select diverse trajectories and goals within hindsight replay [23] and to prioritize diverse transitions in high-dimensional environments [24]. The recurring obstacle in this line is computational: DPP kernels over N stored transitions are expensive, and much of the engineering effort in these works goes into approximating them. Our proposal can be read as the cheap linear-algebra limit of this idea: rank-k leverage scores are exactly the single-point marginals of a projection DPP, computable for the entire buffer in O(N dk ) per refresh and maintainable incrementally at O(d2) per transition. To the best of our knowledge, leverage-score sampling has not previously been studied as a replay-sampling rule for RL. Recent robot learning has increasingly shifted toward large-scale imitation and vision-language-action models [25]. These trends reduce the role of replay in some settings, but do not eliminate the regime considered here: when physical interaction is expensive, sample-efficient off-policy learning remains important [11], [12]. Recent work such as FastTD3 further shows that replay-based off-policy learning can remain competitive even at large scale and transfer to real hardware [26], while emerging VLA systems are themselves beginning to incorporate reinforcement-learning post-training from autonomously collected experience [27]. Thus, the replay buffer is not disappearing; the question is how stored experience should be selected and reused. III. LEVERAGE-MIXTURE REPLAY

The score (3), the tail, and the mixture (V-A) are unchanged. Table II and Figure 8 summarize the results. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced a diversity-aware experience replay frame-work that uses statistical leverage to preferentially revisit underrepresented experience without relying on rewards, labels, or the current value function. Analysis of Gaussian-mixture buffers characterized when leverage amplifies rare data, showing that amplification is governed by geometric separation rather than rarity alone. The analysis also revealed that variance-ranked subspaces can miss rare modes, moti-vating kurtosis-based feature selection. In the gridworld, the resulting leverage and tail-based replay rules improved learn-ing in the underrepresented region compared with uniform replay and PER. The robot-arm experiment further showed that the representation used for scoring is critical: when rare behavior could not be distinguished from individual state coordinates, transition-level scoring provided little improve-ment, whereas scoring short goal-relative trajectory windows increased rare-task success from 0.49 to 0.69, compared with 0.74 for a labeled oracle.
