## ABSTRACT 

Agent trajectories often contain both successful decisions and failed attempts, yet optimization with sparse trajectory-level rewards struggles to identify which critical decisions should be reinforced or suppressed. We observe that meaningful distinctions among on-policy trajectories can be distilled into natural-language process rubrics and used as process supervision signals. Based on this insight, we propose O NLINE RUBRIC SELF -D ISTILLATION (ORSD), which leverages self-derived process rubrics for dense token-level supervision, thereby augmenting outcome-centric reinforcement learning. Specifically, the current policy inde-pendently generates multiple rubric bundles at each step according to the same group of trajectories, extracting candidate process rubrics from cross-trajectory behavioral differences. A frozen verifier then evaluates all rubric items against each trajectory in the group, and selects one candidate bundle based on bundle-level alignment between rubric satisfaction and task success. ORSD treats the selected rubric bundle as privileged information during training, then compares the log-likelihoods of the original tokens with and without the rubric. The re-sulting likelihood gap provides token-specific process credit, which is added to the original trajectory-level GRPO advantage through additive advantage shap-ing. ORSD thus bridges trajectory-level outcome supervision and fine-grained process supervision, enabling token-level credit assignment using process signals. Extensive experiments on ALFWorld and WebShop show that ORSD accelerates policy improvement and boosts final task performance for both Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B under the same environment-interaction budget. Our code is available at https://github.com/AnnaXChun/ORSD .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language model (LLM) agents have shown strong capabilities in web interaction (Zhou et al., 2024a) and software engineering (Yang et al., 2024), with applications extending to embodied exploration (Wang et al., 2024). Yet reliable decision-making in interactive environments remains challenging: agents must plan sequences of actions, explore unfamiliar situations, and reflect on feedback to revise unsuccessful strategies. Recent progress in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) offers a promising training approach. DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) shows that verifiable reward feedback can substantially improve reasoning performance, with reflection and self-verification emerging during reinforcement learning. Building on these advances, agentic RL has also adopted Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) to train agents through environment interaction, as demonstrated by RAGEN (Wang et al., 2025) in multi-turn tasks. Despite these advances, relying solely on outcome rewards provides limited guidance about which intermediate decisions deserve credit. Under trajectory-level GRPO, all policy-generated tokens within a trajectory share the same advantage, so useful actions, redundant steps, and recoverable

To assign credit to individual steps, GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025) groups actions from matching environment states across trajectories and estimates step-level advantages through within-group comparisons. This refines credit across decisions, although tokens within each action still share the same step-level signal. More recently, natural-language rubrics have emerged as another source of supervision by making task-specific evaluation criteria explicit. Rubrics as Rewards (Gunjal et al., 2026) use these criteria to construct reward signals. RLCF (Viswanathan et al., 2025) constructs instruction-specific checklists in advance and aggregates criterion-level judgments into a response score, while OpenRubrics (Liu et al., 2026) derives rubrics from existing preference pairs and trains a rubric-guided reward model for preference optimization. Although rubric-based methods introduce richer evaluation criteria, their feedback usually still remains at the trajectory level, leaving credit assignment to specially individual tokens unresolved. Moreover, their rubric construction is separate from the policy’s ongoing learning process, so the criteria are not continually updated by the updated policy. These limitations motivate the use of rubrics as privileged context for finer-grained credit assignment. Our core insight is that comparing the log-probabilities assigned to the same sampled tokens with and without rubrics can turn rubric-based guidance into token-level advantages. Such signals can refine trajectory-level advantages, allowing tokens within the same trajectory to receive different credits. Moreover, as the policy evolves during training, its exploration patterns and failure modes may also change. Generating rubrics online from on-policy trajectories allows them to reflect the policy’s current behavior, capturing task-specific constraints, failure patterns, and successful action sequences that static rubrics may overlook. This enables the credit assignment signal to adapt to the evolving policy rather than relying on fixed, predefined criteria. However, jointly deriving rubrics from on-policy experience and leveraging them as privileged context for token-level credit assignment remains underexplored. In this work, we introduce O NLINE RUBRIC SELF -D ISTILLATION (ORSD), a framework that turns on-policy experience into adaptive process supervision for token-level credit assignment. ORSD reuses these trajectories to construct process supervision in three steps. First, conditioned on both task prompt and the group of trajectories, the same policy independently generates multiple rubric bundles, each containing multiple criteria for evaluating intermediate states along the trajectories. Second, ORSD employs a verifier to assess the satisfaction of each rubric criterion on every trajectory, producing a satisfaction profile for each rubric bundle. It then measures the alignment between each satisfaction profile and the outcome rewards, and selects the rubric bundle with the highest correlation as privileged information. Third, ORSD augments the prompt with the selected privileged information and recomputes the token likelihoods of the sampled trajectories. The gap between the original and augmented token likelihoods is then used to compute the token-level advantage. As a result, ORSD transforms a group of on-policy trajectories into token-level credit, requiring neither additional rollouts nor additional environment interactions. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose O NLINE RUBRIC SELF -D ISTILLATION (ORSD), a framework that uses online-generated process rubrics to derive token-level credit. These rubrics are distilled from the policy’s own rollouts and serve as privileged information that augments the replay context, with token-level log-probability differences between replay with and without the rubrics underpinning this fine-grained credit allocation. • To address the quality variation among generated rubrics, we propose a rubric-reward alignment mechanism that filters out low-value rubrics by measuring the alignment between rubric satisfaction and task success, thereby enhancing the quality of token-level credit. • Experiments on ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B show that ORSD improves overall success over GRPO under the same environment-interaction budget. Ablations support the contributions of online rubric generation and rubric–reward alignment. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

RLVR for LLM Agents. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) uses automati-cally verified outcomes to train language models. PPO and GRPO are commonly used for policy optimization, with GRPO estimating relative advantages within groups of sampled responses (Schul-2man et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024). For LLM agents, training involves multiple rounds of interaction, during which policy’s decisions affect subsequent observations and the final reward. RAGEN exam-ines learning dynamics in multi-turn agent RL, and Agent Lightning provides a framework for training agents from their execution traces (Wang et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). The environment interaction also supports additional learning signals. EnvRL uses state transitions for representation learning, while T 2PO adjusts exploration through uncertainty estimates and turn-level resampling (Wang et al., 2026d;a). 

Credit Assignment in Agentic RL. Credit assignment assigns different credit to individual steps or tokens in an agent trajectory. GiGPO groups steps across trajectories by state similarity and estimates relative advantages within each group (Feng et al., 2025). HGPO further groups steps according to their interaction contexts (He et al., 2026). ArCHer uses rewards from subsequent interactions to estimate the value of the entire response generated at the current turn, then uses this estimate to update the policy that generates the response (Zhou et al., 2024b). POAD further considers the role of individual tokens within an action: it treats action generation as a sequence of token-level decisions and estimates future rewards for each token, providing a finer-grained training signal (Wen et al., 2024). SC-GRPO conditions on verified solutions and weights token gradients according to the resulting distributional divergence (Shan et al., 2026). 

Natural-language Rubrics. A natural-language rubric is a set of written criteria specifying which aspects of a response or reasoning process to evaluate. Prior work uses rubrics to provide feedback at different levels: Rubrics as Rewards assigns a reward to the entire response based on rubric satisfaction (Gunjal et al., 2026); Rubrics to Tokens estimates the relevance of each criterion to individual response tokens, providing token-level feedback (Xu et al., 2026); and Step-wise Rubric Rewards checks whether each reasoning step satisfies the criteria and assigns step-level rewards (Xie et al., 2026). These methods use rubrics to construct fine-grained supervision, with criteria generated offline and held fixed. We further investigate how to generate rubrics online that better match the current policy and use them for token-level credit assignment. 

On-policy Distillation with Privileged Context. Knowledge distillation trains a student model us-ing teacher predictions, with supervision applied to individual tokens or complete sequences (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim & Rush, 2016). Training can also use data generated by the learner itself: DAgger collects supervision at states visited by the learner’s policy (Ross et al., 2011), while GKD (Agar-wal et al., 2024) trains on student-generated sequences with supervision provided by a teacher. OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026) uses the same model for self-distillation: the model first generates a response, then computes its log-probability given additional information to provide a training signal for the original policy. This additional information is called privileged context. Prior work also extracts such information from task execution records: Skill-SD (Wang et al., 2026b) summarizes experience from completed trajectories into natural-language skills, while SDPO (H¨ ubotter et al., 2026) uses environmental feedback and successful attempts for self-distillation. 

## 3 METHOD 

In this section, we present the proposed O NLINE RUBRIC SELF -D ISTILLATION (ORSD), a framework that turns on-policy rollouts into rubrics and then into token-level credit signals. As shown in Figure 1, at each training step, the current policy first samples a group of trajectories for the same task and obtains a reward for each trajectory. It then generates candidate rubric bundles from the task description and these trajectories. The rubric-reward alignment mechanism selects the bundle whose rubric satisfaction scores correlate most strongly with the rewards across the group. Finally, the token-wise log-likelihood difference between rollouts with and without the selected bundle enables per-token advantage shaping of the original trajectory-level advantage. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks and Metrics. We evaluate ORSD on ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) and Web-Shop (Yao et al., 2022) two long-horizon interactive benchmarks using Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025). For ALFWorld, we report task-wise and overall success rates on the unseen split. For WebShop, we report the official task score and success rate. 

Prompting Baselines. We include published results for prompting methods that guide interaction without RL training. ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) interleaves reasoning and actions. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) uses reflections on previous attempts to guide later attempts, while ReflAct (Kim et al., 2025) relates the current state to the task goal when choosing actions. ReAct w/ Self-Guidance (SG) adds self-generated guidance during interaction (Wang et al., 2026c). 

Training Baselines. We compare ORSD with several baselines. (a) GRPO: its training data, reward definition, thinking mode, rollout count, and the number of training steps are strictly consistent with ORSD. (b) GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025): it estimates step-level advantages by grouping actions from similar states. We run GiGPO on both benchmarks with both Qwen3 backbones. (c) Self-distillation methods, including OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026), GRPO+OPSD, and SDAR (Lu et al., 2026). GRPO+OPSD combines GRPO with an auxiliary distillation loss. The sources of published results and their evaluation protocols are specified in the notes to Table 1. 

Training Details. We fine-tune all parameters of both backbones with VERL/HybridFlow (Sheng et al., 2025). Each run uses four A100 80GB GPUs. Every update contains eight tasks with eight rollouts per task. We train for 300 training steps at a learning rate of 10 −6. WebShop episodes allow for at most 15 assistant actions, while ALFWorld interactions end according to the environment’s termination conditions. We generate M = 4 candidate rubric bundles per task and use α = 0 .01 ,

εc = 0 .2, and η = 10 −3 by default. Appendix C provides the remaining implementation details.

The following ablations examine the contributions of online rubric generation and rubric-reward alignment. 4.3 ABLATION STUDY 

In this section, we use ablation experiments to examine the effectiveness of ORSD’s components: online rubric generation, the number of candidate bundles and their selection, and the advantage shaping coefficient α. All ablations use WebShop. We study rubric generation and selection with both Qwen3 backbones and vary α with Qwen3-1.7B. 

Effectiveness of Online Rubric Generation. To evaluate the effectiveness of online rubric gener-ation, we construct an Offline Rubric variant. Before RL training, the initial policy generates one task-specific rubric bundle for each task, and these rubrics remain fixed throughout training. As shown in Table 2, Offline Rubric consistently improves over GRPO on both model scales, confirming that task-specific rubric supervision is already beneficial even when generated only once. However, it still falls short of online ORSD by 4.80 percentage points in success rate for both backbones. This gap highlights the value of refreshing the rubric from the current policy’s rollouts, so that the supervision remains aligned with the policy’s changing behavior and failure modes during training. Importantly, both variants generate rubrics automatically and require no manually authored criteria. 

Effectiveness of Rubric-Reward Alignment. ORSD selects candidate rubric bundles based on their alignment with outcome rewards. We examine this design through two ablations. ORSD w/o Rubric-Reward Alignment generates a single bundle and uses it directly, omitting verifier scoring and candidate selection. Random Rubric retains the same candidate count as full ORSD ( M = 4 ) but replaces alignment-based selection with random selection. Table 2 shows that full ORSD outperforms both variants on both model scales. Relative to the single-bundle variant, ORSD improves success by 2.15 and 9.45 percentage points on Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B, respectively. With the candidate count held fixed, alignment-based selection improves success over random selection by 3.40 and 7.15 7percentage points, with corresponding score gains of 5.36 and 4.08 points. These consistent gains demonstrate the effectiveness of rubric-reward alignment in selecting process supervision for policy training, with the selected rubrics serving as privileged context for token-level advantage shaping. ## 5 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Rubric Knowledge is not explicitly accumulated across updates. ORSD generates task-specific rubrics from the current rollout group at each update, but does not maintain a persistent repository of previously discovered criteria. How to retain, refine, and reuse useful rubrics across training stages and related tasks remains an open question. Extending ORSD with a rubric memory could support cumulative process knowledge while preserving adaptation to the evolving policy. 

Outcome alignment does not fully resolve credit assignment. ORSD selects rubric bundles by their correlation with trajectory-level rewards. This objective favors rubrics that distinguish stronger trajectories, but does not directly optimize their usefulness for assigning credit within a trajectory. Future work could develop selection objectives that account for how rubric guidance affects individual decisions and subsequent policy improvement. 96 CONCLUSION 

We introduced O NLINE RUBRIC SELF -D ISTILLATION (ORSD) to improve credit assignment in LLM agent reinforcement learning. ORSD generates task-specific process rubrics online from differences among on-policy trajectories, without manually authored or fixed offline criteria. Rubric-reward alignment selects the bundle whose satisfaction scores best correlate with outcomes. Through privi-leged paired replay, the selected rubric converts token-level likelihood gaps into token-level advantage corrections, providing fine-grained supervision without additional environment interactions. Ex-periments on ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B show the best overall performance among the compared methods, while ablations validate online rubric generation and candidate selection. These results highlight the value of deriving adaptive process supervision from the policy’s own experience for credit assignment in long-horizon agent tasks.

Paired replay uses records saved at each generation turn. A record contains the turn index, context messages and token IDs, complete response token IDs, action-only response history, subsequent observation, response mask, and rollout log probabilities. Plain replay packs ct + yt independently for each turn. Rubric-conditioned replay inserts the selected complete bundle into ct and reuses the same target response. The response mask selects the log probabilities used by the correction. The implementation mask ei,ℓ equals one when the tokenization round-trip succeeds, response tokens align, and both log-likelihoods are finite. A token with ei,ℓ = 0 retains its GRPO advantage.
