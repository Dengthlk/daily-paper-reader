ABSTRACT 

While multi-turn agentic reinforcement learning (RL) excels on long-horizon tasks, its perfor-mance gains remain highly sensitive to interaction constraints. Evaluating across sampling breadth K and interaction depth T via Pass@( K, T ), we show that trajectory-level RL ad-vantages depend on sufficient turn budgets, diminishing significantly when budgets tighten. Besides, agentic RL does not uniformly elevate single-step decision quality; rather, its strength concentrates in error recovery and long-horizon resilience. To mitigate the deployment risks caused by this budget dependence, we provide a preliminary discussion on the benefits and limitations of process supervision signals and introduce BudgetMix RL as an alternative that varies interaction budgets during training. Experiments demonstrate that BudgetMix RL achieves robust performance across diverse execution settings.

1 INTRODUCTION 

Multi-turn agentic reinforcement learning (RL) has recently emerged as an important paradigm for improving the long-horizon capabilities of large language model (LLM) agents (Li et al., 2026a; Xi et al., 2025a). By interacting with environments persistently, consuming feedback, and adapting subsequent actions, agents trained with RL have achieved substantial gains on multi-turn tasks (e.g., code or tool use). Despite these improvements, a fundamental question remains insufficiently understood: what capabilities does multi-turn agentic RL actually improve? Answering this question is important not only for understanding how agentic RL differs from its base model, but also for assessing whether agentic RL models remain reliable under realistic deployment scenarios. Existing works primarily evaluate agents at the trajectory level, typically based on whether a task is completed within a given budget. Prior work on single-turn RL further uses Pass@ K to probe model capability boundary (Yue et al., 2025), which increases the number of independently sampled solutions. Pass@ K captures one dimension of test-time scaling: sampling breadth . Thus, we first apply Pass@ K to the evaluation of agents in §3.1. The results show that agentic RL models consistently outperform base models as the number of sampled trajectories k increases. These results suggest that agentic RL effectively expands the empirical capability boundary of the base model. However, for interactive agents, there is another test-time dimension: the number of interactions allowed per trajectory (Shen et al., 2026), which captures interaction depth . Accordingly, we evaluate performance using Pass@ T by varying the maximum allowed interaction turns (§3.2). When we vary budget T , an interesting phenomenon emerges: given a large budget, agentic RL models demonstrate a substantial advantage over base models. However, under tighter budgets, this advantage shrinks considerably and can even disappear or reverse on certain tasks (right-hand panel of Figure 1). These results suggest that the gains of agentic RL depend on a sufficient interaction budget . To ensure

To further study this budget-dependent shift in capability, we analyze the step-level action quality of models before and after multi-turn RL (§3.3). Results show that, given the same state and history, RL models do not consistently produce better next-step actions and can even perform worse in certain settings. Instead, their advantage becomes more pronounced at error states and later stages of a trajectory. As illustrated in Figure 1, multi-turn RL does not uniformly improve action quality of every step; it primarily strengthens ability of error recovery and sustain long-horizon execution, which may partly explain why RL models need a larger budget T to demonstrate their advantages. To understand this phenomenon more deeply, we perform a series of mechanistic analyses (§4). Our log-likelihood analysis (§4.1) shows that RL-generated actions remain locally plausible under the base policy, yet complete RL trajectories receive substantially higher likelihood under the RL policy than under the base policy. This suggests that multi-turn RL may improve how reliably the model composes locally plausible actions into long-horizon trajectories. This phenomenon offers a possible explanation for how RL models can surpass the empirical Pass@ K capability boundary of base models, even without consistently superior single-step actions to the base models. The training dynamics in §4.2 further show how this pattern emerges progressively during training. These analyses reveal a hidden risk masked by conventional trajectory-level success rates: models trained with multi-turn RL may fail to perform consistently under varying interaction budgets. Furthermore, their single-step action quality can decline, which are critical in real-world deployments involving strict budget limits or irreversible errors (Ma et al., 2026; Doshi et al., 2026). To address this, we provide a preliminary discussion on some candidate solutions (§5). Specifically, we examine the benefits and limitations of process supervision signals and introduce BudgetMix RL , an alternative training strategy that varies interaction budgets. Experiments show that BudgetMix RL consistently improves performance across budgets while mitigating the decline in step-level action quality (§5.2).

3 WHEN DOES AGENTIC RL O UTPERFORM THE BASE MODEL ?

We now examine whether the gains from multi-turn agentic RL can be consistently achieved, as well as its potential risks. We evaluate models with two dimensional metrics Pass@( K, T ) introduced in §2.2 (§3.1 and §3.2), analyze step-level action quality (§3.3), and summarize the potential risks (§3.4). We adopt GRPO as our primary RL algorithm and evaluate on four interactive benchmarks: DeepCoder-MT, BFCL, InterCode-SQL, and FrozenLake, spanning diverse domains and modalities. Additional results are reported in Appendix C and D, implementation details in Appendix A and B. 3.1 TRAJECTORY -L EVEL GAINS ACROSS SAMPLING BREADTH 

We begin by evaluating the trajectory-level performance using the Pass@ K. By increasing the number of independently sampled trajectories, Pass@ K probes the empirical capability boundary of a model, which we use to characterize how this boundary changes after multi-turn RL. As shown in Figure 2, the RL model consistently outperforms the base model as the sampling breadth k increases, suggesting that multi-turn RL can push trajectory-level success beyond the empirical capability boundary of base model. This contrasts with prior observations in single-turn tasks, where RL does not expand capability boundary beyond the base model. Further results are shown in Appendix C. 

Takeaway 3.1 Pros of Agentic RL: unlike single-turn RL, multi-turn agentic RL can push trajectory-level performance beyond the empirical Pass@ K capability boundary of the base model.

Beyond sampling breadth , we evaluate how model performance evolves with interaction depth . As shown in Figure 3, we fix the sampling breadth at k = 8 and vary the maximum interaction turns T .The results reveal an interesting pattern: the advantage of the RL model depends on a sufficient interaction budget . As the budget T increases, the performance advantage of RL model over base model becomes increasingly pronounced. Conversely, under tight budgets (small T ), this advantage diminishes, and can even reverse on certain tasks. To rule out the possibility that the performance drop at small T is trivially caused by tasks becoming structurally unsolvable under restrictive budgets, we also compute the structural performance upper bound for each task and discuss in Appendix B.3. Notably, at small T , even when substantial headroom remains below this upper bound, agentic RL fails to exhibit any clear advantage over the base model. More results are shown in Appendix D. We conjecture that agent RL primarily strengthens long-horizon capabilities whose benefits emerge when sufficient interaction budget is available. Under restricted budgets, these benefits may disappear, suggesting that its gains do not necessarily stem from uniformly better step-level actions .

Agentic RL excels after errors. Since the RL model does not consistently yield superior single-step decisions across every matched state, a natural question arises: why does multi-turn RL achieve marked trajectory-level performance gains? To answer this, we focus on states immediately following an error (e.g., error states), where effective recovery ability of models becomes critical. Specifically, we construct an error state pool by injecting an incorrect action at the preceding step. As shown in Figure 5, the pattern reverses under these error states: data points shift predominantly above the diagonal, with the RL model selecting the superior action in 65.7% of cases on FrozenLake and 67.3% on DeepCoder-MT. We additionally compare the terminal success rates of RL and base models when continuing from identical error states. RL outperforms base on both FrozenLake ( 51 .3% vs. 

38 .6% ) and DeepCoder-MT ( 38 .5% vs. 22 .9% ). Appendix E.4 also illustrates how the RL model corrects an erroneous tool call on BFCL. These results demonstrate that RL achieves higher action quality and continuation success on these error states. When errors occur in interaction trajectories, agentic RL demonstrates superior error recovery capabilities and resilience. 

The RL advantage emerges later in trajectories. Finally, we examine how decision quality evolves over the course of a trajectory. We group intermediate states by turn index and define the normalized depth at turn t as dt = t/T max ∈ [0 , 1] , where Tmax is the maximum interaction budget. As shown in Figure 6, the RL model shows little advantage early in execution and even underperforms the base model. As interaction proceeds, however, the gap gradually narrows and reverses. This indicates that the RL model does not make superior decisions at the beginning of a trajectory; its advantage emerges as interaction unfolds. This suggests that the step-level advantage of agentic RL is not uniform across a trajectory, but becomes more pronounced at later stages of long-horizon interaction.

Takeaway 3.3 

Multi-turn agentic RL does not consistently improve single-step action quality . Its advantage is instead more pronounced in error states and later stages of long-horizon trajectories. 3.4 RISKS : R EAL -W ORLD DEPLOYMENT OF AGENTS 

These findings highlight a deployment risk: the gains from agentic RL depend on sufficient interaction budget and may diminish or reverse under tight budgets. This is particularly concerning for tasks involving irreversible actions, such as financial or database operations (Doshi et al., 2026), where agents must make high-quality and conservative actions at each step. Yet higher trajectory success does not ensure better individual actions. As LLM agents are increasingly deployed in real-world settings, understanding such limitations beyond final task success becomes increasingly important.

5 DISCUSSION : T OWARDS MORE STABLE AGENTIC RL 

The preceding sections indicate that the gains of agent RL rely on sufficient interaction budgets, while local action quality does not consistently improve and can even decline. This raises reliability concerns for real-world deployment. Consequently, this section offers a preliminary discussion on potential mitigation strategies . Our objective is to preserve its long-horizon execution capabilities and and trajectory-level gains while reducing its reliance on extra interaction budget for downstream error recovery and encouraging greater prudence in single-step decision making.

A natural direction is to introduce finer-grained signals to improve per-step action quality through either process rewards or credit assignment. We therefore evaluate several methods to examine whether step-level signals can alleviate above limitations. As shown on the left of Figure 9, on FrozenLake, accurate process supervision can substantially mitigate the limitations of outcome-only RL. In particular, oracle Q-value process rewards and GiGPO stabilize performance across various interaction budgets, especially under tight budgets. However, as discussed in §3.3, such precise process supervision is rarely accessible in realistic agent tasks. Results of BFCL on the right illustrate this bottleneck, where no exact step-level oracle is available. Dense signal methods such as GiGPO and PPO fail to enhance performance under constrained budgets and perform similarly to GRPO. These findings demonstrate that while high-quality process rewards can alleviate budget reliance, acquiring such supervision signals remains impractical for most real-world applications .5.2 BUDGET MIX RL: O PTIMIZING RL ACROSS INTERACTION BUDGETS 

We therefore take a simpler route: BudgetMix RL . It keeps the same reward mechanism as traditional multi-turn RL, but varies the interaction budget across training trajectories. BudgetMix RL can be combined with existing agentic RL algorithms (e.g., GRPO, PPO, and GiGPO). Standard multi-turn agentic RL samples trajectories under a fixed maximum budget Tmax and optimizes either outcome or per-step rewards. However, this objective does not explicitly distinguish solutions that require different amounts of interaction, provided they finish within the budget. BudgetMix makes a single modification to this procedure. 5.4 RESULTS AND ANALYSIS 

Table 1 reports the main results. First, under tight budget constraints ( 25% , 50% and 75% Tmax ), BudgetMix outperforms both the base model and standard full-budget RL across all four tasks, achieving average performance gains of 7.5, 5.3 and 4.0 percentage points over full-budget RL, respectively. Second, at full interaction capacity ( 100% Tmax ), BudgetMix maintains parity with full-budget RL, demonstrating that its low-budget enhancements do not compromise long-horizon execution capability. Overall, BudgetMix improves the stability of agent performance, delivering strong performance across diverse interaction budgets and retaining strong full-budget results. We further examine the training dynamics of BudgetMix. As shown in Figure 8, during BudgetMix training (yellow and gray curves), the trajectory-level reward Rmulti steadily increases, while the step-level reward Rsingle remains stable or improves slightly. In contrast, standard full-budget agent RL (blue curve) degrades step-level quality. Thus, BudgetMix largely mitigates local decision-quality degradation while preserving long-horizon gains. Consistent results from direct step-level action-quality evaluation are provided in Appendix E.1. 

6 RELATED WORK 

6.1 MULTI -T URN AGENTIC REINFORCEMENT LEARNING 

LLM agents are widely used for interactive tasks like coding and tool use (Zhou et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a; Hao et al., 2026b; Men et al., 2025), with RL becoming a major training paradigm (Jin et al., 2026a; Zhu et al., 2026a). Many methods extend single-turn outcome-based RL (Yu et al., 2025) to interactive tasks using trajectory-level rewards (Xi et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025d), enabling applications such as deep research (Jin et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026), tool-augmented reasoning (Feng et al., 2025a), and web interaction (Qi et al., 2025). However, sparse terminal rewards make credit assignment increasingly difficult as interaction horizons grow (Tao et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026). To address this, methods derive process signals from outcomes (Liu et al., 2025b; Zheng et al., 2026), including state-grouped advantages in GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025b), stepwise reward decomposition in SPA-RL (Wang et al., 2025a), and advantage attribution with entropy-guided exploration in ARPO (Dong et al., 2025). Other work explicitly models intermediate decision quality (Xi et al., 2025b; Lin et al., 2026). Despite these advances, evaluation remains largely centered on trajectory-level success (Li et al., 2026c; Men et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026b). We therefore analyze multi-turn RL beyond final task success to better understand its gains and limitations. 

9Rethinking Agentic RL 

6.2 UNDERSTANDING AND RETHINKING RL P OST -T RAINING 

Recently, post-training has become a key stage to improving reasoning and planning capabilities of LLMs (Guo et al., 2025), prompting studies of its mechanisms and limitations (Zhu et al., 2026b; He et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026). For single-turn reasoning, Pass@ K analyses suggest that RLVR primarily reweights solutions already reachable by the base model (Yue et al., 2025). Later work reports boundary expansion with prolonged RL (Liu et al., 2025a), while others attribute apparent gains to recombining existing skills (Yuan et al., 2025) or amplifying latent base-model behaviors (Wu et al., 2026). These findings suggest that final success may obscure the source of RL gains. Other studies examine entropy dynamics (Cui et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026a), identify high-entropy tokens as key carriers of learning signals (Wang et al., 2025b). Related work further compares SFT and RL in generalization and capability acquisition (Chu et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026b). Mechanistic analyses have recently extended to multi-turn agents (Wang et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026a; Hao et al., 2026a). Most relevantly, prior work introduced Pass@ (K, T ) to characterize multi-turn capability boundaries and showed that agentic RL can surpass capability boundary of base model (Zhai et al., 2026). We go beyond this perspective by analyzing interaction-budget dependence, step quality and mechanisms to identify the sources of RL gains and potential limitations. 

7 CONCLUSION 

We revisit the benefits of multi-turn agentic RL beyond final task success. Through Pass@( K, T )

evaluation, we demonstrate that RL can extend the empirical capabilities of the base model, though this advantage may diminish or reverse as interaction budgets become more constrained. Our analysis suggests that these gains stem primarily from long-horizon execution and error recovery, rather than consistently superior single-step actions. As this may impact the reliability of agents deployed in real-world settings, we further discuss potential strategies to mitigate this risk and their limitations.

ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work studies the capabilities and limitations of multi-turn reinforcement learning for LLM agents, with particular attention to their reliability under different interaction constraints. Our experiments are conducted on benchmark environments covering tool use, interactive coding, planning, and terminal operations. We do not conduct human-subject experiments in this study. Our analysis highlights a potential deployment risk: the gains of agentic RL can shrink under tight interaction budgets, while single-step action quality of RL models does not consistently improve. This limitation can be particularly relevant in applications involving strict interaction budgets or irreversible actions. We therefore believe that agentic systems should be evaluated not only by final task success, but also by their robustness across execution constraints and the reliability of individual decisions.

We train and evaluate agents on five representative tasks, covering tool calling, interactive code generation, embodied planning, terminal operations. For FrozenLake, we independently generate training and test maps, ensuring that the two splits are disjoint. For Terminal-Bench 1.0 (Merrill et al., 2026), we use the training tasks provided by Tmax (Ivison et al., 2026). A.2 MODELS AND TRAINING HYPERPARAMETERS

GRPO and GiGPO do not use value networks, whereas PPO trains a separate critic to estimate state values. GiGPO derives step-level credit assignment from terminal rewards and recurring environment states. Neither GiGPO nor PPO requires an additional external process reward model. Our implementation builds on the VeRL framework (Sheng et al., 2024), and we thank its developers for their excellent work. All algorithms are trained from the base model. As described in §3.3, we extract intermediate states from complete base and RL trajectories and construct a state pool with equal contributions from the two policies to mitigate bias. Each state includes the current observation and the history. For each state, we sample G = 16 next-step actions from each policy and estimate its single-step decision quality by the sample mean of the corresponding action values. For FrozenLake, we use the known deterministic transition dynamics of the environment to compute the exact Q∗ value of each candidate action via finite-horizon dynamic programming (DP) or breadth-first search (BFS). For DeepCoder-MT, we execute each candidate program independently and use its pass rate on the official unit tests as the action-quality measure. B.5 ERROR -S TATE CONSTRUCTION 

We construct error states to evaluate single-step decision quality as follows. For FrozenLake, we select a random intermediate state st along a trajectory and execute a randomly chosen action at. If the action increases the shortest-path distance to the goal, we regard the resulting state as worse than the preceding state and include it in the error-state pool. For DeepCoder-MT, we use Qwen3-1.7B to modify the code at a randomly selected intermediate state. If the modified program passes fewer unit tests than the original program, we regard this as a degradation in code quality and use the resulting state for subsequent evaluation. Both constructions serve as proxies rather than rigorous definitions of error states. B.6 EFFECT OF PROCESS SUPERVISION SIGNALS 

In §5.1 of the main text, we preliminarily investigate whether high-quality process supervision can alleviate the dependence of agentic RL gains on interaction budgets. On FrozenLake, we use oracle 

Q-values as process rewards, following the same procedure as in § 3.3: we compute the Q-value of each action via finite-horizon dynamic programming. For both tasks, we additionally explore using an LLM judge as a process reward model to provide step-level supervision. Specifically, we use GPT-5.6-Sol as the judge model with the prompt shown in Table 3. Our results show that even a strong judge model cannot consistently provide high-quality process supervision across all tasks, particularly in more realistic settings such as BFCL.

The preceding sections of the appendix focus on Pass@( K, T ) results. Here, we present additional results of analysis experiment to complement §3.3 and 4 of the main text. E.1 ADDITIONAL RESULTS OF STEP -L EVEL ACTION QUALITY 

In §3.3 of the main text, we analyze how agentic RL affects single-step action quality. While the main experiments use GRPO, here we provide additional results with GiGPO. As shown in Figures 19 and 20, on FrozenLake, where GiGPO can obtain informative step-level credit signals, the RL model achieves better single-step action quality than the base model at both all matched states and error states. In contrast, on DeepCoder-MT, recurring matched states are difficult to obtain, making reliable step-level credit assignment substantially harder. Consequently, the RL model again does not consistently outperform the base model in single-step action quality. These results suggest that high-quality process-level supervision can improve single-step action quality. However, such supervision may be difficult to obtain in more realistic agentic tasks. Similarly, we evaluate the single-step action quality of models trained with BudgetMix GRPO. As shown in Figure 21, on randomly sampled matched states, BudgetMix RL yields a clear improvement in single-step decision quality. Unlike standard GRPO, which does not consistently improve action quality and can even degrade it, as shown in Figure 4 of §3.3, BudgetMix does not exhibit such degradation and instead produces consistently higher-quality local decisions. E.2 ADDITIONAL LOG -L IKELIHOOD ANALYSIS 

In §4.1, we present a mechanistic analysis based on log-likelihoods, primarily on Terminal-Bench. Here, we extend this analysis to BFCL. As shown in Figure 23 (a), we observe a consistent pattern: base model assigns similar likelihoods to individual actions generated by base and RL models, yet at the trajectory level, base systematically assigns lower likelihoods to RL trajectories than to its own. These results show a substantial shift between the RL and base policies over complete long-horizon trajectories, despite similar per-token NLLs under the base policy for RL and base actions.

The separation of cumulative LLRs thus helps us to partly rule out the influence of trajectory length. E.3 ADDITIONAL TRAINING DYNAMICS 

In §4.2, we track how agentic RL affects trajectory success and single-step action quality as training progresses. Here, we provide additional training dynamics on FrozenLake. As shown in Figure 24, under standard RL, trajectory-level reward Rmulti (red curve) increases, indicating agent RL can improve trajectory success. However, single-step action quality reward Rsingle (blue curve) does not improve and instead tends to decline. Thus, trajectory-level gains during training do not necessarily translate into better local actions. However, with BudgetMix RL, single-step decision quality Rsingle 

(gray curve) remains stable or improves slightly, ending approximately 0.1 above the standard RL curve. These results suggest that BudgetMix RL can help mitigate the decline in single-step decision quality. For each of FrozenLake and DeepCoder-MT, we construct a fixed pool of 512 states from base model trajectories on held-out tasks, using multiple rollout seeds and sampling across tasks and trajectory depths to encourage diversity. Each state includes its history and remaining budget. At each validation checkpoint, we sample 8 next-step actions per state and average their quality scores over actions and states to obtain Rsingle . All checkpoints share the same state pool and scoring protocol. E.4 ADDITIONAL CASE STUDY RESULTS 

In §3.3, we find that RL models produce higher-quality actions in error states, suggesting agent RL may improve error recovery. Here, we provide a more direct case study on BFCL, examining whether models correctly revise a failed tool call in their next action. We collect trajectory prefixes containing tool-call errors (191), drawing half from RL rollouts (91) and half from base (100). Both models then take one action from each identical prefix. The RL model achieves an error correction rate of 

41 .4% , substantially exceeding the 27 .2% of base model. Figure 25 illustrates a case in which the RL model removes an unsupported argument rather than repeating the same error. Together, these results provide further support for our conclusion that agentic RL strengthens error recovery and resilience.
