## Abstract 

Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) is a widely used deep reinforcement learning algorithm that combines entropy regularization with an actor-critic architecture. Standard implementations rely on one-step temporal-difference learning of Q-functions, yet alternative critic design choices remain underexplored. In this work, we revisit three such choices: learning soft advantages instead of soft Q-values, the use of multi-step return targets, and the training of multi-step critics from replay buffer data. Keeping the policy objective fixed, we compare the six critic variants these choices generate: (i) advantage and (ii) dueling-advantage critics learned from one-step transitions, and λ-return and soft Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) critics learned either (iii, iv) on-policy or (v, vi) from replayed trajectory segments. Each variant is evaluated across an entire random hyperparameter search on fourteen tasks, treating the hyperparameters as part of the algorithm. Learning soft advantages instead of soft Q-values does not improve performance, and the soft GAE critic adds nothing over the simpler 

λ-return critic. What does improve performance, most clearly on continuous control, is training a λ-return critic on replayed trajectory segments, even without importance weighting or trace cutting. This extends earlier evidence that uncorrected multi-step targets work well under replay to the full soft λ-return, entropy terms included. By design, the underlying ideas studied here extend beyond SAC, and our empirical findings aim to encourage their application to a broader class of reinforcement learning algorithms.

SAC oc-cupies the one-step, replay-based Q-value cell; the other five cells are the variants we study. Soft Actor-Critic (SAC; Haarnoja et al. 2018a) is one of the most widely adopted algorithms in deep reinforcement learning. Its entropy regularization sustains exploration (Haarnoja et al., 2018a), improves robustness (Eysenbach & Levine, 2022), and yields a unique optimal policy, unlike the unregularized objec-tive (Jhaveri et al., 2025). Modern implementations (Raffin et al., 2021; Bou et al., 2024) follow Haarnoja et al. (2018b) in learning one or two soft Q-functions from one-step temporal-difference errors. This design is a convention rather than a requirement: SAC is defined by its actor-critic structure and entropy regularization, not by a particular critic. This leaves considerable freedom in how the critic is learned – freedom that remains largely unexplored. We study three dimensions of this freedom, which together span the grid of critic variants in Figure 1: (i) learning soft advantages instead of soft Q-values; (ii) the rollout depth of the target, through soft λ-returns; and (iii) whether multi-step targets are computed from on-policy rollouts or replayed from a 1arXiv:2611.05666v1 [cs.LG] 3 Nov 2026 buffer. Since all variants share the policy update, the implementation and the evaluation protocol, differences in outcome can be attributed to the critic. In A3C (Mnih et al., 2016) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO; Schulman et al. 2017), advantages reduce the variance of score-function policy gradients. This role does not carry over to SAC, whose policy gradient is unaffected by state-dependent shifts of the critic (Section 2). Advantages can nevertheless be numerically favorable: SAC’s policy depends only on the differences between actions, which can be small relative to the value itself and hard to resolve with an approximate Q-function (Tallec et al., 2019), whereas an advantage critic learns them directly (Baird, 1994; Wang et al., 2016). We test whether this makes SAC more robust to its hyperparameters, learning soft advantages with an explicit advantage loss (SAC-A) or jointly with a soft state value in a dueling architecture (SAC-DA); all other components are inherited from SAC. The rollout depth of the target trades off bias and variance in the critic’s learning signal. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE; Schulman et al. 2015) interpolates between one-step bootstrapped targets and Monte Carlo returns, and PQN (Gallici et al., 2024) recently carried the same idea over to Q-learning, replacing the replay buffer with many parallel environments. Following the idea of PQN, we drop the replay buffer, and train a soft Q-function on soft λ-returns from parallel on-policy rollouts (SAC-λ). Combining both axes, we also implement a variant in which we estimate soft advantages using GAE (SAC-GAE). Multi-step targets are harder to combine with replay, since a replayed trajectory was generated by an older policy and biases the target. Many methods correct for this with importance weights or truncated traces (Espeholt et al., 2018), including concurrent work on SAC (Łyskawa et al., 2025), yet uncorrected multi-step targets from a buffer have been demonstrated to work well in value-based methods (Hessel et al., 2018; Fedus et al., 2020; Clark et al., 2024) and in actor-critic continuous control (Kozuno et al., 2021). For the entropy-regularized target, the picture is less settled: Kozuno et al. (2021) found its per-step entropy terms destabilizing and kept only the final one. We train both multi-step critics on replayed segments with the full soft λ-return and no correction (SAC-λ-R, SAC-GAE-R), and compare them with their on-policy counterparts to see whether the bias outweighs the benefit of reusing data. All of these choices solely concern critic learning and fit into existing SAC implementations with little overhead. We present them for reinforcement learning with a general convex regularizer, of which SAC’s entropy bonus is the most prominent instance, and then measure what each buys in SAC across an entire random hyperparameter search on fourteen tasks from three benchmark suites. Soft advantages improve on soft Q-values neither in return nor in robustness, and the GAE critic adds nothing over the λ-return critic. In continuous control, multi-step gains come with replay rather than depth: within our sampled ranges, neither a larger λ nor a longer horizon helps, whereas training on replayed segments does, despite the uncorrected bias. This extends earlier evidence for uncorrected multi-step targets under replay to the full soft λ-return. In practice, the critic parameterization can be chosen on grounds other than return, and in SAC, too, multi-step targets can be paired with replay without an off-policy correction.

We consider SAC an off-policy algorithm, because its one-step target can be computed from transitions generated by any policy: the current policy only enters at the next state, through the expectation over 

a′ ∼ π(·| s′). SAC-DA inherits this property; SAC-A only in part (Section 3.2). A multi-step target, in contrast, follows the actions taken along a trajectory, so without a correction such as importance weighting or trace cutting (Espeholt et al., 2018; Łyskawa et al., 2025), it is unbiased only for trajectories sampled from the policy being evaluated. Replay is nonetheless attractive, since reusing data typically improves sample efficiency. Following other methods that combine uncorrected multi-step targets with a replay buffer (Hessel et al., 2018; Fedus et al., 2020; Kozuno et al., 2021; Clark et al., 2024), we train both multi-step critics on trajectory segments drawn from a buffer and deliberately apply no correction. The result is an off-policy estimate of an on-policy target, which is biased by the age of the stored segments; for SAC-GAE-R, whose score-function update also uses the replayed actions, the same holds for the policy gradient. Whether that bias matters in practice is an empirical question we return to in the next section. These two variants, SAC-λ-R and SAC-GAE-R, complete the grid in Figure 1. 

## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate all variants on fourteen tasks from three suites: classic control (Acrobot, CartPole, MountainCar, Pendulum; Osband et al. 2020) and MinAtar (Freeway, Asterix, Breakout, SpaceInvaders; Young & Tian 2019), both from Gymnax (Lange, 2022), and six MuJoCo control tasks from Brax (Ant, HalfCheetah, Hopper, Humanoid, Reacher, Walker2d; Freeman et al. 2021). Since Pendulum and Brax tasks have continuous action spaces and the remaining seven tasks discrete ones, we report the two groups separately. As external reference points we include PPO and, on discrete tasks, PQN, sampled under the same protocol. All implementations build on Jaxnasium (Ponse et al., 2026); additional results, experimental details, and pseudo-code are given in Appendices B, C and D. 

Evaluation protocol. We treat hyperparameters as part of the algorithm rather than tuning them away before reporting: a method that performs well only in a narrow region of its hyperparameter space demands more expertise to deploy on a new task than one that performs well broadly. For a fixed algorithm, let 

Xh,m denote the normalized return after training with hyperparameters h ∈ Λ on task m ∈ M . Even for fixed h and m it is random, through initialization, training seeds and hardware nondeterminism (Agarwal et al., 2021b), and through the stochasticity of environment and policy at evaluation. We therefore treat 

Xh,m : Ω train × Ωtest → R as a random variable and report properties of its distribution over h and m.Returns are normalized per task so that zero corresponds to the lowest achievable return and one to the 95th percentile of per-run mean returns pooled over all algorithms, which ties the scale neither to a single method nor to a single noisy draw. Configurations are drawn by Sobol sampling from a region centered on a configuration tuned once for SAC, with no prior tuning of any variant, which amounts to roughly 27 ,000 training runs; all sampled ranges are listed in Appendix C. Two properties of this protocol shape how the results should be read: Any statement about a distribution over hyperparameters is conditional on Λ: widening it lowers every score, and re-centering it can reorder methods. Centering on the baseline’s optimum is the conservative choice with respect to our variants. Moreover, Λ does not have the same dimension for every method: variants with a second critic carry additional learning rates and update counts, and are therefore sampled more sparsely at a fixed budget of H configurations per task, which again works against them. Each metric is defined in the subsection that reports it.

SAC-GAE-R attains the second-highest peak, far above SAC-GAE, though at a higher sensitivity; replacing on-policy rollouts by replayed segments thus lifts both target types. The advantage variants do not improve on SAC here either: SAC-A, which by design has a single critic, reaches a lower peak, and SAC-DA a similar peak at a higher sensitivity.

4.4 A closer look at the multi-step variants 

In continuous tasks, the λ-return critics, and SAC-λ-R in particular, are the clearest improvement over SAC. Three ingredients set them apart: the λ-weighting of the target, its horizon, and, for SAC-λ-R and SAC-GAE-R, the replay buffer. All three vary within our search (Appendix C), so each can be examined without further runs, within the sampled ranges. 

More weight on long returns does not help. Since λ is sampled independently of every other hyperparameter, its effect can be read off directly. Performance is flat or mildly decreasing in λ on both splits: of the sixteen slope estimates in Appendix B.5, two exclude zero, and both are negative. 

Nor does a longer horizon. The on-policy variants use rollouts of 8 to 32 steps and the replay-based ones replay segments of 32 to 256, so an aggregate comparison between the two families conflates replay with a 8horizon up to eight times longer. Within each family, the score does not show a consistent increase with the horizon N on either split (Fig. 4c,d). 

Replay does, despite the uncorrected bias. At N = 32, the one horizon both families share, the replay-based variants lead for both the λ-return and the GAE critic and on both splits (Fig. 4c,d), so the gain is not due to the horizon. Across all fourteen tasks, a randomly drawn run of a replay-based variant outperforms one of its on-policy counterparts for both critics (see also Fig. 8). This gain persists despite the uncorrected bias of the replayed targets. Since the replay-based variants also use larger batches and their own update schedule, this comparison bounds the combined effect of replay rather than isolating the bias. These results neither make the multi-step target dispensable nor isolate its contribution. Both the λ and the horizon ranges begin well above the one-step limit, so they show only that additional depth beyond them buys nothing. Relative to the one-step SAC, SAC-λ-R is clearly ahead on continuous tasks (Fig. 3b,d), but the two differ in more than their target, since the replay-based λ-return critic is trained on sampled segments, with larger batches and its own update schedule. We therefore attribute the gain of SAC-λ-R on continuous control to the combination it implements, λ-return targets computed on replayed segments, rather than to any single ingredient. What our data does rule out is that this gain depends on a large λ, a long horizon, or an off-policy correction. 

## 5 Related Work 

SAC as an instance of Policy Mirror Descent. Policy Mirror Descent (PMD; Geist et al. 2019; Vieillard et al. 2020; Xiao 2022; Vaswani et al. 2022; Lan 2023; Zhan et al. 2023) has emerged as a unifying framework for common RL algorithms. The PMD policy update (e.g. Lan 2023, Algorithm 1)

SAC is thus an instance of PMD, with h the negative Shannon entropy and no trust-region term, and the design choices studied here should transfer to other PMD algorithms, including those built on more general convex regularizers (Lee et al., 2019; Alfano et al., 2024). They are also complementary to efforts that add trust-region terms to SAC (Tomar et al., 2021; Neumann et al., 2025). 

Multi-step returns & advantages in maximum-entropy RL. Soft advantage estimators are not new: Huang et al. (2020) introduced a variant of GAE for entropy-regularized objectives, and Choe & Kim (2025) use a closely related soft GAE in PPO-style algorithms, whereas we integrate soft advantages into SAC. Multi-step targets have likewise been studied in entropy-regularized settings (Shil et al., 2023), including for transformer-based policies (Tian et al., 2025); Huang et al. (2021) and Łyskawa et al. (2025) train SAC on replayed λ- and n-step returns, corrected by importance sampling and truncated importance sampling, respectively. Kozuno et al. (2021) show that Peng’s Q( λ), which applies no correction, often outperforms conservative operators in continuous control, and characterize when its bias is benign; for SAC, however, they drop the entropy bonus from all but the last step of the target for stability. Our results add to these findings: the full soft λ-return, entropy terms included and with a learned temperature, trains stably from replay across a distribution of hyperparameters. More broadly, recent work revisits core design assumptions in SAC, particularly regarding critic learning and target construction (Shil et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Asad et al., 2025); we complement it with a systematic study of soft advantages, multi-step targets and replay within a unified SAC framework. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We asked which choices in SAC’s critic influence its outcomes by keeping the policy objective fixed and varying three of these choices: the learned quantity, the rollout depth of the target, and experience replay. In total, we evaluated across six critic variants over an entire random hyperparameter search on fourteen tasks. 9Learning soft advantages instead of soft Q-values neither makes SAC more robust to its hyperparameters nor improves its performance: the advantage and dueling critics stay close to SAC in the share of well-performing configurations and in transfer, and on continuous tasks SAC-A even reaches a lower peak. Since SAC’s policy gradient is unaffected by state-dependent shifts of the critic, this is a finding about critic learning: in our setting, fitting the state-dependent level of the value is not what limits SAC’s critic. The soft GAE critic, in turn, adds nothing over the simpler λ-return critic. The clearest gain comes from training a λ-return critic on replayed segments. In continuous tasks, SAC-λ-R holds the largest share of well-performing configurations, reaches the highest peak score at a lower relative sensitivity than SAC, and keeps most of that peak under transfer across tasks. Its lead over the on-policy SAC-λ persists at a matched horizon, while neither a larger λ nor a longer horizon helps within the sampled ranges; on discrete tasks, no variant improves on SAC. In line with earlier evidence for uncorrected multi-step targets, the missing off-policy correction does not prevent these gains, and our results extend this observation to the full soft λ-return. In practice, SAC’s critic parametrization can thus be chosen on grounds other than return, and multi-step targets can be paired with replay without correction. Several limitations bound these conclusions. Our continuous-control evidence rests on a limited number of continuous action tasks, and given SAC’s prevalence in that domain, further benchmarks are warranted. The replay comparison bounds the combined effect of target staleness, larger batches and a different update schedule rather than isolating the bias, and since SAC-λ-R also differs from SAC in its training regime, we attribute its gain to the combination rather than to the multi-step target alone. Any statement about a distribution over hyperparameters is conditional on the search space, which we centered on a configuration tuned for SAC. Finally, we studied the variants in isolation from other recent SAC extensions to attribute effects cleanly; combining them remains open.

## D Algorithm Details 

Following the main text, θ denotes critic parameters, ϕ value-network parameters and ψ policy parameters; target networks are indicated by ¯θ and ¯ϕ. N denotes the horizon (rollout or segment length), as in the main text, and C the period of the hard target-network updates. 

Implementation differences at a glance. All variants share the policy objective of Eq. 3, the learned temperature with its target-entropy schedule (Remark D.3) and the optimizer. Beyond the critic target, they differ as follows: (i) SAC, SAC-λ and SAC-λ-R use two critics (Remark D.1), whereas SAC-A and SAC-DA use, by design, a single advantage and value network; (ii) the GAE variants use an unsquashed Gaussian policy, the score-function gradient of Eq. 35 and batch-normalized advantages, which rescales the advantages relative to α and thus the effective temperature of their policy update; (iii) the on-policy variants take a single full-batch policy step per iteration, all others Kπ minibatch steps; (iv) the GAE variants use no target network.

Remark D.11 (Soft Peng’s Q( λ)) . With ¯V (s) = Ea∼πψ [Q¯θ (s, a )] + αH(πψ (·| s)), the recursion equals Eq. 11: with weight 1 − λ the return bootstraps from the soft value of the next state under the current policy rather 31 than from the action actually taken, and with weight λ it continues with the next return plus the entropy bonus at the next state. This is the soft analogue of Peng’s Q( λ) (Peng & Williams, 1994); unlike Kozuno et al. (2021), we keep the entropy bonus at every step. At segment ends and episode boundaries ( ei,n = 1) the trace is cut, and bootstrapping is suppressed only on termination ( di,n = 1), so on truncation the return bootstraps from the true final state.
