# Where It Breaks: Diagnosing Perception, Memory, and Decision Failures of a Vision-Language Agent in Pac-Man 

# Xinglu Wang, Edward Wang, Yong Zhang, Zhenan Fan 

Abstract 

Training an agent to play Pac-Man with only up, down, left and right actions raises a basic question: when performance is poor, what should be improved? Difficulty can arise from reading the screen or from choosing actions over a long se-quence. We examine these problems in a Qwen3.5-9B-based agent through separate perception checks and primitive-policy reinforcement-learning studies. In the policy study, which uses textual boards to remove image transcription from the decision interface, completion on eight held-out mazes increased from 0% before this optimization to 80.2% at a selected check-point, with three extra lives. The perception checks show that accurate static screenshot reading can coexist with er-rors during interaction. A separate policy comparison also shows that further optimization can accompany worse action choices. Together, these findings motivate concrete checks for post-training: evaluate perception in use, distinguish training success from full-game validation, and examine decisions and later performance rather than relying on one selected score. The study provides empirical lessons for improving future agent training and evaluation. 

# 1 Introduction 

Long-horizon spatial control requires an agent to interpret its observations and choose actions whose consequences unfold over many steps. Even when the action interface is only up, down, left and right, successful post-training is not estab-lished by a high training win rate or one strong validation checkpoint. The central question in this paper is which mea-sured perception and decision problems must be checked before interpreting a primitive-action RL result as a reliable improvement in play. We examine this question using the development records of a Qwen3.5-9B-based Pac-Man agent (Qwen Team 2026). The main setting is deliberately simple: a policy selects primitive directions to clear ordinary pellets while avoiding ghosts. Perception and policy learning are studied through distinct evidence families. Screenshot audits measure the reading of visible board state; historical ASCII primitive-action runs study decisions without image transcription. They are related parts of agent development, not one uniform end-to-end RL experiment. The primary empirical result is a documented normal-start validation improvement on eight fixed held-out mazes: check-point 0 before this policy optimization clears 0/96 episodes, and selected checkpoint 270 clears 77/96. The complete re-tained trajectory is less simple than that endpoint contrast. Its highest observed count is 78/96 at 335, while the last complete check at 425 is 60/96. Earlier-stage validation also shows mixed outcomes while lower-stage rehearsal contin-ues. These observations distinguish attained performance on a repeatedly used panel from stable retention or generaliza-tion to a fresh maze sample. The other records identify pitfalls relevant to interpreting that training result. A reader can transcribe a static image bank correctly yet make errors on different observations dur-ing interaction. In a separate ASCII primitive-policy lineage, continued optimization reduces validation clears and replay cases include immediately lethal choices. Neither result iden-tifies a single cause of RL failure: prompt compatibility, observation conditions, start distributions and the learning objective require separate tests. Higher success after end-to-end training likewise leaves open whether skills transfer or gains reflect overfitting to familiar layouts, including possible memorization of layout-specific action patterns. A small action vocabulary does not remove the scientific difficulty of evaluating a learned controller. The same avail-able direction can collect a pellet, enter a dangerous corridor or postpone a mistake whose consequence appears much later. A completion score combines these choices across the game. To improve an agent, a developer therefore needs ev-idence that connects the score to an actionable question: whether the observation was read correctly, whether the cho-sen direction was useful, and whether the evaluation still measures the intended starting task. The contribution is empirical rather than algorithmic. We organize the retained measurements around three compar-isons: selected and later normal-start checkpoints, static and interactive perception tasks, and training wins from differ-ent starting situations. Each comparison supplies a different unit of evidence. Together they show why learning-curve in-terpretation requires more than reporting an endpoint, while leaving the causal contribution of individual training choices open. Interactive-agent benchmarks compare performance across tasks and interfaces (Paglieri et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2025); studies of learned control and agent interfaces provide further context (Wang et al. 2025; Ichter et al. 2023). # 2 Primitive-action Task and Protocols 

The task is to clear ordinary pellets in Pac-Man while avoid-ing ghosts. The primitive policy outputs one of U/D/L/R ;the ASCII protocol restricts selection to its declared open-action support. Full-game panels retain their stated 512-environment-step budget and 0- or 3-extra-life setting. The primary eight-maze comparison uses three extra lives and normal resets. Power pellets need not be cleared. Actions advance the game between model decisions. Exact stage di-mensions, game rules and timing contracts are retained in the appendices. The observation conditions must be kept distinct. Per-ception audits present RGB screenshots and score struc-tured board/entity readings. The primitive-policy RL panels presented here use native ASCII boards with the declared state/history summaries, bypassing image transcription. A correct reading score is not a game completion score, and the ASCII result does not measure an image-only policy. The precise input and action contracts are given in Appendix G. The historical protocol documents a frozen multi-layout catalog and fixed validation layouts, rather than new mazes generated at every update. The eight-maze endpoint compari-son has paired layout, environment-seed and policy-sampling bindings. Complete per-training-slot map identities are not retained, so panel names alone cannot independently prove global train/validation separation. We use the documented held-out panel for descriptive validation and keep stronger transfer claims separate. Catalog examples and binding limits appear in Appendix M. The ASCII policy receives a spaced board representa-tion together with the state/navigation history declared for that run, and emits one direction token at each decision. It therefore has to connect positions, available exits and mov-ing threats to an action, but it does not have to locate those objects in pixels. In the perception studies, by contrast, the model reads visible board contents or actor positions from a screenshot. This separation lets us ask whether a failure concerns reading or action selection without claiming that text input makes all state interpretation correct. Episode-level grouping. Rollouts from the same start state are compared by their episode returns. Pooling intermediate returns from different decisions into one group is misleading: the pooled mean estimates the value of no particular state, and a single death penalty inflates the standard deviation and compresses all other advantages. In a three-episode example, changing one late death from −100 to +2 flipped the advan-tage of an unchanged early decision from +0 .17 to −1.26 

under pooling, while episode-level grouping changed it only from −1.41 to −1.31 . This is a worked arithmetic diagnostic, not an independently evaluated training intervention. A return-based objective can reinforce an episode that re-covers from a poor move, without assigning that move a separate outcome. This motivates checking group composi-tion and action-level evidence when learning stalls; it is not evidence that the surrogate or any one reward choice caused the observed regressions. 

## 3.4 Questions answered by the comparisons 

The learning trajectory asks whether the policy attains bet-ter normal-start completion on the documented validation layouts and whether that level persists. Its paired initial and selected endpoints give a more specific comparison than a training-batch curve alone. Later checks add a second ques-tion: does continued optimization retain the attained result and the earlier-stage performance? The stage allocation is reported because an apparent retention result has a different meaning when earlier tasks remain in training. The perception panels ask a component question. They compare generated descriptions with visible-pixel labels, counting complete images or query outputs rather than game wins. The independent ASCII regression asks whether de-cision problems remain when image transcription is absent. These panels cannot be pooled into a single success estimate: their observations, checkpoints and outcomes differ. Their usefulness is in choosing the next comparison. A reading discrepancy calls for an observation-specific test; a textual decision regression calls for inspecting action choices and the learning signal under the declared inputs.

## 4.4 Further optimization can coincide with worse decisions 

A separate primitive-action ASCII lineage falls from 59/96 validation clears at an early checkpoint to 37/96 later in training on the same 96-attempt panel. Thirty matched re-gression cases were replayed; retained cases include imme-diately lethal choices while an exit is available, or earlier choices that enter a ghost pincer before the final loss of options. An available exit does not prove a safe complete escape, and these cases do not identify one cause of the re-gression. The independent historical records and checkpoint conditions are retained in Appendix P.6. ASCII removes image transcription, but not state inter-pretation or long-horizon decision difficulty. The observed regression therefore motivates checks of action choices and the learning objective even when pixels are absent. It does not establish that RL is generally harmful or that any particular reward or credit-assignment mechanism caused the decline. For the independent ASCII regression, the useful next check is therefore not another static-image test. One must examine which alternatives were available at the decision, whether the policy assigns them different probabilities, and whether the training objective distinguishes the resulting out-comes. The existing cases motivate these checks but do not identify terminal-reward credit assignment, filtering or pol-icy drift as the cause. They connect an aggregate decline to concrete action errors without turning a local replay into proof of a complete-game repair. 

Primitive decisions have both immediate and delayed con-sequences. The small action vocabulary helps separate two questions that a completion score combines. First, can the policy choose a direction that is open in the current obser-vation? Second, can a sequence of such choices complete the remaining game? Masking closed directions addresses the first question, not the second. A locally available move can lead toward a threat or leave fewer alternatives later. The replayed regression cases illustrate this temporal distinction: some losses concern the final choice, while others are pre-ceded by entry into a pincer. Counting only the final invalid or lethal move would miss the earlier decision that changed the available options. The learning signal also spans that sequence. The episode-based surrogate in Section 3.3 weights sampled decisions us-ing the episode advantage; a binary completion count sum-marizes the final outcome. Neither quantity is a direct label of the quality of every intermediate direction. The worked grouping example shows why the population used to compare returns matters even when one early action is unchanged. It supplies a reason to inspect how returns become advantages before changing the policy objective, rather than assuming that a larger training win fraction means that every relevant action has improved. 

Use the two curves to locate the next decision question. 

The training and validation panels make this distinction ob-servable at different scales. Training records describe per-formance on the situations presented to the learner, includ-ing short remaining objectives; normal-start validation asks whether the policy can reach completion through the full se-quence of decisions. A successful suffix demonstrates useful behavior from its sampled state, but leaves the earlier route to that state untested. This is why the remaining-objective table and source-coverage counts accompany the learning curve: they explain what part of the task a training win actually exercises. 

# 5 Supported Post-training Pitfalls 

The findings suggest how to choose the next measurement or training change. 

Check what is being learned. The reading and action re-sults answer different questions. Static-image accuracy tests a component under a specified prompt and decoding condi-tion; normal-start game completion tests the consequences of many decisions. When interactive reading is wrong, addi-tional policy optimization need not address that error. When decisions regress on ASCII, better pixel reading alone cannot explain or repair the observation. A useful evaluation there-fore tests the suspect component and then measures whether its change improves the intended game outcome. 

Check what a win represents. Restart training deliber-ately changes the problem presented to the learner. It can expose useful situations, but a win near the end of a game is not interchangeable with a win from the initial state. The all-win batch and its concentrated source states make this distinction concrete. Reporting first-pass outcomes along-side accepted batches helps separate sampling from filter-ing; retaining normal-start validation prevents a change in the training metric from silently becoming a claim of full-game mastery. 

Check what survives continued optimization. The at-tained 80.2% checkpoint is useful evidence of improvement on the retained eight mazes. The later trajectory is equally informative for deciding what to test next: the selected peak, subsequent declines and mixed earlier-stage outcomes show why a single reported score is insufficient. Continued re-hearsal rules out describing these records as retention with-out old-task exposure. Follow-up comparisons should distin-guish checkpoint selection, layout transfer and repeatability across training seeds, rather than collapsing them into one generalization claim. 

Use the measurement unit to choose an intervention. Acomponent score and a task score can disagree without ei-ther measurement being wrong. The static reader bank tests descriptions of particular images; the training curve counts success from sampled starts; normal-start validation tests completion from the intended initial condition. For example, repairing an output-format mismatch should first be checked against the corresponding reading contract, then against the downstream outcome it is expected to affect. Improving restart suffixes should be reported as such until normal-start performance changes. The distinction keeps an engineering change testable instead of allowing every score increase to stand for an unspecified improvement in the whole agent. 

Separate attained skill from checkpoint selection. Re-peated validation is valuable for detecting regressions, but the best observed checkpoint and the last observed checkpoint answer different questions. The former records an attained level on the selection panel; the latter helps assess whether later optimization retained it. The retained trajectory supplies both, and the earlier-stage panels expose changes hidden by a single final-stage average. A future comparison can select a checkpoint on one panel and reserve a separate layout sam-ple for reporting, while repeating training to study variability across seeds. Those measurements would address questions that the current repeated panel cannot settle; they are not pre-requisites for reporting the existing historical improvement accurately. 

Limitations and future work. The final two training stages have longer logged updates than earlier stages, while validation can decline during extended training. Logged time combines waiting and training; it is not calendar duration, per-episode cost or inference latency, and it does not increase monotonically by stage. Long-horizon credit assignment and reward design are possible explanations to test, not isolated causes of these declines. The study also lacks independent training-seed replication and a complete historical per-slot map manifest. Complementary fixed-model runtime-control compar-isons and exploratory assistance records are retained in Ap-pendices H and F.1. They motivate future studies that im-prove policy post-training and runtime support together, us-ing the lessons from both. A combined or autonomous self-improvement gain has not been measured; this is a research direction, not a new algorithmic result. 

# 6 Conclusion 

Primitive directions do not make long-horizon learning triv-ial. The retained Pac-Man records show an attained normal-start validation improvement, but also fluctuating later perfor-mance, mixed retention under rehearsal, observation-specific reading errors and a separate decision regression. # B Training Details 

Scope. This section describes historical headline runs. Their remaining provenance gaps are kept explicit; the sep-arately verified action-head and ASCII restart settings are specified in Appendix G and must not be substituted for these runs. 

Optimisation. Group-relative policy optimisation (Shao et al. 2024) with a terminal win reward (1 for clearing all reg-ular pellets within 512 decisions without losing a life). Train-ing on 8×H800 GPUs; inference on one GPU per model. These historical records do not recover the group size, learn-ing rate, KL coefficient or per-update rollout budget; verified settings below belong only to their named runs. The histor-ical episode-grouping arithmetic diagnostic is discussed in Section 3.3; it is not an evaluated intervention. 

Curriculum. Tiers grow maze size first and ghost count second: 13 ×13 /2 ghosts → 15 ×15 /2 → 15 ×15 /4 →

17 ×17 /4 → 19 ×21 /4 → 21 ×25 /4 (columns × rows). A tier is promoted when the training win rate over a three-batch win-dow passes a threshold (70–80%) with at least 384 episodes on 8 layouts, on two consecutive checks; promotion takes effect after the optimiser update. 13/16 rollout groups come from the current tier and 3/16 review easier tiers. Validation never triggers promotion. In this configuration every episode starts from the initial state. This ordinary-start configuration is separate from the verified failure-state restart continuation in Appendix G; it does not imply that every curriculum run used ordinary starts. 

Fit versus generalisation. The pipeline can fit fixed train-ing seeds (training-seed evaluation 83.33%), while the best validation win rate in a separate run was 44.17%; these are different evaluation populations and should not be read as one learning curve. 

Preventing entropy collapse. We use Clip-Cov (Cui et al. 2025), which detaches the gradient of a small fraction of to-kens whose log-probability and advantage have the highest covariance, restricting those selected token gradients; its ef-fect in these runs is not isolated. Historical token fractions, thresholds and matched with/without entropy curves are un-available; no causal benefit is attributed to Clip-Cov. Raising the upper clipping bound (Clip-Higher) and discarding roll-out groups with identical rewards (Dynamic Sampling), both from DAPO (Yu et al. 2025), are alternatives that target the same collapse and the zero-signal groups of C3, respectively; we did not use them. 

Restart-state provenance. Appendix G details the verified failure-state restart continuation, including pre-death sam-pling and source concentration. Source episodes can eventu-ally succeed; these starts are not uniformly sampled backward from a successful terminal state. A controlled success-versus-distance curve remains unavailable. 

# C Engineering Pitfalls 

Several failures during development came from the training system rather than from the learning problem. We list them so that others can rule them out early; each result must be tied to its source and checkpoint bindings to identify which fixes were in force. This appendix is a catalogue of engineering issues, not a completed timing audit of every historical result. The per-run revision/fix-status ledger is incomplete. 

Rollout–training consistency (fixed). 

• Lost action constraints. A missing cross-process patch dropped the allowed-token list, and in the vLLM v1 en-gine a row swap of the allowed-token mask written as a tuple assignment of tensor views overwrote one row with the other, so a request could sample outside its legal set. Fixed by copying through a cloned buffer. 

• Temperature. Rollout and training rescoring used differ-ent temperatures; aligned. 

• Multimodal position IDs. The rollout engine’s fused 1D RoPE kernel read only the temporal row of the 3 × N

multimodal position IDs, and the training path did not generate per-axis (temporal, height, width) positions for the vision tokens. Fixed with an mRoPE-aware kernel and per-sample position IDs preserved through splitting and packing, verified on full-model action log-probabilities rather than kernel tests alone. 

• Recurrent-state leakage. Packing several samples into one FSDP sequence leaked Gated DeltaNet recurrent state across samples. Fixed by running samples separately and accumulating gradients. 

Training-system reliability (fixed). 

• CPU offload device mismatch. With FSDP2 parameter offload, the vision model read its positional-embedding weight’s device while the shard was still on CPU, created indices on CPU, and then indexed a CUDA weight. Fixed by taking the device from the multimodal input. 

• Uneven micro-batches. Token-balanced allocation across data-parallel ranks does not equalise the number of se-quences, so ranks could run different numbers of micro-batches and hence different numbers of collectives, caus-ing errors or deadlocks. Fixed by padding with zero-loss dummy rows so all ranks pack the same number of rows. 

Remaining training–inference mismatch. Enabling prefix-cache reuse in the inference engine increased the gap between rollout and training log-probabilities; we kept it for throughput and did not implement a correction. Smaller numerical differences also remain from operator implementations and precision (projection results that depend on padded length, convolution and activation outputs, Q/K normalisation, the Gated DeltaNet recurrence, BF16 logit rounding) and from batch composition in the inference engine. These observations concern historical configurations that retained prefix reuse. The audited ASCII restart continuation disables prefix/radix caching and CUDA graphs; no claim about its prefix-cache error follows from those earlier observations. No matched numerical with/without prefix-reuse gap is available for these historical configurations. 

# D Harness Details 

Options. At each decision the harness offers up to 4 Col-lect options (C0–C3), 4 Avoid options (A0–A3), and 2 Eliminate options (E0–E1), each mapped to a fixed single-letter code (e.g., B/F/G/H, J/K/M/N, P/Q) whose target changes with the state. After the model picks a code, Route-option harness converts the target into U/D/L/R moves, re-validates the option at every step, and calls the model again when the target is reached, the option becomes invalid, or a step limit is hit. 

Information. In the engine-state condition the harness reads Pac-Man and ghost positions and states from the en-gine at every step, so that condition is not purely visual. The verified factorial route gates and shared fallback boundary are specified in Appendix H; the public-memory execution sketch is in Appendix G.1. These descriptions apply to their named versions, not every historical harness.

# G Experiment-specific Protocols 

The experiments below answer different questions and re-tain their own training distributions, checkpoints and evalu-ation panels. We distinguish optimizer updates from roll-out batches, training starts from normal-start validation, and an execution seed from a new map. In particular, the fixed-map visual action-head experiment is not a replication of the multi-map ASCII curriculum, and neither supplies an unseen-layout success rate for the complete screenshot-driven system. 

## G.1 Visible Inputs and Public-feedback Memory 

This subsection describes the later typed-receipt public-feedback variant, not the early events prototype in Figure 6, whose retained feedback has no relative-displacement vector (Appendix P.6). The public-feedback variant starts with one screenshot-based map transcription and then reads visible ac-tors after native actions. It maintains static terrain separately from ordinary pellets, power pellets and actor estimates; writ-ing an actor never overwrites remembered terrain. Coordi-nates are zero-based [row , column] , increasing downward and rightward. The initial map must have the prescribed di-mensions and exactly one Pac-Man. The strict parser retains hidden cells as unknown rather than replacing them with engine truth. After an action, the environment-facing adapter projects a typed public receipt containing the frame number, elapsed logic ticks, self-relative motion, localized pickup events, death, respawn, terminal status and ghost-eaten events. This projection contains no hidden map, ghost coordinates, ghost identities, planned ghost paths, random-number-generator state or private vulnerability timer. A receipt is consumed once and must follow the preceding frame. Pellet contacts update the appropriate set before death/respawn handling. Unlocalized contacts retain uncertainty. Death clears vulner-ability memory; respawn restores the visually observed initial spawn; terminal states clear the current position. Position is otherwise propagated only onto known floor. The public vul-nerability rule supplies an interval based on a 360-logic-tick duration, rather than an exact private timer. The actor query asks for all visible characters, including partial occlusions, with a Pac-Man coordinate or null and a list of ghost coordinates and states (normal, vulnerable or eyes), sorted by row and column. The parser preserves extra detections and multiplicity. Temporal ghost association min-imizes Manhattan displacement over possible assignments; ambiguous ties remain uncertain. Its per-observation dis-placement bounds are two cells for normal/vulnerable ghosts and four for eyes. These are visual estimates, not persistent engine identities or guaranteed next-step headings. Existing options continue only while their public validity checks hold; a new Agent decision is required when an option is absent or invalidated. 

Audit boundary. Privileged engine state is retained by a separate replay auditor. It checks RGB, state/RNG restora-tion, receipts and action-before state, but is not available to the policy or gradient computation. An audit failure blocks the affected training update instead of providing corrective labels. A perception fault is recorded separately with an un-defined game reward; it is not relabeled as a death or an or-dinary zero-reward loss. Exact audited Pac-Man, pellet and clock state does not imply exact ghost perception.

## G.2 D5A Perception SFT and Image Contract 

The documented reconstruction recipe uses Qwen3.5-9B and two supervised stages: vision encoder/merger adaptation fol-lowed by language-only LoRA for visual questions. The reconstruction regenerates screenshots and labels from the game; it is not asserted to be byte-identical to the original multi-round D5 dataset. We therefore distinguish this exe-cutable recipe from measurements attached to the original frozen D5A checkpoints. Native screenshots use 16-pixel cells. The shared image function applies exactly 2× bicubic enlargement, producing 32-pixel cells, with processor limits of 4,096 and 537,600 pixels. The same contract is used for SFT, evaluation and serving, targeting one visual token per board cell for boards up to 25 × 21 . The prompt’s board coordinates remain native grid coordinates after enlargement. The system instruction is “You answer questions about the visible Pacman screen-shot precisely.” The user prompt specifies board dimensions, coordinate axes and symbols: wall, ghost-house door, ordi-nary/power pellets, Pac-Man, three ghost states, empty and hidden cells. It requests JSON only. Hidden labels describe pixel occlusion, including sprites and the HUD; they do not reveal objects behind an occluder. The language-model chat template disables thinking, and cross-entropy is computed only on answer and end-of-sequence tokens; image/prompt and padding tokens are masked. The vision implementation uses four-way FSDP2, FP32 master parameters and AdamW states, BF16 computation and FP32 gradient reduction. The language stage accumu-lates 16 single-example microbatches on one GPU; all vision parameters and non-language adapters are explicitly frozen after adapter construction. The vision sampling-plan seed is 20261008; the language sampling-plan seed is 20261010. Plan generation and model initialization seeds are distinct fields and should not be conflated. Reconstruction test fam-ilies include two native levels excluded from training and unseen procedural 25 × 21 maps; the size panel covers 18 sizes from 11 ×11 through 25 ×21 . The shipped-map stream panel tests unfiltered map transcription. These VQA/whole-map scores are not full-game win rates.

This experiment freezes the base model, perception and public-feedback harness, and learns only a rank-8, alpha-8 residual at the output head: z = z0 + ( α/r )U (Dx ). # P Expanded Context, Methods, and Interpretation 

## P.1 Related Work 

Tasks and evaluation Interactive game benchmarks. Games expose the con-sequences of sequences of decisions, rather than isolated answers. BALROG evaluates VLM agents across games and compares visual and textual interfaces (Paglieri et al. 2024). lmgame-Bench studies game control with perception and memory support, including transfer through game-specific RL (Hu et al. 2025). These benchmarks ask how agents per-form across tasks and interfaces. Our Pac-Man study instead follows one development process, using targeted compar-isons and traceable failures to ask which explanations a re-sult supports. It does not introduce a general benchmark or establish a cross-game ranking. 

Potential problems Perception, spatial understanding and visual uncertainty. 

Fine-grained tests reveal difficulties with simple patterns and precise visual localisation (Tong et al. 2024; Rahmanzade-hgervi et al. 2024). SpatialVLM improves spatial question answering with spatial training data (Chen et al. 2024), while Thinking in Space examines spatial recall from sequential vi-sual observations (Yang et al. 2025). Uncertainty-aware map-ping represents uncertain semantic readings (Bajpai et al. 2025); monoVLN acquires further views when observations are incomplete (Lu et al. 2025). These works distinguish unreliable readings from missing information. Our largely visible board can still be misread, and the resulting error can affect later state and actions. Static answer accuracy therefore measures a different requirement from reliable closed-loop control. 

State memory and updating. Remembered information can become stale, as studied by When Memory Lies (Sun and Zhang 2026); EvolvingNav maintains time-indexed beliefs about moving objects outside the current view (Gao et al. 2026). Belief revision provides a classical foundation for reconciling new evidence with retained beliefs (Boutilier, Friedman, and Halpern 1998). Our fixed-maze case concerns a complementary failure: a new actor misreading overwrites correct terrain. The traced failure and bounded checks of revised rules support that diagnosis, while their full-episode benefit remains a separate question. State uncertainty is thus broader than uncertainty in the current image. 

Decision, planning and skill execution. Even useful ob-servations and retained state must be turned into feasible action sequences. Uncertainty about action consequences can persist after image transcription is removed; it is not simply visual uncertainty. Our textual controls, action-head training outcomes and fixed-weight harness comparisons ad-dress different parts of this problem. They provide evidence about particular decision and execution limitations, without completing full-episode causal replacements of perception, memory and policy. 

Solving approaches Teacher supervision and imitation. PlayJev is a model re-lease that first imitates programmatic action teachers through behavior cloning, then obtains teacher action labels for states visited by the learner (OmniJev 2026). DAgger (Dataset Ag-gregation) adds these visited-state labels to earlier data and retrains, addressing distribution shift and compounding er-rors when the learner’s mistakes take it away from demonstra-tion states (Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell 2011). This supplies a dense-supervision alternative to our reward-driven action training. We do not assume an additional competent action teacher, while retaining pretrained models and environment-derived perception supervision. Different game and obser-vation contracts prevent treating the releases as a matched performance comparison. 

RL and post-training. DQN learns game control from reward (Mnih et al. 2015); hybrid reward decomposition ad-dresses the structure of Ms. Pac-Man control (van Seijen et al. 2017). VAGEN trains multi-turn visual agents to rea-son about states and transitions (Wang et al. 2025). These approaches motivate learning better decisions, but a changed overall score does not by itself identify which component im-proved. We report protocol-specific training outcomes along-side comparisons that hold model weights fixed. Our contri-bution is evidence-grounded development experience, not a new general RL objective or demonstrated transfer of the resulting lessons. 

Curricula and start-state design. Backplay moves starts backwards through a successful demonstration (Resnick et al. 2018); reverse curriculum generation expands starts out-wards from a goal (Florensa et al. 2017). Both organize practice to make useful learning signals easier to obtain. Our failure-state restart lineage instead samples states be-fore recorded deaths. Concentrated training starts and weaker normal-start validation motivate checking the training distri-bution, but do not isolate a causal benefit or harm of reverse curricula. 

Harnesses, robust updates and executable options. 

NavHarness combines goal generation, completion verifi-cation and memory compression (Shi et al. 2026). Ro-bust mapping rejects unreliable constraints (Sünderhauf and Protzel 2012), and occupancy integration fuses uncertain ev-idence (Hornung et al. 2013). Skill selection (Ichter et al. 2023) and temporally extended options (Sutton, Precup, and Singh 1999) structure decisions around executable behav-ior. Our fixed-weight comparisons examine bounded risk checking and route binding under a shared candidate scaf-fold within their named panel. Together with traceable failure evidence, these comparisons connect a development problem to an intervention and its actual validation unit; they neither introduce a new options framework nor establish universal gains or a full-game benefit for every state-update revision. 

## P.2 Problem Setup 

Environment. We use a grid-based Pac-Man engine with mazes of up to 25 × 21 cells (rows × columns), up to four ghosts, and an episode horizon of 512 environment steps. An episode succeeds when all regular pellets are cleared before the agent runs out of lives; we evaluate both a 0-extra-life and a 3-extra-life budget. The source-bound normal-ghost, power-pellet and portal rules for the newer paired panels appear in Appendix K; earlier generations and variable-size curricula keep their own source contracts.

terminated , truncated and info . The runner’s learn-ing reward is computed separately. Agent decomposition. Let st be the true engine state and 

ot the observation the agent is permitted to see. For visual harnesses, we use the following functional decomposition: 

zt = Vθ (ot) (perception) (3) 

mt = U (mt−1, z t, e t) (state maintenance) (4) 

at = πϕ(ot, m t, Ct) (decision) (5) 

Vθ is the VLM’s reading of the screen (map cells and en-tity positions), U is the harness’s update rule, et is explicitly permitted feedback, and πϕ selects either a primitive move or one of the route candidates Ct proposed by the harness. These are functional dependencies, not full-memory model inputs. Protocol-specific renderers select state/history sum-maries and candidates; direct ASCII policies bypass image transcription. A failure in the final score can originate in any of the three stages, and errors in an earlier stage can be amplified by a later one. 

Information contract. We distinguish the visual informa-tion conditions and a separate textual control. Engine-state 

(diagnostic reference): the harness uses the engine fields de-clared by the panel. In the inspected current recipe, the model receives a screenshot plus selected state and candidate sum-maries; this current renderer is not a source mapping for every historical frozen panel. Screenshot-feedback : in the later typed-receipt variant, all state comes from screenshots plus public interaction feedback—the realised relative dis-placement of Pac-Man, pellet and life events, and episode termination—together with publicly stated game rules such as tunnel teleportation and respawn locations. The engine’s full map, absolute coordinates, and ghost internals (targets, timers, paths) are never available to the agent in this con-dition and are used only for scoring and failure attribution. Because realised-displacement feedback is more informa-tive than a binary moved/blocked signal, we report it as part of the interface rather than as a property of the memory algorithm. ASCII controls replace pixels with the native tex-tual board/state under their own protocol. They remove im-age transcription from the loop; they are distinct from the screenshot-feedback condition and do not measure visual grounding. External screenshot-plus-structured-state base-lines likewise retain their stated information access rather than being called image-only. 

Metrics. Besides completion, we report pellet clearance, deaths per episode, timeouts, harness aborts (kept separate from deaths), model calls, and environment steps. Static perception is measured independently by whole-map exact match and per-entity accuracy. Generalisation is reported separately for new random seeds on seen layouts and for held-out layouts. Each panel retains its own layout/seed split and denominator rather than a universal train/test pool. A new execution seed on one map is not a held-out layout. The action-head figure shows marginal Wilson 95% intervals; paired outcomes and matched-success costs are reported sep-arately. The perception panels distinguish frames, repeated decodes and query records (Appendix J). Appendix L distinguishes marginal rates, paired differ-ences and clustered/repeated observations; its intervals refer to their explicit fixed-map sampling model.

The Route-option harness Rather than emitting prim-itive moves, the VLM selects among route candidates gener-ated by the Route-option harness. Options come in three families—Collect (reach nearby pellets while keeping an escape route), Avoid (move to a distant junction with enough exits when ghosts approach), and Eliminate (chase an edi-ble ghost with time to spare)—and are presented as single-letter codes whose meaning changes with the state, so a code is not a fixed direction command; this does not rule out other shortcuts. Legal-code decoding and matching the rollout/rescoring support and temperature are the intended policy contract. The verified action-head panel uses that contract; it is not a blanket qualification of all historical runs, some of which retained a cache-related mismatch (Ap-pendix C). Two mechanisms are studied as separate factors: 

• Risk check —before a candidate is offered, the harness estimates over a bounded horizon whether a ghost can reach the route first, and ranks or filters candidates ac-cordingly. It is a bounded check, not a guarantee of route safety. 

• Route binding —after the VLM picks a route, the har-ness executes it across several steps until completion or an interrupt condition, while the factorial control instead retains the target and replans within a capped commit-ment; both can reuse a model choice. This follows the idea of temporally extended options (Sutton, Precup, and Singh 1999). Fallback is protocol-specific: historical refusal stops the episode when safe execution is unavailable, whereas later risk-ranked fallback may continue despite residual risk. Only the factorial arms share the same candidate scaffold; his-torical harness generations can also differ in proposal and information rules (Appendix H).

## P.4 Discussion: Deciding What to Fix 

The five insights suggest a rule for spending a finite devel-opment budget: locate the failing stage before choosing the intervention . Table 19 separates an observed diagnostic sig-nal from the next intervention to validate. Evidence for an input contract, a typed update rule or an exact replay concerns those specific fields; it is not by itself evidence that changing a component rescues a full game. The human investigator chooses which comparison can resolve the remaining uncer-tainty. 

Why brute force was not enough. Direct RL on primitive actions asked one model to solve all three stages at once, from a sparse terminal reward. Errors at several stages can confound the terminal signal. Separation makes the questions testable: perception can be measured as supervised transcrip-tion, state maintenance as a rule with explicit invariants, and decision quality under controlled inputs. It does not guarantee that the remaining policy learns: the action-head continuation did not improve the fixed panel. Denser credit assignment, in-cluding reward decomposition as in van Seijen et al. (2017), is a hypothesis to test against the observed shaped-reward failure, not a demonstrated fix here. This is consistent with the view of an agent as a coordinated system—a slower plan-ner selecting among options and a faster executor carrying them out—rather than a single policy to be scaled. 

Separating state accuracy from usable control informa-tion. Fit to geometric and spatial reasoning. The failures we observe are spatial: localising entities to the right grid cell, maintaining a consistent topology over time, and reasoning about whether a ghost can reach an intersection first. Our results suggest that spatial-reasoning benchmarks for VLMs should evaluate these abilities in the loop and over time, because the static versions can be nearly solved while the closed-loop task is not. 

Future work: learning from failures. Future researchers could use the student’s actual failure states to select correc-tive exercises for post-training RL, compare a search-based teacher with simpler rules, and test whether action supervi-sion or optional reasoning supervision transfers to indepen-dent play. Such transfer remains to be evaluated. The central evaluation must remove the teacher and extra reasoning as-sistance, check for regression on prior skills, and account for teacher and training costs. Our small single-goal interven-tion screen (Appendix F.1) tests assistance at inference, not learned improvement.

## P.5 Conclusion 

A VLM agent that can describe a Pac-Man board can still fail to play it, and the reasons are not uniform. In our case study the agent broke at three distinct places—nearsighted perception, memory corrupted by a single misread, and lethal decisions made on correct state—and each points to a dif-ferent intervention and validation requirement. We establish bounded improvements and failure diagnoses, not a complete solution of all three stages. Scaling post-training without first locating the failing stage spent compute without addressing the bottleneck. We hope the information contract, matched-state replay, and the resulting decision guide help others find where their own spatial agents break.

Model and Intervention Specification Visual adaptation. Transcription errors motivated vision-encoder/merger adaptation with language frozen, followed by VQA-only language LoRA (Hu et al. 2022). Prompt, schema, preprocessing and decoding must match their trained contracts; apparent perception fail-ure can otherwise be an interface mismatch. 

Options and execution. The Route-option harness of-fers state-dependent Collect, Avoid and Eliminate routes as single-letter codes. A code is not a fixed direction. A bounded risk check filters or ranks routes; the factorial’s im-proved gate uses a two-move motion-envelope check and a near-target escape guard (Appendix H). Route binding exe-cutes the selected route until completion or interruption. The factorial control retains its target but replans within a capped commitment, so it can also reuse model choices. Arms share a candidate scaffold and fallback only within that factorial (Ap-pendix H). Historical refusal fallback can stop an episode; later risk-ranked fallback can continue despite residual risk. Neither is a safety guarantee. 

Binding a reported update to a model. The paired action-head panel learns only a rank-8, alpha-8 output residual with 2,019,328 FP32 parameters; base weights, perception and the harness remain frozen. This is distinct from the language-LoRA diagnosis and primitive ASCII training. Each update collects eight 0-extra-life episodes: two execution seeds, four samples per seed. Four returns from the same seed form an advantage group; constant-return groups receive zero advan-tage. The reward is zero without a clear and adds declared life/speed bonuses on a clear. Faulty episodes have no game reward and are not replaced. The loss uses a clipped surrogate (0.2) and KL coefficient 0.01 against the frozen base, on iden-tical legal support at temperature 0.7. AdamW uses learning rate 5 × 10 −4 and gradient clipping at one. Recomputed be-havior log probabilities must agree within 2 × 10 −4 before an update; a completed optimizer step, saved Adam counter, parameter hash and reload-logit check establish the counted checkpoint. The reported twenty-update model contains sev-enteen additional updates after the initial three, not a planned training budget or a count of collected batches. Appendix G.3 preserves the seed schedule and complete loss/weighting de-tails. 

Memory and verification. The revised screenshot-feedback rule separates static terrain, event-based pellets and re-observed actors; actor conflicts cannot overwrite terrain, and uncertain cells remain unknown. This is a revised pro-tocol, not a property retroactively shared by older harnesses. We preserve native state/RNG, observations, choices and ex-ecution records for exact replay. Private truth audits interfaces without repairing deployed inputs. A matched-state oracle re-placement diagnoses component sensitivity; a replay match only verifies the record. Full-episode component rescue re-quires its own outcome comparison. Extended methods and historical engineering qualifications appear in Appendices P and C. 

Development Findings and Secondary Panels We present the findings in the order in which they shaped the project; each insight motivated the next intervention. The evidence spans several historical experiments with different models, feedback, start distributions and checkpoints. Pair-ing applies only within the explicitly identified panels; we do not pool their rates. The newer full-episode panels use 64 execution seeds on one fixed map, not 64 independent layouts. The independently reloaded profile in Table 20 is separate from legacy exploratory percentages, whose un-resolved provenance is retained in Appendix I.1. Detailed verified protocols appear in Appendix G. Historical primitive-action training and curriculum diffi-culties. Our first agent mapped each screenshot directly to one primitive move token and was trained with group-relative policy optimisation (Shao et al. 2024) and a curriculum over maze size and ghost count. It improved on the easiest stages but stalled on harder ones: after five to six days of train-ing, success on later stages had not converged. Stage-specific compute curves are not available in the retained historical records. Alongside the engineering faults catalogued in Ap-pendix C, we identified five interacting learning and interface challenges. Per-run fix status remains incomplete; we do not attribute every historical failure to learning alone. 

• C1 Limited exploration and shortcut risk. Training-seed fit without matching held-out performance, weak visual transcription and low policy entropy are consis-tent with layout-specific shortcuts. They do not localize memorisation to the language component or prove that visual error caused entropy collapse. Sparse terminal re-wards can reinforce a narrow set of successful trajecto-ries, consistent with the covariance account of entropy collapse (Cui et al. 2025). We used Clip-Cov to limit this concentration (Appendix B), but the absent matched with/without curves prevent a causal claim about how much it preserved exploration. 

• C2 Credit assignment under sparse reward. The only reward came at the end of a long episode. Training was slow and unstable, with reward sometimes dropping for long stretches. A win that followed a recovered mistake rewarded every action in the episode, including the mis-take. Denser shaped rewards were not a safe fix: in one run, shaped evaluation reward rose from 48.78 to 126.38 while wins fell from 15/96 to 2/96, consistent with an ob-jective mismatch, although that historical run was not in-dependently reloaded and re-evaluated. We therefore kept a terminal win reward and grouped returns per episode rather than per intermediate state (Appendix B). 

• C3 Cold start and curriculum calibration. On hard stages an initial policy may never win, leaving groups with no reward variation and no learning signal. The initial model also oscillated at the start. Starting closer to success, as in Backplay (Resnick et al. 2018), is one possible intervention. Our verified continuation instead samples states before recorded deaths, including from episodes that eventually win. Its training-start concentra-tion and separate normal-start validation are described below; it is not a controlled demonstration of Backplay benefit. 

• C4 A single frame is not a Markov state. One screenshot does not directly expose ghost velocity (normal pupils in this renderer track Pac-Man, not motion; Appendix K), separates overlapping ghosts poorly, and does not show how long a frightened ghost has left before it turns dan-gerous again. Short of reading the engine’s internal state, acting well requires memory across frames. Asking the model to emit one action token per step also forces it to plan implicitly, which makes its behaviour a black box. 

• C5 Cost. Every step is a model call, and an episode has hundreds of steps, so rollouts are expensive and slow down training further. With options, one model decision covers 1.83 environment steps on average, and the amor-tised decision pause falls from 169 to 148 ms per step even though each harness decision takes longer to prepare. These decision-cost measurements come from a separate 12-game-per-condition profile (Appendix I). C1 includes a possible perception bottleneck and short-cut risk, C2–C3 are learning-signal problems, and C4–C5 are problems of the interface itself. C4 in particular means that even a perfect perceiver would need history or memory to infer ghost heading, speed, and frightened timers; imple-menting that memory in a harness is our design choice. This motivated the next two steps: visual adaptation (Appendix J) and placing memory and candidate generation in a harness so that the model chooses among a few routes instead of hun-dreds of primitive moves (Appendix H). This single-game, mostly single-family study lacks multiple training seeds and system-atic unseen-layout evaluation of the complete screenshot system; held-out ASCII results do not fill that gap. # Q Artifact Scope and Release Status 

The accompanying review package is deliberately narrower than a full training release. It contains sanitized evidence ledgers, CPU-only fixed-map paired-statistics code, recorded gate-output aggregation, and a reference input-substitution core. The paired analysis recomputes 128 seed pairs; the gate census re-aggregates all 431 recorded outputs and its 321-state strict subset. The packaged checks were rerun af-ter extraction. Gate-output aggregation verifies the supplied outputs, not the original gate computation: repeating that computation requires raw states and the frozen gate callback, which are not distributed in this package. The package excludes model weights, private trajectories, credentials, server paths, repository history and third-party game sources. It is not a complete game environment or train-ing reproducer. Historical settings and unavailable original manifests remain marked as such in the relevant appendices. All numerical claims retain the unit and source scope of their panel. Subject to employer and other rights-holder authorization, we plan to release the experimental recipe supporting this case study: task-specific configurations and procedures, re-leasable training/evaluation logic, and analysis scripts for the reported findings. The contribution is the documented failure analysis and condition-specific lessons, rather than a new infrastructure stack. Company-proprietary infrastruc-ture is outside this commitment; its disclosure is governed by company policy. For internal dependencies, the recipe will document their role, interfaces and required settings, with portable alternatives where available. Model weights and third-party sources are not implicitly included. The release will distinguish original configurations from reconstructions and identify unavailable historical inputs rather than promise exact recovery of every run. The maintained companion is https://where-it-breaks-review.vercel.app/; it provides the manuscript and recorded demonstrations. Apache-2.0 is proposed for original recipe code only where the employer and other rights holders au-thorize its release and licensing; third-party dependencies retain their own terms. The current analysis archive is not a complete training release, and local source candidates are not claimed publicly available. Identifying repository links are omitted during review. The anonymous archive’s README identifies runnable analyses, input-dependent reference code and omissions explicitly.
