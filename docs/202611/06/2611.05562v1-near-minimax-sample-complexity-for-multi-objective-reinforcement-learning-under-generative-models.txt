# Abstract 

In multi-objective reinforcement learning, preferences may be specified only after data collection, requiring one dataset to support a continuum of reward trade-offs. We study finite discounted Markov decision processes with unknown transitions, S states, A actions, and m known reward functions taking values in [0 , 1], given access to a generative model. The learner must provide an ε-optimal policy for every convex combination of these rewards without further sampling, with a guarantee that holds simultaneously over all preferences and initial states. Writing H = (1 − γ)−1, we establish the constant-confidence minimax sample complexity ˜Θ

Thus, up to these logarithmic factors, the worst-case statistical cost of supporting all preferences grows logarithmically with the number of objectives and saturates at the number of states. The upper bound uses uniform state–action sampling and empirical planning, with reward rounding in the logarithmic-objective regime. Its analysis chains optimal-value increments over covers of the reward hull. A one-sided Bellman variance bound controls signed value differences under a second transition kernel, preserving the H3ε−2 dependence. A complementary uniform guarantee for all bounded rewards yields the state-dimension saturation. We establish lower bounds matching up to logarithmic factors through a rare-exit construction with subset rewards and action encoding. These bounds apply to adaptive simulator queries and arbitrary randomized history-dependent output policies, while the upper bounds are attained by deterministic stationary policies. 

# 1 Introduction 

Many sequential decision problems involve trade-offs among competing objectives. A robot may balance task completion, energy consumption, and wear, and a language model may be evaluated for both helpfulness and safety. Multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) represents such criteria as separate reward signals and studies how the resulting decisions depend on the user’s preferences [1, 2]. We consider linear scalarization. The learner knows m rewards r1, . . . , r m, which we call the coordinate rewards; together they form the reward dictionary {r1, . . . , r m}. A preference vector w ∈ ∆m assigns them nonnegative weights summing to one and induces the scalar reward rw = ∑mi=1 wiri. The dynamics are shared across preferences, but the optimal policy can change with the weights. Preferences are often specified only after data collection. Different users may weigh the same objectives differently, and a designer may inspect the available trade-offs before fixing a reward. Preference-conditioned value functions [3] and interpolation between models fine-tuned on different rewards [4] address this need for adaptation. The statistical question is what a single dataset can guarantee. For one fixed scalar reward, the dataset must support one near-optimal policy. For a finite list of rewards, it must support a near-optimal policy for each listed reward. Uniform MORL asks for a policy-selection rule that is simultaneously accurate for every convex combination of the coordinate rewards. This requirement covers a continuum of rewards, including preferences for which no policy that is optimal for a single coordinate reward is optimal. arXiv:2611.05562v1 [stat.ML] 3 Nov 2026 Preprint 

We study this uniform target in finite discounted Markov decision processes (MDPs) with unknown transitions and access to a generative model, that is, a simulator that returns a next state for any queried state–action pair. Write S and A for the numbers of states and actions, γ ∈ [0 , 1) for the discount factor, and H = (1 −γ)−1

for the effective horizon. For one fixed bounded reward, the minimax sample complexity is ˜Θ( SAH 3/ε 2) in the standard nontrivial parameter regime [5–7]. The closest discounted MORL analysis gives an ˜O(mSAH 3/ε 2)bound for estimating scalarized Q-functions uniformly over preferences [8]. A direct reduction through a net of preferences also gives linear dependence on m for uniform policy learning. For a finite list of known rewards, recent online episodic results instead depend logarithmically on the list size [9]. These results motivate the following question. 

How many simulator queries are needed to support a near-optimal policy for every reward in the convex hull of a known reward dictionary? 

Our learner collects one dataset and then answers every preference query without further simulator calls. Its guarantee holds on a single high-probability event, simultaneously for all preferences and all initial states (Section 2.3). Specifically, our contributions are summerized as follows.

Supporting every convex combination of the known rewards thus costs only a logarithmic factor in the number of objectives, and this factor never exceeds the number of states. The upper bound combines two procedures. Empirical planning for a rounded reward gives the log( em ) factor (Theorem 4.1). Direct empirical planning for the requested reward gives the factor S, since its guarantee holds for every reward in [0 , 1] SA (Theorem 5.1). The learner uses whichever procedure needs fewer samples. Each procedure samples every state–action pair equally once, and returns deterministic stationary policies without further simulator queries. 

A chaining analysis for uniform policy learning. Nearby preferences have nearby optimal values, so the data needed to serve them overlap; the difficulty is to exploit this overlap without losing accuracy. The upper bound rests on finite covers of the convex reward hull. At resolution η, their logarithmic size is of order η−2 log( SA ) log( em ). A single union bound over the cover at the final resolution ε/H would add a factor of order H2/ε 2. We instead write each optimal value as a sum of increments across successively finer reward approximations, and the variance of each increment shrinks with its resolution. The central technical step is a one-sided Bellman variance bound for signed differences of optimal values, with variances taken under a second transition kernel (Lemmas 4.4 and 4.5). The bound uses only an upper bound on the one-step drift. It therefore applies even though the policy that is optimal for the target reward need not be optimal for its approximants. Increments of empirical optimal values depend on the data used to compute them. An adaptation of the absorbing-state construction of [6] supplies concentration for these increments. The variance bound then turns the row-wise concentration into bounds on policy values, in both directions of the comparison between the true and empirical models. Across levels, the growth of the cover sizes is balanced by the decay of the increment variances, which preserves both the H3 and the ε−2 dependence. 

Lower bounds with bounded rewards and simplex preferences. We construct stationary discounted MDPs in which answering every preference query accurately identifies many unknown exit distributions. Rare exits produce the H3/ε 2 dependence. Subset rewards distinguish distributions over d shared absorbing outcomes using 2 d coordinate rewards, which yields a factor of order log m, capped by S. An additional action code of length O(log( A + 1)) lets a simplex preference single out one pair of actions while keeping every reward in [0 , 1] (Theorem 6.4). The lower bounds allow adaptive simulator queries and randomized history-dependent output policies. For any fixed output policy, its normalized pre-exit action weights do not depend on the unknown exit distributions. This identity lets the learned policy-selection rule serve as a decoder. A scalar construction covers the remaining parameter regimes (Theorem C.1). 

1.1 Related work MORL targets and Pareto geometry. Surveys distinguish MORL problems by their utility functions, policy classes, and the information available about preferences [1, 2]. For linear utilities, a convex coverage set contains an optimal policy for every preference, whereas Pareto-front recovery describes the attainable 2Preprint 

nondominated value vectors. Lu et al. [10] study convexity and the relations among Pareto notions for stationary policies. Li et al. [11] exploit geometric locality to recover the exact Pareto front in a known model. Other learning targets include approachability of a target set in average-reward problems [12] and convergence to Pareto-stationary policies [13]. Our target is uniform scalarized policy accuracy in an unknown model, delivered by a policy-selection rule. Section 2.3 explains how this rule acts as an implicit approximate convex coverage set. 

Preference-conditioned and offline methods. Abels et al. [14] develop deep MORL methods for changing weights, and Yang et al. [3] use an envelope Bellman update to share one value representation across preferences. Zhu et al. [15] learn preference-adaptive policies from offline demonstrations, and Ramé et al. [4] interpolate models fine-tuned on different rewards. These works study representations and algorithms for preference adaptation. We instead quantify the worst-case number of transition samples needed for a simultaneous policy guarantee in a tabular discounted model. 

Generative-model sample complexity. For a single reward, variance-sensitive arguments and empirical planning give sharp sample bounds [5–7]. Zhou et al. [8] extend model-based envelope value iteration to MORL and obtain a bound linear in m for a preference-weighted Q-function error. This value-estimation target differs from the policy loss in the true model studied here. A preference net combined with scalar empirical-planning guarantees yields a uniform policy bound with the same linear dependence on m. Our analysis instead uses multiscale covers of the reward hull to obtain the logarithmic factor. In a related simulator setting, Vaswani et al. [16] characterize the cost of relaxed and strict feasibility in constrained MDPs. Their objective is a constrained scalar optimum, whereas ours is a family of unconstrained scalar rewards. 

Online multi-objective learning. Wu et al. [17] study adversarial preferences and preference-free ex-ploration in finite-horizon MDPs. With Hep denoting the episode length, their exploration upper bound is 

˜O(min {m, S }SAH 3ep /ε 2) episodes. Qiu et al. [18] develop online and preference-free algorithms for Tchebycheff and smooth Tchebycheff scalarizations. Hu and Yu [19] study near-minimax learning under predictable adversarial preferences and preference-free exploration in linear MDPs. These works differ from ours in the interaction protocol, the scalarization, or the model class. We study linear scalarization in a stationary discounted tabular model with direct state–action sampling. 

Finite reward lists and reward-free exploration. Reward-free exploration collects data before any reward is specified and must support arbitrary bounded rewards afterward [20]. Task-agnostic exploration instead considers a finite collection of tasks whose rewards are unavailable during exploration [21]. Later work sharpens these guarantees [22–24]. In particular, Li et al. [23] separate reward-agnostic learning for a finite reward class from the all-rewards requirement. Ridel and Cohen [24] reduce the lower-order term that restricts the accuracy range of the reward-agnostic bound. They also prove a reward-free lower bound of order 

S2AH 3ep /ε 2 for time-inhomogeneous MDPs, which matches the reward-free upper bound up to logarithmic factors. For rewards known before exploration, Chen and Zhang [9] give an online episodic upper bound of 

˜O(SAH 3ep log m/ε 2) episodes for a list of m rewards. Under the parameter conditions of their Corollary 1, they also prove a lower bound of order SAH 3ep min {S, log m}/ε 2. Our upper bound covers the entire convex hull of the given rewards, not only the list. Our lower bounds use a stationary discounted kernel and count individual simulator observations. Episodic bounds count trajectories, so their horizon factors cannot be compared directly with H = (1 − γ)−1. Theorem 5.1 supplies the complementary all-rewards guarantee under our sampling model. 2.2 Which rewards must one dataset support? 

We further distinguish different learning targets, determined by two features: the reward information available during sampling, and the query class, that is, the set of rewards for which the learner must later return a near-optimal policy. Table 2 compares four targets under a common discounted simulator model. 

A fixed reward versus a finite list. If w0 is fixed and known before sampling, optimizing rw0 is an ordinary single-reward learning problem, and the sampling procedure may be tailored to that reward. For the finite list {r1, . . . , r m}, one dataset must support policies ̂π 1, . . . , ̂π m that are simultaneously near-optimal for their respective rewards. This joint guarantee on the listed rewards says nothing about other preferences. 

A finite list versus its convex hull. Uniform MORL also covers every mixed preference, and policies that are optimal for individual coordinate rewards need not suffice. Consider a single-state problem with three actions whose reward vectors ( r1(s, a ), r 2(s, a )) are (1 , 0), (0 , 1), and (0 .6, 0.6). The first two actions are optimal for r1 and r2, respectively. The third is optimal for w = (1 /2, 1/2), and no randomization between the first two attains its value. This distinction concerns the required outputs. By itself, it does not imply a larger minimax sample complexity for the hull than for the finite list. 4Preprint

# 8 Discussion 

The objective dependence is determined by the geometry of the known reward hull. A fine cover used in a single union bound would introduce H2/ε 2 through its entropy. Chaining avoids that loss because finer reward increments have smaller resolved variance. The one-sided Bellman inequality remains valid even though the policy optimal for the target reward need not optimize its approximants. The logarithmic-objective guarantee applies to empirical planning after reward rounding. Direct empirical planning for every bounded reward has the separate state-saturation guarantee. Both statements assume known bounded reward coordinates, simplex preferences, a stationary discounted kernel, and uniform accuracy at every initial state. The result identifies the joint rate up to logarithmic factors; exact logarithmic and confidence dependence remain separate questions.
