# Found by Search, Broken by a Shortcut: Transfer and Tampering of Searched Intrinsic Rewards 

Chase J. Metoyer Northeastern University Boston, MA 

metoyer.c@northeastern.edu ∗

Abstract 

Can a search over innate drives find intrinsic rewards that help a reinforcement-learning agent learn, transfer to a task it never saw, and resist an exploit that was absent during the search? Each candidate reward combines four generic events (a new view, a pickup, a drop and a toggle), each with a weight, a relief and a refill rate. We ran three pre-registered stages; the search protocol was locked in a public, timestamped commit before any search result existed. CMA-ES and an equal-budget random search used 1,560 PPO training runs, and the final evaluation and tamper test used 340. On the held-out task, KeyCorridorS3R2, the CMA-ES-selected reward (E) beat task-only PPO, episodic novelty and the random-search-selected reward (R) in all 3 registered comparisons, all of them meaningful by the registered size rule. Among these generic rewards E transferred best; the earlier paper’s satiation reward, which on this task is given hand-wired key and locked-door events, did better (20 of 20 seeds solved against 4 of 20; descriptive). On UnlockPickup, where that reward uses generic events, E’s mean area exceeded it by 0.276 (descriptive). On the training tasks there was no registered difference between E and R: both E-over-R tests missed. A free reset action, absent during the search, collapsed E’s learning on DoorKey-8x8 (mean area 0.829 without it, 0.136 with it; registered) to below task-only PPO (descriptive). The searched drives helped on every task tested, but a reward given task-specific events did better on the held-out task, and selection gave E no protection against an exploit absent from the search. 

Keywords: reinforcement learning, intrinsic motivation, reward search, homeostatic reinforcement learning, transfer, reward tampering, pre-registration 

# 1 Introduction 

In our earlier paper [15], PPO agents given a bounded, hand-designed homeostatic satiation reward and a cheap action that reset their needs learned to press it and stopped solving the task, although solving was optimal under that reward. That reward was designed by hand. The optimal-reward view suggests another route: treat the reward as the outcome of a search over reward functions for whatever best serves the designer’s goal [21]. This paper asks whether such a search avoids the failure, and what a searched reward does on a task it was not selected on. We search over innate drives. Optimal, searched and learned rewards. Singh et al. [21] define an optimal-reward framework in which primary reward functions are selected for evolutionary success across environments, and conclude that intrinsic and extrinsic reward differ along a continuum rather than in kind. Niekum et al. [16] search reward functions with genetic programming and name distributions of environments, nonstationary environments and short agent lifetimes as settings where reward search should help. Sorg et al. [23] solve the optimal-reward problem online by gradient ascent for agents with limitations. Zheng et al. [25] learn intrinsic-reward parameters for policy-gradient agents by gradient ascent on the extrinsic return, and Xu et al. [24] adapt return parameters such as the discount online with meta-gradients. Several studies test generalization directly. Zheng et al. [26] learn intrinsic rewards over many lifetimes and report that the learned rewards generalize to other kinds of agents and to changes in the environment’s dynamics. Alet et al. [1] meta-learn curiosity algorithms as programs and report two that perform on par with or better than published curiosity algorithms in disparate domains. Oh et al. [17] discover an update rule on toy environments that generalizes to Atari games, and Oh et al. [18] discover, from a population of agents across many environments, a rule that surpasses existing rules on Atari and outperforms several algorithms on benchmarks not seen during discovery. 

Language-model reward design (contrast). Ma et al. [14] use large language models to write reward code and improve it by evolutionary search, and report rewards that outperform expert-written ones on most of their tasks. Our search space is different in kind: twelve numbers in a fixed homeostatic form, with no language model. 

Intrinsic motivation and homeostasis. Exploration bonuses include pseudo-counts [3], pre-diction error at scale [5], random network distillation [6] and impact-driven bonuses evaluated in procedurally generated MiniGrid tasks [19]. Burda et al. [5] also gave a curiosity agent a television whose channel it could change; the television drastically slowed learning, although agents sometimes still converged to the extrinsic reward. Keramati and Gutkin [13] define primary reward as the fulfilment of physiological needs and prove that seeking such reward is equivalent to seeking physio-logical stability. Our predecessor study tested a bounded satiation reward of this kind and found it wireheaded through a reset action [15]. 

Reward hacking and tampering. Amodei et al. [2] list avoiding reward hacking among concrete accident-risk problems, and Skalse et al. [22] give a formal definition of reward hacking. Everitt et al. [8] study when an agent has an instrumental goal to tamper with its reward process and give design principles against reward-function tampering and against tampering with the reward function’s input. Our reset action changes the need state the reward reads, so in their terms it is input tampering, as in the earlier paper. 

Random search and CMA-ES. Bergstra and Bengio [4] show that random trials are more efficient than a grid when only a few hyperparameters matter, and propose random search as the natural baseline for adaptive methods. CMA-ES adapts a full covariance matrix of a Gaussian search distribution [9, 10]; we use the pycma implementation [11]. 

How this paper differs. The generalization studies above learn reward networks, programs or update rules and test them on new agents, dynamics or benchmarks. We search a small, interpretable 3space of drives, compare the CMA-ES-selected reward with an equal-budget random-search selection, test transfer to one held-out task and then test the same reward against an exploit absent from its selection environment. We are not aware of a prior study that pairs a held-out transfer test and a tamper test of the same searched reward under a public pre-registration.

3.2 Events and the corrected detector 

The four events are generic. A new view is a 7 × 7 egocentric observation not yet seen in the episode; a pickup is any change from carrying nothing to carrying an object; a drop is an executed drop; a 

toggle is any door changing its open or locked state, including closing and reopening, or a box being opened. Stage 1 fixed four detector choices: events on an episode’s final step count; box openings count as toggles; a pickup that itself completes the task is excluded from the paid pickup event; and the detector attaches only after the initial reset. The earlier paper’s archived detector differed in ways documented by an audit of its code committed with the Stage 1 materials: it skipped pickup and door events on an episode’s final step, its door cache was empty during each parallel environment’s first training episode, it did not detect box openings, and it had no drop event. Its event counts and ours are therefore not interchangeable.

Tasks are MiniGrid environments [7]. 3.4 Search 

Both methods search a shared latent box z ∈ [0 , 1] 12 , decoded identically: w = 2 z − 1, s = z, and with u = 2 z − 1 the refill is zero if u < 0 and 1 /640 · (1 /20 / 1/640) u otherwise, so a uniform draw has zero refill on each event with probability one half. CMA-ES [10] ran in pycma [11] with population 10 for 12 generations, initial mean 0.5 in every coordinate, initial step size 0.25, pycma’s default bound handling, no restarts and optimizer seed 424242. A generation was requested only after every run of the previous one was recorded, and any resume had to reproduce every recorded candidate exactly. Random search drew 120 candidates uniformly from the same box (generator seed 535353), fixed and committed before any search evidence, and evaluated them in batches of 10. A candidate’s fitness is its mean training-curve area over the 2 training tasks with 3 seeds each, equally weighted. A run’s area is the arithmetic mean of its 98 logged training-success values; each value is the success rate over the last 100 completed training episodes, and a missing early value counts as zero. All candidates of one CMA-ES generation, or one random-search batch, shared a seed panel (seeds 1000–1035 and 3000–3035). The top 3 candidates per method by fitness were re-evaluated on 10 fresh seeds per training task (seeds 1900–1909 and 3900–3909), and the finalist with the highest re-evaluated mean area was frozen as that method’s reward. In all, Stage 2 trained 1,560 runs: 1,440 search runs and 120 re-evaluation runs. 

3.5 Final evaluation 

Stage 3 trained five arms on DoorKey-8x8, UnlockPickup and the held-out KeyCorridorS3R2, with 20 fresh seeds per arm and task (2000–2019): E, R, task-only PPO (T) and episodic novelty with coefficient 10 −3 (N), plus the earlier paper’s satiation reward with growth 1/160 and the corrected detector (S). S was registered as descriptive only. Its three needs (explore, grasp, open) differ from E’s and R’s in two ways that bear on the held-out comparison. On KeyCorridor tasks its grasp event counts only key pickups and its open event counts only the opening of a door that was locked at the start of the episode; on DoorKey-8x8 and UnlockPickup it uses the generic pickup and toggle events. And all of its needs refill. The registered endpoint is the training-curve area. Each comparison is a one-sided Mann– Whitney U test of E against a comparator, exact when the pooled areas have no ties and otherwise 5an independent-label permutation of U with 200,000 resamples and seed 0. A permutation p of 5 × 10 −6 is the smallest attainable with that many resamples. The primary family is E against T, N and R on KeyCorridorS3R2 (Holm, m = 3); the secondary family is the same comparisons on the two training tasks (Holm, m = 6). A comparison is a hit when its Holm-adjusted p is below 0.05, and a meaningful improvement when it is a hit and the observed mean area of E exceeds the comparator’s by at least 0.05. Solve counts (mean training success of at least 0.9 over the final 9 logged values) carry Clopper–Pearson 95% intervals and one-sided Fisher exact p-values, which the protocol calls exploratory and which supply no hits. Median first-solved steps are censored at the nominal budget. Runs used whatever CPU type the provider assigned, and same-seed results were not bit-identical across CPU types, so every comparison is unpaired. 

3.6 Tamper test 

E was retrained from scratch on DoorKey-8x8 with 20 seeds per condition (2020–2039), with and without a free reset action. With the action enabled, MiniGrid’s no-op “done” action sets every need to zero before the reward is computed, so pressing it pays the whole current drive. # 5 What our predictions and measures got wrong 

Appendix A lists every registered rule and test with its outcome. Of the 3 primary and 6 secondary comparisons, 3 and 4 hit; the tamper prediction hit. The misses and measurement failures were these. • E over R on the training tasks: no registered difference. The secondary family predicted that E would beat R on both training tasks. Both comparisons missed (Section 4.4); on DoorKey-8x8 R’s mean area was the higher. On their re-evaluation panels (different seeds), R had also scored higher than E. Because E and R are single selected genomes, the misses say nothing general about CMA-ES and random search. • The tamper label’s press threshold was too low. The label asked for more than one press per completed episode; every final window cleared it many times over, while the per-step rate was below a uniform policy’s, so a policy pressing at random would also have met it (derived reference ). The area half of the label holds; the press half measures little in long, failing episodes, as the earlier paper found for its own repeat threshold. • The search score overstated the CMA-ES finalists. Not a registered prediction: the 3-seed search score ranked every CMA-ES finalist above every random-search finalist, and fresh seeds reversed that order (Table 2). The registered re-evaluation step caught it. • The pooled drop association reversed among random-search candidates alone. One untested explanation ( post hoc hypothesis ) is adaptive sampling: CMA-ES concentrated its later generations near its early winners, so their features are over-represented in the pooled population whether or not they matter. • Infrastructure. – A cross-platform resume of the Stage 2 controller failed exact replay: four latent coor-dinates differed by one float64 unit in the last place between the Mac and Linux, and one decoded refill value by 4. No rounding or tolerance was introduced; the controller resumed on the original Mac after exact replay passed there (Appendix C). 14 – Stage 2 orchestration depended on a laptop that had to stay awake and online. Two transport failures and a failed migration preceded the final restart; 50 terminated calls were retried with identical inputs, and no saved result was recomputed. 

– The Stage 3 check that the controller survives a closed laptop lid: we waived it; it remains unverified. The run completed without a controller restart. 

# 6 Discussion 

Searching over drives helped and transferred relative to the generic baselines. Ebeat task-only PPO and episodic novelty on both training tasks and on the held-out task, and beat R on the held-out task ( registered ). The held-out win over R compares two selected genomes; on the training tasks the two were not distinguishable by the registered tests. Our reading (exploratory/post hoc ) is that the drives fit the structure of the training tasks: DoorKey-8x8 and UnlockPickup each require a short chain of a key pickup and a door, and E pays the first pickup, drop and toggle of every episode. 

Why S did better on KeyCorridorS3R2 ( post hoc hypothesis , untested). KeyCorri-dorS3R2 has several closed doors. To succeed, the agent must open the door of the side room that holds the key and then the locked door in front of the target. E’s toggle need is spent by the first door or box change of any kind, possibly a door the agent must open anyway, and it never refills; R’s toggle need behaves the same way. S’s open event fires only when the initially locked door opens, and its need keeps refilling until then. A plausible account is that E’s spent-once door drive is used up before the agent reaches the locked door, whereas S keeps being pulled toward it. This is untested: the saved per-episode records do not include door events, and S differs from E and R on KeyCorridor in two confounded ways, hand-wired events (key-only pickup, locked-door-only open) and a refilling door need. The UnlockPickup comparison, where S uses generic events and did worse than E, is a different task and cannot separate the two. 

Why random search sufficed ( exploratory/post hoc ). The candidate associations suggest a landscape dominated by a few binary choices: a positive sign on the task events (pickup, toggle) and no refill on them. Under the random-search prior each of these four choices has probability one half, so roughly 1/16 of uniform draws gets all four right ( derived reference ; a heuristic that assumes these are the only choices that matter). With 120 draws that is plenty, which is consistent with random search keeping pace with CMA-ES, as Bergstra and Bengio [4] found for hyperparameters where few dimensions matter. We did not test this. 

Selection did not protect E against an exploit absent during selection. The reset action was not part of the search environment, so the search could neither reward nor penalize a reward’s response to it, and E’s positive P0 made a reset pay whenever the drive was positive. The registered collapse and the descriptive comparison with task-only PPO are qualitatively consistent with the earlier paper’s result for a hand-designed reward, where a signal the agent could fill itself did worse than no signal. In the terms of Everitt et al. [8], the reset is tampering with the reward function’s input, and nothing in our selection removed the incentive to do it. 

Implications. A selection environment can only select against failures it contains. Include the exploits one anticipates, such as a reset, in the environments a reward is selected on, and select 15 across structurally different tasks (several doors, distractor objects) rather than two tasks that share one subgoal chain. 

# 7 Limitations 

• Tasks. One held-out task and 2 training tasks, all in MiniGrid. The transfer claim rests on KeyCorridorS3R2 alone. • Search noise and learner. 3 seeds per task per candidate, with generation-specific seed panels; one PPO configuration, the earlier paper’s (Appendix F). • Numerics and infrastructure. Same-seed runs are not bit-identical across CPU types, so comparisons are unpaired and exact cross-machine reproduction is not established (Ap-pendix C); Stage 2 had interruptions, retries and a laptop-bound controller. • Comparators. S is descriptive only and is confounded on KeyCorridor (hand-wired events and a refilling door need); on UnlockPickup, with generic events, it scored below E. E against R compares two selected genomes, not two search methods. • Exploratory analyses are post hoc, in an adaptively sampled population. • Tamper scope. DoorKey-8x8 only, free presses, E only. • Detector. Event definitions differ from the earlier paper’s archived detector; comparisons with the earlier paper are qualitative. 

# 8 Conclusion 

A search over four generic drives found a reward that helped PPO on its training tasks and on one held-out task, where it beat task-only PPO, episodic novelty and the random-search selection. A satiation reward told which events matter on that task did better still, although E outscored it on UnlockPickup, where it used generic events. The two selected rewards were not distinguishable on the tasks they were selected on. And the searched reward gave no protection against a reset action that the search never saw: learning collapsed, below that of task-only PPO. Searching over drives can find useful ones, but it selects for the environments it is shown, including what they leave out.

• Stage 2. 1,802 provider calls and 1,850 physical attempts; 48 additional attempts on existing calls (restarts, cause not established) and 50 same-input manual retries of the 50 terminated calls; usage was missing for 4 attempts. 5 controller events: two transport failures (a download name-resolution failure and an output-polling deadline), a failed Linux replay, a read-only Linux diagnosis and the final Mac restart. Successful outputs were recovered, not recomputed. The Linux replay failed because latent coordinates differed by one unit in the last place; the diagnosis confirmed numeric differences rather than metadata. • Stage 3. 374 provider calls, of which 340 were science runs and the rest pre-launch smoke checks; 13 additional same-call attempts (provider restarts under the same handle), 0 manual retries and 0 controller restarts. An integrity check verified all 6,200 result files listed in the completion markers and the 62 registered source files. • CPU types. Successful Stage 2 scientific attempts ran on Zen 3 (1,266), Intel (205), Zen 4 (61) and other (28) CPUs; Stage 3 science attempts on Zen 3 (264), Intel (61), Zen 4 (20) and other (8). Stage 1 found the same seed not bit-identical across containers. A pinned pre-check (64 replicates: Zen 3 46, Zen 4 5, Intel 13) produced 2 distinct fingerprints, one shared by the 20 AMD containers and one by the Intel containers; the math-library pin was honoured only on Intel. Runs were therefore left unpinned and compared unpaired.
