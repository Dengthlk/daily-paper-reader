# Aligning Language Models with General Preferences: An Empirical Study of Direct Nash Optimization 

Austin Tsai 1 Saurav Nanda 1 Ganapathy Parthasarathy 1 Bhuvnesh Kumar 1 Parivesh Choudhary 1

## Abstract 

Direct Nash Optimization (DNO) aligns a lan-guage model to a general preference function by approximating the Nash equilibrium of a two-player preference game. Its practical variant, DNO-Prct, iterates on-policy sampling, LLM-based rating of the samples together with a teacher’s gold response, large-margin preference-pair selection and a DPO-style contrastive up-date. We re-implement DNO-Prct independently and study it on two open-weight instruct mod-els, Llama 3.1 8B Instruct and Mistral 7B In-struct v0.1, with GPT-4o or a larger open-weight model of the same family as the annotator. On AlpacaEval 2.0 with GPT-4o as judge, three iter-ations raise the length-controlled (LC) win rate against GPT-4o from 31.7% to 45.3% for Llama and from 6.8% to 18.4% for Mistral, and the tuned 8B model scores above Llama 3.1 70B Instruct on every reported win rate. With same-family open-weight annotators, the runs retain 62% (Llama) and 82% (Mistral) of the LC gain achieved with GPT-4o as annotator, so the rating step does not require a proprietary model. In a separate abla-tion configuration, variants that remove the gold responses, remove the data look-back, or accept only teacher-preferred pairs score 6 to 10 LC points below the full procedure, whereas vary-ing the number of on-policy samples per prompt (1 to 7) moves the LC win rate by at most 2.1 points, and a margin sweep (1 to 5) run in its own pipeline by at most 2.4 points. We also report the cost of the pipeline and where the practical algorithm departs from the theory it approximates. All results are single runs, and GPT-4o is both the teacher and the evaluation judge; we discuss what this implies for the reported gains.

## 1. Introduction 

Pre-trained large language models (LLMs) have advanced rapidly (Radford et al., 2018; 2019; Brown et al., 2020; Rae et al., 2021). Trained only to predict the next token, they are not by themselves aligned with what humans prefer. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023; OpenAI, 2023) bridges this gap by collecting human preferences, training a reward model on them, and optimizing the policy with reinforcement learning. Single-stage contrastive methods such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023) remove the explicit reward model. In their standard formulations, both assume a pointwise (Bradley– Terry) reward and therefore cannot represent intransitive preferences, which Liu et al. (2025) show arise with high probability under a Luce-type preference model; they are also vulnerable to reward hacking (Amodei et al., 2016), and a reward model or preference dataset fixed in advance becomes stale as the policy distribution shifts during training (Ross et al., 2011; Cheng et al., 2024; Azar et al., 2024; Munos et al., 2024). To address these limitations, a line of work optimizes the general preference function directly by finding the Nash equilibrium of a two-player game, solving that game with single-player (self-play) algorithms that exploit the sym-metry of the preference function (Munos et al., 2024; Swamy et al., 2024). For a preference function with

The im-provement is against the previous iterate under P and ρ;it is not a statement that benchmark scores rise at every iteration or that the student surpasses a fixed teacher. The practical procedure below, with finite samples, LLM ratings, margin filtering, look-back and a single selected checkpoint, approximates the exact update and does not inherit these guarantees. 

DNO-Prct: The main obstacle in practice is the expectation in Eq. (1). Rosset et al. (2024) therefore propose DNO-Prct, each iteration of which has three steps: 1. Batched on-policy sampling and rating: K outputs per prompt are sampled from the current policy πt and, to-gether with a gold response ygold from a stronger teacher model, are scored by an annotator; the scores serve as a proxy for the pairwise win-rate ranking under P.2. Preference pair construction: Only pairs whose scores differ by a sufficiently large margin are kept, with the intent that the target σ(∆ ⋆t ), after a rescaling of rt, be close to one so that Eq. (5) reduces approximately to the pure contrastive (DPO) loss (an approximation discussed in Section 6.6); a decaying fraction of pairs from ear-lier iterations is mixed in (data look-back, described in Section 5.1 of Rosset et al. (2024) rather than in their Algorithm 2). 3. Contrastive update: The policy is trained with the DPO objective on the constructed pairs, with πt as the refer-ence policy. DNO-Prct is thus an iterative, on-policy form of DPO with the same contrastive objective; the difference lies in how the preference pairs are constructed (Figure 1 in Section 4.2). We refer readers to Rosset et al. (2024) for the derivation. 

## 3. Related Work 

RLHF and offline contrastive alignment: The reward-model-plus-RL recipe (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022) is a standard route to aligned chat models. Offline contrastive methods such as DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), IPO (Azar et al., 2024) and SimPO (Meng et al., 2024) fit the pol-icy directly to a preference dataset; Zephyr (Tunstall et al., 2023) popularized distilled supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on UltraChat followed by DPO on UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2024). DPO and SimPO inherit the transitivity of a pointwise (Bradley–Terry) reward, which IPO avoids, and DPO in particular is known to exploit response length (Park et al., 2024). Meng et al. (2024) report that Llama-3-8B-Instruct trained with SimPO reaches 44.7% LC on the public AlpacaEval 2.0 leaderboard, above Llama-3-70B-Instruct (34.4%), so a small model scoring above a larger sibling on this metric is not by itself evidence of a better algorithm. 

Iterative, online and self-play alignment: Iterative DPO (Xu et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024), online AI feedback (Guo et al., 2024) and Self-Rewarding LMs (Yuan et al., 2024) regenerate preference data from the current policy each round. Tang et al. (2024) find that the advantage of online over offline alignment is not explained by data coverage or quality alone and point to on-policy sampling as the deciding factor; Lanchantin et al. (2025) find that semi-online training, in which the response genera-tor is synchronized with the policy only every few updates, as in DNO-Prct’s per-iteration sampling, performs on par with fully online training and well above offline methods. SPIN (Chen et al., 2024) treats the SFT targets as always preferred over self-generated responses. Game-theoretic methods seek the Nash equilibrium of the preference game: Nash-MD (Munos et al., 2024), online IPO (Calandriello et al., 2024), SPO (Swamy et al., 2024), SPPO (Wu et al., 3An Empirical Study of Direct Nash Optimization 

2025), INPO (Zhang et al., 2025b), DNO (Rosset et al., 2024) and, concurrently with or after our experiments, MPO (Wang et al., 2025), optimistic online mirror descent (Zhang et al., 2025a) and RSPO (Tang et al., 2026). REBEL (Gao et al., 2024) regresses relative rewards and extends to intran-sitive preferences, and Zhang et al. (2025c) learn general preference models beyond Bradley–Terry for such methods. INPO reaches 42.6% LC on AlpacaEval 2.0 from a Llama-3-8B SFT model with an open-weight pairwise preference model and no proprietary model in the loop. We have not found a published independent re-implementation of DNO-Prct on other base models, nor a public implementation of it. 

Where do the gains of AI feedback come from? Sharma et al. (2024) show that improvements attributed to rein-forcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) are largely explained by using a weaker teacher for SFT than the critic used for feedback, and that SFT directly on GPT-4 com-pletions outperforms the pipelines they study. Our gold-response ablation (Section 6.3) is related but not a repli-cation of their comparison: we did not run a matched-teacher SFT control. Ivison et al. (2024) disentangle pref-erence data, learning algorithm, reward model and pol-icy prompts in a comparable empirical study of DPO and PPO. Open-weight models trained specifically as evaluators can match or exceed GPT-4 on reward-model benchmarks (Wang et al., 2024); our open-weight annotators were not evaluator-trained and retained 62–82% of the LC gain of the GPT-4o-annotated runs against GPT-4o (Section 5.4). 

LLM-as-a-judge evaluation and its failure modes: We evaluate with AlpacaEval 2.0 (Li et al., 2023) and its length-controlled win rate (Dubois et al., 2024), which with the default judge and reference correlates strongly with the rankings of Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024); our judge and references are not the default (Section 4.1). LLM judges exhibit position, verbosity and self-preference biases (Zheng et al., 2023; Panickssery et al., 2024; Wataoka et al., 2024). Li et al. (2026) find that a judge favors students trained on data from a related model: their average preference-leakage score is about 24% when judge and data generator are the same model, as GPT-4o is in our main runs, and about 3–9% when they share a model family. Zheng et al. (2025) show that a null model that always returns the same, adversarially crafted response reaches 86.5% LC on AlpacaEval 2.0. Both appeared during or after our experiments, and Section 8 uses them to interpret our results. 

3.1. Relation to Online DPO 

DNO is conceptually close to online DPO (Guo et al., 2024). In each round of online DPO the model generates two can-didate responses to a prompt, an annotator decides which is better, and the resulting pair is used for a DPO-style update. DNO likewise samples candidates, rates them and learns from the resulting preferences, but differs in three ways. 

(1) Rating guideline and margin: DNO rates responses against an explicit rubric and forms a pair only when the positive–negative rating margin is sufficiently large. The margin filter is what allows the regression objective of Eq. (5) to be approximated by the pure contrastive loss without an excessively large KL coefficient (Rosset et al., 2024, Section 4); it is an approximation device rather than a condition for Nash convergence. Its empirical effect is studied in Section 6.2. 

(2) Multiple on-policy candidates: Instead of a single pair per prompt, DNO samples K candidates per prompt, intended to cover more of the policy’s output distribution than a single pair. Section 6.1 varies K.

(3) Teacher responses as candidates: DNO adds the teacher’s response ygold to the candidate set. Rating teacher and student responses together captures cases where the student outperforms the teacher, which SPIN-style self-play ignores (Sections 6.3 and 6.4). Online DPO as instantiated by Guo et al. (2024) additionally keeps a fixed reference policy and samples pairs per mini-batch, whereas DNO-Prct collects a large batch per iteration, resets the reference to πt, and mixes in look-back data. 

## 4. Experimental Setup 

4.1. General Setup Timeline: All experiments were run between the release of Llama 3.1 and Mistral Large 2 in late July 2024 and the end of December 2024, with the model and API versions available then. All reported results come from these 2024 runs; a few descriptive statistics, such as the prompt overlap and the truncation rate, were computed afterwards from their stored outputs. 

Dataset: We use the UltraFeedback dataset (Cui et al., 2024; OpenBMB, 2023) as the fine-tuning and preference-alignment dataset. UltraFeedback comprises 63,967 prompts, four responses per prompt from a pool of sev-enteen models (including GPT-4, GPT-3.5 Turbo, Bard, Llama-2-70B-chat, Vicuna-33B and Falcon-40B-Instruct), and GPT-4 annotations along four aspects (instruction fol-lowing, truthfulness, honesty and helpfulness), each on a 1–5 scale, together with an overall score on a 1–10 scale. The prompts are sourced from UltraChat, ShareGPT, Evol-Instruct, TruthfulQA, FalseQA and FLAN. The SFT and offline-DPO thresholds below are applied to the average of the four per-aspect ratings. In addition to the original content, we generate one response from GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024) for every prompt to serve as the gold response ygold 

in the GPT-4o-annotated runs. 4An Empirical Study of Direct Nash Optimization 

Base models and annotators: We experiment with two base models, Llama 3.1 8B Instruct and Mistral 7B In-struct v0.1. The base model provides the initial weights for every run except the two-stage runs of Section 5.5 and the look-back ablation of Section 6.4, which start from an SFT checkpoint. GPT-4o is the annotator in the DNO exper-iments except those of Section 5.4, where the annotator is Llama 3.1 70B Instruct or Mistral Large 2 (Mistral-Large-Instruct-2407, 123B parameters; hereafter Mistral Large; Mistral AI, 2024). 

Baselines: We compare DNO with our own implementa-tions of supervised fine-tuning (SFT), offline DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), a SPIN-style baseline (Chen et al., 2024), DNO-Restrictive, SPPO (Wu et al., 2025) and online DPO (Guo et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024). Our SPIN-style baseline marks the teacher’s gold response as always preferred over the on-policy samples, which is the “mark the teacher ( T )always positive” rule of Section 6.4; it is not the original SPIN recipe, which uses the SFT targets as winners. DNO-Restrictive accepts only pairs whose positive is the teacher’s response. SPPO and online DPO were run on UltraFeed-back with only the learning-rate search described below; both ended below the base model, and we therefore draw no conclusions about those methods from our runs. 

Pre-processing per algorithm: For SFT, we keep as tar-gets the UltraFeedback responses whose average rating is five, the maximum, forming a question-answering dataset in OpenAI’s conversation format with the “user” role for the prompt and the “assistant” role for the response. For offline DPO, we mark responses with an average rating of five as positive and responses rated at least two points lower as negative, and collect all positive–negative pairs per prompt; the prompt takes the “user” role followed by a generation token, and each chosen or rejected response is followed by an end-of-sequence or end-of-turn token. DNO uses only the prompts and the gold responses; on-policy responses are generated on the fly. We split the prompts into three disjoint partitions, one per DNO iteration, so that no prompt is used for new candidate generation in more than one itera-tion; pairs built in earlier iterations may be replayed through look-back (Section 4.2). 

Training setup: We follow most hyperparameters of the Llama recipes (Meta AI, 2024). The batch size is 2 per device with 4 gradient-accumulation steps on four NVIDIA A100-SXM 80 GB GPUs, an effective batch size of 32 for all experiments, and training uses low-rank adaptation (LoRA; Hu et al., 2022) (rank 16, α = 32 , dropout 0.05; Appendix B). We train SFT for 20 epochs (reporting the epoch-3 and epoch-10 checkpoints), DPO for 3 epochs, and DNO for three iterations, each a single epoch over that iter-ation’s training pairs, to limit overfitting to them. Each iter-ation consists of on-policy generation, rating, large-margin pair selection and the contrastive update with the DPO loss (β = 0 .1). It starts from the merged checkpoint of the pre-vious iteration, and the reference policy is that checkpoint with the adapters disabled, i.e., πt, as DNO-Prct prescribes. All algorithms are implemented with the Hugging Face TRL library (von Werra et al., 2020). All runs use seed 2024. 

Learning rate: We search over {5 × 10 −5, 5 × 10 −6}

during the first epoch (first iteration for DNO) of every experiment and keep the better rate for the remaining epochs or iterations; the rate is 5 × 10 −5 for SFT and 5 × 10 −6 for DPO and most DNO experiments. The two rates differed by 5 to 10 points in win rate against GPT-4-0613, so in our runs the choice of learning rate had a large effect. 

Evaluation: One objective of this study is to see how far a small model can be pushed relative to a larger model of its family, so we evaluate all models, including the larger ones, under one pipeline: AlpacaEval 2.0 (v0.6.5; Li et al., 2023) on its 805 prompts, reporting the raw and the length-controlled (LC) win rate (Dubois et al., 2024). The protocol departs from the public leaderboard in three ways. (i) Opponents: instead of gpt4 1106 preview ,every model is compared with two opponents, GPT-4o and GPT-4-0613, whose reference outputs we generated through Azure OpenAI (temperature 0, at most 4,096 to-kens). (ii) Judge: the judge is GPT-4o, prompted with AlpacaEval’s alpaca eval gpt4 pairwise-ranking tem-plate at temperature 0, rather than the leaderboard’s default GPT-4 Turbo annotator; GPT-4o is therefore also the teacher and one of the opponents (Section 8). (iii) Decoding: each evaluated model answers with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023), its chat template, temperature 0.001 and at most 4,096 new tokens. AlpacaEval computes the length-controlled win rate relative to whichever reference outputs it is given, so LC values are defined for both opponents, but neither LC nor raw values are comparable to the public leaderboard or to numbers in other papers, and the reported correlation of LC with Chatbot Arena (Spearman 0.98; Dubois et al., 2024) was established for the default judge and reference, not for this protocol. 

Uncertainty: The standard error of the raw win rate re-ported by AlpacaEval is at most 1.9 points per evaluation (1.76 for the Llama Iteration-3 model against GPT-4o). Its standard error for the LC win rate (at most 0.26) is an error of the regression fit, not a sampling error, so we do not use it to compare models. These are evaluation uncertainties for single models; a comparison between two models on the shared prompts would need a paired analysis, which we have not done, and where we relate a difference to standard errors we treat the two evaluations as independent. The variation between training runs is unknown, because each configura-tion was trained once. We therefore read differences of a few points between reported runs as suggestive rather than 5An Empirical Study of Direct Nash Optimization 

established, and we do not claim that two configurations are equivalent when their scores are close.

On-policy response generation: At the start of each iter-ation we sample K = 5 responses per prompt (varied in Section 6.1) from the current model, the initial checkpoint in the first iteration and the model from the previous itera-tion thereafter, with temperature 0.7 and nucleus sampling (top-p, p = 0 .95 ; Holtzman et al., 2020).

(2024), with an additive rubric of six criteria whose integer scores range from 0 to 6 (Appendix A). Our prompt modifies the original, which was written for GPT-family annotators, so that it also works with Mistral Large and Llama 3.1 70B Instruct. Each response is evaluated along dimensions such as relevance, correctness and conciseness. For each prompt all candidates are sent to the annotator in one query, in a fixed order with ygold last, and scored side by side; the annotator returns one score per candidate. Can-didates containing certain degenerate character sequences are dropped before rating, and a reply that cannot be parsed or does not contain one score per candidate is discarded. 

Preference pair construction: We select pairs (x, y +, y −)

whose rating margin rating( y+)−rating( y−) is sufficiently large. We mark all responses with a score of at least five as positives. For each positive, responses rated at least two points lower are negatives, and we form all positive– negative pairs. If the positive is from the student and the negative is the teacher’s response, we relax the margin to one point, because GPT-4o tends to rate its own responses highly (Panickssery et al., 2024; Wataoka et al., 2024); whether such a gap reflects judge bias or a real quality difference cannot be determined from the scores alone (Chen et al., 2025). This is a deviation from Rosset et al. (2024), who relax the margin whenever the positive is from the student, including S ≻ S pairs, on the grounds that their teacher’s responses rarely scored below five. Following the original paper, we include part of the data from past iterations to preserve earlier preference behavior ( data look-back ): at iteration t we sample at most 30% of the training pairs of iteration t − 1, 15% of those of iteration t − 2, and so on. These pairs are reused as they are, without re-rating. Appendix C gives the procedure as pseudo-code.

5.5. Two-Stage Training: SFT Followed by DNO 

To compare with the conventional two-stage recipe (Ouyang et al., 2022; Tunstall et al., 2023), we first fine-tune each base model with SFT and then apply DNO on UltraFeed-back, with the pre-processing of Section 4.1; DNO then starts from the SFT checkpoint. The right-hand blocks of Tables 3 and 4 compare two-stage training with direct DNO on the instruct model. For Llama the two recipes end close to each other in LC win rate (44.92% vs. 45.32% against GPT-4o; 68.96% vs. 70.18% against GPT-4-0613), while direct DNO keeps a lead in raw win rate (3.1 and 5.3 points). These comparisons use the canonical run of Table 1; the two-stage runs were trained in the configuration of the ablation reference of Table 7 (43.90% and 69.46%), against which two-stage training is 1.0 point higher on LC win rate against GPT-4o and 0.5 points lower against GPT-4-0613. Two-stage responses are shorter than direct DNO’s in the first two iterations (2,092 and 2,285 vs. 3,281 and 3,626 characters) but longer at It-eration 3 (4,342 vs. 3,786); in both recipes the LC win rate peaks at the third iteration. For Mistral the conclusion does not transfer: two-stage training has lower point estimates on all four metrics (LC 12.58% vs. 18.44% against GPT-4o and 30.56% vs. 38.20% against GPT-4-0613; raw 13.29% vs. 18.53% and 37.27% vs. 37.85%), and its responses are about 73% shorter at Iteration 3 (2,030 vs. 7,506 characters). The SFT stage is a plausible cause: the epoch-3 SFT check-point lowers Mistral’s LC win rate against GPT-4-0613 by 3.6 points (14.76% vs. 18.40% in Table 2; by 1.4 points against GPT-4o). We have not tested this explanation with a different SFT initialization.

All ablations use Llama 3.1 8B Instruct with GPT-4o as annotator and three iterations, in configurations separate from the canonical runs, and ablation runs are compared with runs outside their own table only where stated. The full-DNO reference row of Tables 5 and 7 (43.90% LC against GPT-4o, 5,324 characters) is a different run from the canonical run of Table 1 (45.32%, 3,786 characters). The margin sweep of Table 6 was run with its own pair-construction and training scripts and at most 2,000 training steps per iteration. Within each sweep, the hyperparameters other than the swept one are fixed, but the number and mix of selected pairs, and hence of updates, change with it, so a sweep does not isolate one factor.

6.2. Rating Margin 

A large calibrated reward gap motivates the contrastive ap-proximation to Eq. (5); a large rubric-score gap is only a proxy for that condition (Section 6.6), but a margin that is too large discards most pairs and shifts the data mix toward teacher positives. Table 6 evaluates margins of one to five with all other hyperparameters fixed; the number and mix of selected pairs, and hence of updates, change with the margin. The LC win rate against GPT-4o lies between 36.60% and 38.99% for all five margins, with the highest value at margin three; over this range the LC win rate changes by only 2.4 points, and the main experiments keep the default margin of two from Rosset et al. (2024). Rosset et al. (2024, Sec-tion 5.2) compared margin filtering with training on all pairs and found filtering beneficial; our sweep varies the margin but has no unfiltered run. Three observations follow from the pair statistics. First, the share of pairs whose positive is the teacher’s response is about 30% at margins one and two and 35–38% at margins three to five; it is not monotonic in the margin. Second, the average rating of positives stays at 5.8–6.0 while that of negatives falls from 3.70 to 0.92, so large margins select very poor negatives. We did not measure whether such pairs are less informative per sample. Under the DPO loss the per-pair gradient weight is σ(−z),where z is the model’s implicit log-ratio margin scaled by 

β; because the reference policy is the starting checkpoint of each iteration (Section 4.1), z = 0 for every pair at the first update with the default adapter initialization, regardless of the rubric gap, so the rubric gap does not by itself change the initial gradient weight. Third, the average response length decreases with the margin, which may partly reflect the larger share of the teacher’s typically shorter responses at margins three to five, although the share does not track length at every step. The margin also sets the data volume, from 148,764 reported selected pairs at margin one to 1,815 at margin five. 

Comparability with the other ablations: All five margin runs score below the full-DNO reference of Table 7 (43.90% LC against GPT-4o), including the margin-2 run (37.72%), which uses the same margin. Because the sweep used a separate pipeline (Section 6), this gap is confounded with implementation differences that single runs cannot separate from run-to-run variation, so we compare the margin runs only with each other. 

6.3. Gold Responses 

We examine the role of ygold . In addition to preference signals, DNO includes the teacher’s response as one of the candidates; in practice, gold responses may be unavailable because they cost more than preference labels alone. Re-moving them (row “Remove ygold ” of Table 7) lowers the LC win rate against GPT-4o from 43.90% to 36.97% and against GPT-4-0613 from 69.46% to 55.36%, while the raw win rates change by −0.9 and +1.9 points. The drop is con-sistent with the teacher’s responses being rated well above the student’s in the first iteration (Table 8). Without ygold ,DNO becomes a bootstrapped online DPO with more than two candidates per prompt. Its LC win rate against GPT-4o (36.97%) is close to that of our offline DPO baseline (37.02% in Table 1); the two runs use different data and training budgets, so the proximity is not a controlled com-parison. Two controls that we did not run would separate the questions this raises: (i) offline DPO under the abla-tion training budget, to test whether the budget explains the proximity; and (ii) offline DPO trained from the same initial-ization on the Iteration-1 DNO pairs, with matched update budget, pair rule, gold inclusion and prompt coverage, to test whether iterative on-policy resampling adds anything to a single round of teacher-rated data. With preference signals alone, the variant without ygold still scores 5.3 LC points above the base model (31.71%).

Within these caveats, the results support letting a student response serve as the positive when the annotator rates it above the teacher’s, in-stead of always preferring the teacher as the SPIN-style rule does (Section 6.4). 

6.6. Sources of Approximation Error 

The experiments above expose several places where DNO-Prct departs from the theory of Section 2. 

The large-margin approximation: Since rt(x, y ) ∈ [0 , 1] ,the target weight σ(rt(x, y +)−rt(x, y −)) is at most σ(1) ≈

0.73 and cannot approach one without rescaling. Rosset et al. (2024) therefore rescale rt → c r t and η → c η =: ˜ η,which leaves the soft-policy-iteration target unchanged, and note that the appropriate ˜η, the coefficient of the log-ratio inside the contrastive loss (DPO’s β), is thus expected to be larger than η; because a larger ˜η worsens the sample complexity of their Theorem 1, only large-margin pairs are retained so that ˜η need not be too large. Two points follow. First, although Rosset et al. (2024) call ˜η a learning rate, it is the coefficient of the log-ratio, not the optimizer’s learning rate; our learning-rate search does not test this scaling argument, and our runs use β = 0 .1 (Appendix B). A sweep over β at a fixed margin is the experiment that would test it. Second, a large gap on an ordinal 0–6 rating scale is not a calibrated large gap in the theoretical reward 

rt, so the margin filter is a modeling approximation whose validity we have not measured; in the margin sweep above, margins of one to five changed the LC win rate by at most 2.4 points at this step budget. 

Rating noise: Since we use an LLM to grade responses in a batched setting, following Rosset et al. (2024), the rat-ings depend on the annotator’s instruction following (Zheng et al., 2023). Presenting all candidates in one query relies on the annotator to score each response separately and consis-tently: in GPT-4o’s rating replies we observed incomplete ratings, where fewer scores are returned than candidates, and inconsistencies in the indexing of the scores; Mistral Large was less consistent still (Section 5.4). Such replies are discarded, so the affected prompts contribute no pairs. The candidates are presented in a fixed order with ygold last, so any position bias of the annotator (Zheng et al., 2023) falls systematically on the gold response. The ratings have no explicit length control, and the student’s responses are longer than the teacher’s from the first iteration for Llama and from the second for Mistral (Table 8). 

Finite on-policy samples: The on-policy sample (Step 1 of DNO-Prct) is drawn once from πt at the start of the iteration, so it becomes increasingly off-policy as the update proceeds, and five samples per prompt cover only a small 12 An Empirical Study of Direct Nash Optimization 

part of the policy’s output distribution; we did not measure their diversity. 

## 7. Costs of DNO 

We report the cost of a three-iteration DNO run on Ultra-Feedback in terms of GPT-4o API calls and wall-clock time. By construction the pipeline makes about 2n API calls for n prompts: n calls to generate ygold , and, because the three prompt partitions are disjoint and each prompt’s six responses are rated in one batched query, a further n

rating calls over the three iterations, before any retries. For the 63,967 UltraFeedback prompts this estimate is roughly 128,000 calls; the count recorded in 2024, approximately 126,000, is slightly lower. Failed calls are retried up to three times. Each iteration uses one third of the prompts, for which we generate five on-policy responses each with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023); generation takes about three to four hours on four A100 GPUs. Rating all responses takes five to six hours of API time, which multithreading reduces to about two hours. Training takes six to eight hours on the same GPUs, depending on the number of large-margin pairs. Summed over three iterations with multithreaded rating, the listed stages take 33 to 42 hours; the end-to-end time recorded in 2024 was 35 to 45 hours. 

## 8. Limitations 

The teacher is also the evaluation judge and an opponent: 

GPT-4o generates ygold , rates every candidate, is one of the two AlpacaEval opponents and is the AlpacaEval judge. Between 23% and 67% of the selected pairs in each itera-tion have a GPT-4o response as the positive (Table 8), the GPT-4o response is always the last candidate in the rating query, and LLM judges prefer outputs that resemble their own (Panickssery et al., 2024; Wataoka et al., 2024). Li et al. (2026) measure an average preference-leakage score of about 24% when judge and data generator are the same model, as in our main runs, and about 3–9% when they share a model family. Those magnitudes come from other settings and are not a correction for ours, but they show that part of the measured gain may be GPT-4o-likeness as scored by GPT-4o rather than better responses. The open-weight-annotator runs of Section 5.4 are a partial control only for the rating step, since the judge is unchanged: with Llama 3.1 70B Instruct as annotator the Llama student gains 8.4 LC points against GPT-4o, versus 13.6 with GPT-4o as teacher, and the difference cannot be separated into annotator quality and judge affinity. The comparison with the 70B model (Section 5.3) is therefore a benchmark comparison under one protocol, not a ranking. A judge outside the GPT family, an independent benchmark, matched-length analysis, and a capability-regression check on standard benchmarks are the most important additions. 

Non-standard evaluation and single runs: Our evaluation protocol departs from the public leaderboard (Section 4.1), so our numbers are not comparable to those in other papers, and the agreement of LC win rates with human rankings has not been established for it. Every cell is a single run, and between-run variability is unknown. A verbatim search finds 13 of the 805 AlpacaEval instructions (10 of them at least 40 characters long) in the processed UltraFeedback data; because a match may fall inside a response, about 1.6% is an upper bound on exact prompt overlap, and near-duplicates were not searched. Zheng et al. (2025) show that a constant, adversarially crafted output reaches 86.5% LC on AlpacaEval 2.0; this does not show that our check-points exploit the benchmark, but high absolute LC values, including our 70.18% against GPT-4-0613, are evidence of preference under this judge rather than of quality in general. 

Hyperparameter selection: The learning rate was chosen after the first epoch or iteration of each experiment. If the selection criterion was the evaluation we report, the selection is optimistic; selecting on a separate split, with the reported evaluation kept out of the selection, would remove this optimism. 

Human preferences: No human-preference study is re-ported. Both the training signal and the evaluation are LLM judgments used as proxies for human preference, and the handling of intransitive preferences, the motivation for general-preference methods, was not tested. 

Baselines: SPIN is approximated by the teacher-always-positive rule rather than by the original recipe; SFT on UltraFeedback is a weak baseline for an instruct model; our SPPO and online DPO runs received only the learning-rate search and ended below the base model, so they say nothing about those methods; and no SimPO baseline was run. 

Response length: In the direct GPT-4o-annotated runs, response length grows with iterations, to 3,786 characters for Llama and 7,506 for Mistral (Section 5), and the rating step has no length control. We did not measure truncation for the Mistral runs or validate the length-control regression for these unusually long responses. Length growth alone does not demonstrate reward hacking, but it is the pattern reward hacking would produce; win rates at matched length would make the claim of more informative responses testable. 

A 2024 snapshot: The experiments were run in 2024 with the models and APIs of that time. GPT-4o is no longer a frontier judge, Mistral 7B Instruct v0.1 is superseded, and neither concurrent Nash-style methods such as INPO (Zhang et al., 2025b) nor evaluator-trained open-weight judges (Wang et al., 2024) were compared against. We report the study because the procedural description and the 13 An Empirical Study of Direct Nash Optimization 

design observations do not depend on the models being current; nothing has been re-run on newer models. 

Algorithmic approximations: Section 6.6 lists where DNO-Prct departs from the theory it approximates; in addi-tion, none of our experiments evaluates the uniform mixture of iterates for which the Nash guarantee holds. 

## 9. Conclusion 

We re-implemented Direct Nash Optimization from its pub-lished description and studied it on two open-weight instruct models with proprietary and open-weight annotators. Un-der our evaluation protocol, three DNO iterations raise the length-controlled AlpacaEval 2.0 win rate against GPT-4o from 31.71% to 45.32% for Llama 3.1 8B Instruct and from 6.77% to 18.44% for Mistral 7B Instruct v0.1, and the tuned 8B model scores above Llama 3.1 70B Instruct on all four reported win rates. The rating step does not require a pro-prietary model: same-family open-weight annotators retain 62% and 82% of the LC gain of the GPT-4o-annotated runs against GPT-4o, and against GPT-4-0613 the Llama run retains 45% and the Mistral run matches the GPT-4o-annotated run. The ablations indicate which components matter: removing the gold responses or the look-back, or accepting only teacher-preferred pairs, scores 6 to 10 LC points below the full procedure, whereas the number of on-policy samples moves the LC win rate by at most 2.1 points and, in a separate sweep, the rating margin by at most 2.4 points. The defaults we would therefore keep are the teacher’s gold responses, look-back, and rating teacher and student responses together so that a student response can be the positive, with response length measured explicitly. These findings rest on single runs evaluated by a GPT-4o judge that is also the teacher; an independent judge, repeated runs and the matched controls of Sections 6.3 and 6.6 are the natural next steps.

The rubric is additive over six criteria, one point each, so scores range from 0 to 6, and the annotator is asked to return only a Python dictionary with one score per candidate. Our prompt follows Rosset et al. (2024) with modifications so that it transfers to annotators outside the GPT family. 

Review the user’s question and the corresponding response using the additive 6-point scoring system described below. Points are accumulated based on the satisfaction of each criterion: 1. Give 1 point if the response is formatted as an answer, e.g. does not repeat itself over and over, is not a continuation of the question or other random question/answer pairs or examples, etc. 2. Add 1 more point if the response is relevant and provides some information related to the user’s inquiry, even if it is incomplete or contains some irrelevant content. 3. Add a third point if the response addresses a substantial portion of the user’s question without rambling, but does not completely resolve the query or provide a direct answer. 4. Award a fourth point if the response answers nearly all the elements of the user’s question correctly, in a useful and direct way, and is largely devoid of errors, extraneous content, or excess wordiness. 5. Grant a fifth point if the response is entirely correct (without any errors or made-up facts), and directly answers all aspects of the user’s question in a helpful, well-organized, focused, and succinct style. 6. Bestow a sixth point for a response that is entirely correct *AND* concise, impeccably tailored to the user’s question, without any unnecessary information or wordiness, reflecting expert knowledge, amounting to a superbly high-quality, focused, and insightful answer. ### User: {instructions} ### Candidate Responses: {candidate_answers} ### Instructions: After examining the user’s instruction and the response: 1.
