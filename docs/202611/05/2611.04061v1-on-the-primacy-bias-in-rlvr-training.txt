## Abstract 

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) has emerged as a promis-ing approach for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models. However, it still remains unclear whether RLVR can extend reasoning capabilities beyond what is already encoded in the base model. In this paper, we study this question through the lens of primacy bias — the tendency of models to over-rely on already-learned representations, disproportionately improving performance on problems they already handle well while underexploring harder ones that require de-parting from those representations. Through our controlled experiments and large-scale analysis, we show that RLVR training on mixed data tends to further sharpen performance on easier problems while yielding only marginal gains on harder ones. We find that although hard problems lead to modest gains overall, they are actually the ones that help most in expanding the model’s reasoning capabilities and pushing the representations. However, prolonged training on these hard problems may also hurt and lead to forgetting on easy problems. These findings help to better understand some of the prior conflicting observations about RLVR, specifically, why on (OOD) domains where the base model performs poorly, improvements on hard problems can expand reasoning; while on well-trained domains, forgetting can reduce performance and mask genuine gains obtained from hard problems. ## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) fine-tuned for reasoning have recently achieved remarkable gains on complex reasoning benchmarks. A key driver is Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) [10, 20], which iteratively optimizes LLMs using simple, verifiable rewards, thereby incentivizing complex reasoning behaviors such as backtracking and self-verification within the reasoning traces of these models. Despite this progress, the answer to the question “ does RLVR expand toward new reasoning capa-bilities? ” still remains under debate. Some studies have suggested that RL fine-tuning only improves sample efficiency on problems already solvable within the base model, rather than enabling it to solve new problems [41, 23, 36]. On the other hand, some other works [31, 22, 35, 40] have provided evi-dence that RL-trained models can still induce novel reasoning strategies on out-of-distribution (OOD) domains. These works, however, suggest that the relationship between the base model and the evalu-ated task—specifically the extent to which the base model can already solve the task problems—may play an important role in explaining whether RLVR leads to reasoning expansion or sharpening.

In this paper, we seek a principled explanation for these seemingly contradictory outcomes through the lens of primacy bias —the tendency to overfit to initial experiences (i.e., the pre-trained knowl-edge) that can damage the rest of the learning process [17, 2]. This bias not only limits exploration but also impairs the learnability of on-policy data during RL training. It arises because the model be-comes increasingly rigid and remains confined near its pre-trained initialization, rather than allowing meaningful adaptation of its internal representations towards new downstream tasks—a behavior com-monly referred to as the lazy training regime [24, 8, 24]. To study this hypothesis, we first investigate primacy bias in RLVR using experiments that fully control both pre-training and post-training pipeline (Section 4). We compare coverage dynamics during RL fine-tuning induced by pre-training against random initialization. Our results show that primacy bias leads to coverage shrinkage, regardless of the fine-tuning distribution. Furthermore, we observe that this primacy bias induces sparser parameter updates during training which hinders the network’s ability to learn rare or more challenging problems (i.e., those that the pre-trained or base model struggles to handle). These observations suggest that the RLVR model mostly operates in the lazy regime [8, 15], staying close to its pre-trained initialization and requiring prolonged training to meaningfully adapt to harder examples [27, 18, 24]. We then extend our analysis to LLM reasoning and investigate the existence of lazy training under RLVR ( Section 5). Our results show that RLVR-trained models could often be well approximated by the linearization around their pre-trained base model, while exhibiting minimal feature drift. This result suggests that RLVR in this more general setting also operates in the lazy regime, providing a possible explanation for the sparse parameter updates observed during RLVR training, as reported in [26]. We believe that these findings place RLVR within a theoretically tractable regime, which can provide a foundation for more principled, scalable, and analyzable studies of learning dynamics in reasoning post-training [27, 18, 6]. Motivated by our findings, we identify an unlearnable phenomenon that emerges from the interaction between primacy bias and the lazy training regime in RLVR-based LLM training ( Section 6). To study this, we first perform fine-tuning across a mixture of easy and hard reasoning problems and observe that mixed training disproportionately improves rewards on easy examples while limiting learning on harder ones. Our results show that although hard problems lead to only modest overall gains during training, they are actually the ones that help most in expanding the model’s reasoning capabilities. At the same time, focused training on hard problems introduces a subtle trade-off that must be carefully taken into account. We find that prolonged training on these difficult examples can also hurt and lead to catastrophic forgetting on the easier problems, likely due to substantial deviations from the initial base model representations. This explains why, as observed in [22], prolonged RLVR training can expand the reasoning boundary in less familiar or out-of-distribution domains, where the pre-trained model initially struggles. In such settings, extended training enables the model to gradually escape the lazy regime and meaningfully adapt to the new target tasks [18, 35, 40], ultimately leading to broader reasoning coverage. In contrast, in domains where the pre-trained model already performs well—such as mathematical or code reasoning, particularly on easier problems—prolonged training may provide limited benefit and can even lead to adverse effects such as coverage shrinkage or catastrophic forgetting. In these cases, the gains obtained from adapting to harder examples may be outweighed by degradation on the easier ones due to substantial deviations from the base model representations and the effects of catastrophic forgetting. We hope that our study provides a clearer understanding of the conflicting observations surrounding RLVR post-training and motivates more principled approaches for studying and controlling the trade-off between adaptation and forgetting. In summary, our main contributions are as follows: • We unveil the primacy bias and lazy learning as the underlying mechanism to reconcile conflicting findings of whether RLVR expands or sharpens the reasoning boundary, where models tend to rely on base model’s features when adapting to post-training data, limiting their ability to acquire new capabilities during RL fine-tuning. • Through our controlled and large-scale empirical analysis, we demonstrate that RLVR operates in a near-linearized regime around the base model, with the learned representation remaining largely un-changed throughout the RL fine-tuning process. As a result, this leads models to disproportionately improve on easy problems while limiting their ability to effectively learn on hard ones. • Despite limited overall improvement, learning on hard problems can expand the reasoning boundary, but comes at the cost of forgetting on easy problems. This explains why, on OOD tasks, 2where forgetting is minimal, improvements on unsolved problems can increase Pass@ k, whereas on over-trained domains, severe forgetting masks out improvement on hard problems. 

## 2 Related Works 

RL Generalization of Reasoning LMs. The role of reinforcement learning in driving generalization beyond the base model has been a central topic of study. Early works argue that RL-trained models may not acquire fundamentally new reasoning capabilities beyond those already present in their base models [39, 23, 36]. In particular, [41] demonstrates that RL training can even reduce the number of solvable problems, as measured by the Pass@ k metric, suggesting that performance is largely constrained by the base model’s capacity. Additionally, [36] proposes a theoretical framework indicating that RL is unlikely to surpass the inherent limitations of the base model. By contrast, [22] shows that extended RL training can enhance coverage on synthesized reasoning tasks. [35] further shows that RL can exhibit grokking and develop new reasoning strategies on extremely hard tasks. In contrast, we argue that these conflicting findings may arise from primacy bias: early learned features can hinder learning on hard mathematical tasks, while in OOD settings, where such biases are misaligned with target tasks, RL can promote new reasoning strategies. 

Primacy Bias in Neural Networks. Similar to biological systems, neural networks also exhibit critical periods—early phases of training during which representations remain highly plastic and are disproportionately shaped by initial data [2]. This phenomenon has later been explored in reinforcement learning, where it is often referred to as primacy bias [17]. Primacy bias occurs when the RL learning process is disrupted by overfitting to earlier experiences. One effect induced by primacy bias is the reduction of update/gradient norm, causing parameters to stop changing and hindering learnability during RL training [1, 17]. Recently, [24] attributed primacy bias to limited feature learning, indicating a model’s confinement to the lazy regime in which it fits the data without substantially updating its representations. This effectively behaves as a linear model, and consequently generalizes poorly. We build on this line by connecting coverage shrinkage to primacy bias, showing that primacy bias also arises in RLVR training, hindering learnability on hard problems. 

Neural Tangent Kernel. The infinite-width limit is a well-studied model for deep network optimization [15]. In this regime, models remain close to initialization and behave like linear models governed by the initial Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK), which remains constant during training—known as the lazy training regime [8]. Several studies have investigated the inductive biases in lazy training [27, 6, 5], revealing that the NTKs preferentially fit data using the top eigenmodes of their spectrum, a phenomenon termed spectral bias . While this spectral bias can enable good generalization on some learning problems, target functions y(x) that are not in the top eigenspaces of the kernel (misaligned), which can be measured by centered-kernel alignment (CKA) [9], require a potentially much larger amount of data. In contrast, in a rich (feature-learning) regime, the model can deviate substantially from its initialization, enabling meaningful changes to its internal representations, improving sample efficiency on target functions that are challenging for the lazy regime.[12, 18, 4]. Furthermore, [18] characterizes the effect of delayed generalization (grokking) as a phase transition from lazy to rich (feature-learning) learning dynamics. In this paper, we show that RLVR models operate in the lazy regime, where RL-trained models remain close to the pre-trained model, inducing a bias toward solutions near the initial representation. As a result, optimization tends to prioritize improvements on easy problems while making little to no progress on harder ones, leading to a form of delayed generalization.

## 4 Primacy bias and Lazy learning 

We study how pre-training–induced bias affects coverage expansion (or shrinkage) during RL training through the lens of primacy bias [17, 2], a phenomenon in which early learning experiences hinder later learning phases. For example, pre-trained models tend to learn representations dominated by the majority classes, leaving rare classes underrepresented. Thus, RL finetuning is "initially restricted" by these "suboptimal" representations. Furthermore, we’ll show that, under some conditions, the feature updates during RL stage are limited, a phenomenon known as lazy learning [24, 8]. As a result, due to the primacy bias, hard (or rare) examples remain underexplored during the RL phase. 4.2 Lazy learning regime 

Recently, Lyle et al. [24] have proposed a framework to understand primacy bias in deep learning. In this framework, primacy bias occurs because the network fails to enter a feature-learning regime after early training. Instead, the fine-tuning stage operates in a lazy regime where the network becomes increasingly rigid and does not produce substantial changes in the learned representation. When the data distribution or task changes, e.g., as in post-training, gradient updates are insufficient to significantly modify the previously learned features. As a result, the model continues to rely on representations optimized for the initial data rather than adapting to the new distribution, leading to degraded performance relative to a freshly initialized model. Based on our observation and the above discussion, we hypothesize that RLVR is operated in the lazy regime . ## 6 The Unlearnable Phenomenon in RLVR Training. 

In this section, we show that, due to primacy bias, LLMs primarily improve rewards on already known and easy problems throughout the RLVR training. As a result, they achieve only marginal gains on harder problems. We also observe that, while training on these hard problems yields little improvement, it can improve Pass@ k performance; however, prolonged training comes at the expense of reduced Pass@ 1 due to forgetting on easy problems. 

6.1 The harder the examples are, the harder it is for RLVR models to learn 

Our hypothesis is that, in the lazy regime, RLVR models rely on their initial representations to optimize rewards, which may be insufficient for harder examples and thus lead to degraded learning efficiency. 

Setup. We fine-tune the Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct model on the MATH dataset and filter out unsolved problems under the final policy to focus on solvable instances. The remaining data is partitioned into disjoint subsets of increasing difficulty based on pass@1 accuracy, and separate policies are trained on each subset to analyze how learning dynamics vary with difficulty level. Additional experimental de-tails are provided in Appendix B. In short, difficulty is closely related to CKA: well-aligned examples are easier to learn, whereas poorly aligned examples remain difficult. This not only supports our anal-ysis above, but also sheds further light on the learning dynamics of RLVR on these examples. For eval-uation, we utilize test sets from Olympiad-Bench [13], Minerva [37], and MATH-500 [14] datasets. 

Remark. Throughout this paper, we define example difficulty by how challenging an example is for the model to solve, as measured by its accuracy. In the controlled toy experiments (Sec. 4), we have full control over the pretraining data distribution, allowing us to manipulate difficulty by increasing distribution skew. In contrast, in the large-scale LLM experiments, where the pretraining data distribution is inaccessible, we cannot directly manipulate difficulty. Instead, we estimate it empirically using the model’s accuracy on the MATH benchmark, with lower accuracy indicating greater difficulty. Despite these differences in how difficulty is manipulated or measured, both settings share the same underlying notion of difficulty. 

Training exclusively on hard examples yields little improvement. Our first result is shown in Fig. 7. We observe that RLVR yields meaningful improvements only on easy subsets, whereas on harder subsets, it shows little to no improvement, even when training is focused on them. Our results show that during training, most improvements arise from easier examples, while harder examples contribute minimally to overall reward gains. This provides further evidence to the phenomenon we observed earlier in Sec. 4, where rare (hard) problems exhibit delayed improvement and require substantially more training steps due to a lack of feature learning, and prolonged training is needed to adapt their representations. 80 20 40 60 80 100 120 140 160 180

Discussion. Our analysis suggests that just exclusively learning on hard problems (without any algorithmic modification) could be sufficient to improve models’ coverage (Pass@ k). However, prolonged training can also degrade Pass@ k due to forgetting solvable/easy problems. This explains why, in mathematical reasoning — where Pass@ k is computed over a mixture of easy and hard problems — such forgetting can significantly hurt overall performance and hinder progress on hard problems. In contrast, on OOD and more difficult tasks [22, 35], this forgetting effect can actually be beneficial, since the base model performs poorly with initial representations and improvements on previously unsolved problems dominate the evaluation. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We investigate the ongoing debate over whether RLVR truly expands the reasoning boundary beyond what is acquired during pre-training, through the lens of primacy bias and the lazy training regime. Through extensive, controlled, and large-scale empirical analysis, we confirm that this bias emerges during RLVR training, causing models to remain confined around their pre-trained initialization and preventing meaningful adaptation to hard examples. We then uncover an unlearnable phenomenon as a consequence of primacy bias, where RL favors improvement on easy problems, hindering the ability to learn harder ones. Consequently, attempting to escape the lazy regime can help models learn previously unsolvable problems, but at the cost of forgetting easier ones. This helps explain why, in OOD domains—where forgetting is less harmful—RL can expand the reasoning boundary, in contrast to mathematical reasoning.

This work primarily contributes to the ongoing debate on whether RLVR truly expands reasoning boundaries beyond those acquired during pre-training, attributing these conflicting views to the primacy bias phenomenon. It may help practitioners detect and mitigate the trade-off between adaptability and forgetting in post-training, thereby improving reliability in verifiable domains such as math and code. However, stronger RLVR training may also enhance general reasoning capabilities that could be misused. Moreover, methods relying on automated verifiers are susceptible to reward hacking and over-optimization, while diversity-preserving interventions may increase the likelihood of rare undesirable outputs. Our empirical analysis is limited to finite evaluation sets and task-specific reward signals. Extending our analysis to coding and other verifiable domains would be valuable. However, due to computational constraints, particularly the high cost of sandbox-based program execution for coding RLVR, we leave these extensions for future work.
