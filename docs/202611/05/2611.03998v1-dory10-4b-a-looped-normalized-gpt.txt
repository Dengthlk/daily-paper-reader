# Dory1.0-4B: A Looped Normalized-GPT 

> Maxim Fishman *, Simeng Sun *, Shantanu Acharya, Faisal Ladhak, Brian Chmiel, Santiago Akle Serano, Ron Banner, Boris Ginsburg, Krishna C. Puvvada

Abstract. 

We introduce Dory1.0-4B, a 4-billion-parameter looped (latent-recurrent) language model with elastic depth: a single checkpoint serves different inference compute budgets. Dory1.0-4B is built on a normalized Transformer (nGPT) backbone with interleaved sliding-window and full-attention layers, organized into an input block, a shared recurrent block, and an output block. At inference, the recurrent block can be applied a variable number of times. We pretrained Dory1.0-4B on 6 trillion tokens. The first 4.5 trillion tokens were trained in NVFP4, and additional pretraining then extended the context length to 256K tokens. Next, we post-trained the model using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), with a difficulty-aware loop curriculum that routes harder prompts to deeper loops. Increasing the loop count improves accuracy, with the largest gains on coding, agentic, and long-context benchmarks. With two or more loops, Dory1.0-4B outperforms larger models such as Qwen3.5-9B, Gemma 4 12B, and Gemma 4 26B-A4B on average across 12 benchmarks. Looping also makes Dory1.0-4B more token-efficient on agentic benchmarks: with four loops, it generates 1.3–2.5 × fewer tokens than with one loop, as the extra computation is spent in latent space rather than on longer outputs. To reduce memory during looping, Dory1.0-4B caches keys and values only from the last loop, keeping its KV-cache footprint equal to that of a non-looped model at any loop count while retaining most of the accuracy gains. We open-source the BF16, MXFP8, NVFP4, and GGUF quantized checkpoints. 

## 1. Introduction 

We present Dory1.0-4B, the first open looped language model in the Nemotron Labs family. Scaling test-time compute has become a major driver of LLM capability, but this compute is mostly spent in token space: models generate longer reasoning traces, and latency, KV-cache footprint, and serving cost grow with every generated token. Looped (latent-recurrent) language models offer a complementary compute scaling axis (Dehghani et al., 2019; Saunshi et al., 2025; Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). By repeatedly applying a shared block of layers, a looped model spends more computation per token in latent space without adding parameters. Dory1.0-4B brings this idea to a fully post-trained model built for reasoning and agentic tasks. We train Dory1.0-4B with a mixture of loop counts (Ladhak et al., 2026), so that a single checkpoint can be served at any loop count 𝑘 

and trade inference compute for accuracy per request, with no retraining and no model swap. Figure 1 summarizes the effect of looping. On the equal-weight average of 12 benchmarks (Table 2), Dory1.0-4B improves from 35.2 at one loop to 39.7 at two loops and 40.6 at four loops (Figure 1a). It outperforms larger models such as Qwen3.5-9B, Gemma 4 12B, and Gemma 4 26B-A4B, and comes within 1.7 points of Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, a model with roughly 9 × as many total parameters. The additional latent computation also makes Dory1.0-4B more token-efficient: across four agentic benchmarks, four loops reduce the number of generated tokens per attempt by 1.3–2.5 × relative to one loop, with the largest reductions on GDPval-AA v2 and Terminal-Bench v2.1 (Figure 1b and Section 4.4), partially offsetting the higher compute per token. ## 2. Architecture 

Dory1.0-4B is based on the normalized Transformer (Loshchilov et al., 2025), retaining its residual-update rule. Our full nGPT implementation additionally L2-normalizes the concatenated, gated attention outputs before the output projection and the SwiGLU activations before the MLP down-projection. We implement these normalizations with fused Triton kernels. We initialize the raw residual-rate, query–key scale, and logit-scale parameters at 0.0177, rather than the paper’s 1 /√𝑑 model ,while retaining an initial effective residual rate of 0.05. We augment this formulation with input-dependent sigmoid attention-output gates and grouped-query attention with 16 query heads and 4 KV groups. The stack contains 72 physical layers: 36 MLP-only layers interleaved with 27 sliding-window and 9 full-attention layers. Thus, the attention layers follow a 3:1 sliding-window/full-attention pattern. Figure 2 shows the overall architecture of Dory1.0-4B. 

## 3. Pretraining 

In this section, we provide details on the NVFP4 pretraining recipe, data, and hyperparameters used for Dory1.0-4B Base. We also share details on the long-context extension phase. 

3.1. NVFP4 Training 

We first pretrain a model using Megatron-LM and NVIDIA Transformer Engine’s NVFP4 block-scaling recipe (NVIDIA, 2025b). This mixed-precision recipe yields an end-to-end training speedup of approximately 20% over BF16 by using E2M1 4-bit operands for supported linear-layer matrix multiplications, while retaining BF16 model weights and FP32 optimizer master weights, Adam moments, and gradient accumulation and reduction. We do not exempt the first or last decoder layers from NVFP4. We use distributed Adam with ( 𝛽 1, 𝛽 2) = (0 .9, 0.95), global gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, and no weight decay or learning-rate warmup. We use the pretraining data of Nemotron 3 Ultra (NVIDIA, 2026), following its two-phase curricu-lum (Feng et al., 2024): a phase-1 mixture that favors diversity, followed by a phase-2 mixture that favors quality. We train on approximately 3T tokens of phase-1 data at a sequence length of 8,192, then switch to the phase-2 mixture and increase the sequence length to 32,768. Reducing the global batch size from 768 to 192 sequences preserves 6,291,456 tokens per optimizer step. The learning-rate schedule spans both NVFP4 pretraining and the subsequent BF16 continuation: we hold the learning rate at 3 .2 × 10 −4 through 4.5T tokens, then apply minus-square-root decay toward 10 −5 at 6T tokens. The NVFP4 stage ends at iteration 720,750, after approximately 4.535T tokens. 

3.2. Training with Loops 

We continue from the NVFP4 checkpoint in BF16 for approximately 1.465T additional tokens, reaching the 6T endpoint. This is a normal checkpoint restart: optimizer, scheduler, RNG, and data-position state are preserved, and the phase-2 data mixture remains unchanged. We retain the 32,768-token sequence length and global batch size of 192, using 192 data-parallel GPUs across 48 nodes, a microbatch size of one, and no tensor, pipeline, or context parallelism. We partition the pretrained stack into a 16-layer input block, a shared 40-layer recurrent block, and a 16-layer output block. The input and output blocks run once, while the recurrent block runs 𝑘 

times with all of its parameters, including nGPT residual rates, shared across invocations. The effective forward depth is 32 + 40 𝑘 , ranging from 72 to 192 physical-layer applications. Let 𝑥 denote

The subset rotates with the step and microbatch index, providing additional one-loop training signal; consequently, the realized depth mixture is not uniform. 

3.3. Truncated Backpropagation 

We use truncated backpropagation through loops (TBPTL) with depth one. Earlier invocations run without gradient tracking; the final recurrent invocation and the input/output blocks remain trainable, with the input block receiving gradients through the final invocation’s residual input connection. We apply next-token cross-entropy only after the final sampled invocation, without intermediate-exit or early-loop auxiliary losses. 

3.4. Long-Context Extension 

Starting from the 6T recurrent checkpoint, we perform a long-context extension in BF16, increasing the sequence length from 32,768 to 262,144 tokens. An additional approximately 300B tokens brings the total training budget to approximately 6.3T tokens. We increase the full-attention RoPE base from 500,000 to 10,000,000, while retaining the sliding-window RoPE base of 10,000 and window size of 1,024. The recurrent architecture, input/output transforms, base loop-count distribution, and TBPTL depth remain unchanged. We continue the pretraining objective on a long-context data blend similar to Nemotron 3.5 Super (NVIDIA, 2026). Unlike the NVFP4-to-BF16 continuation, we load model weights only and reset the optimizer, scheduler, RNG, and iteration state. We train for 47,683 optimizer steps at a constant learning rate of 10 −5 without warmup. Reducing the global batch size from 192 to 24 sequences preserves 6,291,456 tokens per step. We use 192 GPUs across 48 nodes with eight-way context parallelism, 24-way data parallelism, a microbatch size of one, and no tensor or pipeline parallelism. All-gather communication accommodates the sliding-window attention implementation. With 24 data-parallel replicas, the unchanged 0.1 override setting selects two replicas (approximately 8.3%) for one-loop execution after integer rounding.

## 4. Post-Training 

Our post-training pipeline (Figure 3) combines a heavy supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage and a light 200-step reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) stage. Initialized from the long-context extended checkpoint, SFT adapts Dory1.0-4B to instruction following using mixed thinking and non-thinking responses. The RLVR stage begins from the 9-epoch SFT checkpoint and optimizes the model using task-specific rewards across multiple environments. Both stages train the shared recurrent block at multiple loop counts. While SFT maintains the variable-loop setup from pretraining and context extension, RLVR dynamically routes harder prompts (lower pass rates) to deeper loops with higher probability.

The SFT data blend is the same as the text blend for post-training Nemotron 3.5 Super, 2 and packed offline to a maximum sequence length of 262,144 tokens. The blend contains both thinking and non-thinking responses. For thinking examples, the prompt supplies an open <think> tag, and the model generates a reasoning trace followed by </think> and the final response. For non-thinking examples, the prompt supplies an empty <think></think> block, and the model generates the response directly. The chat template delimits messages with <|im_start|> and <|im_end|> and exposes tool definitions in the system message. Tool calls use XML-style <tool_call> blocks while tool results are wrapped in <tool_response> blocks within user-formatted messages. We optimize token-level cross-entropy on assistant tokens, including the reasoning trace and closing </think> 

tag in thinking examples, while masking system, user, and tool tokens from the loss. Extended SFT yields continued improvements across multiple benchmarks evaluated with one loop. Between iterations 3,600 and 32,400 (epochs one and nine), SciCode improves from 13.11% to 18.14%, Terminal-Bench v2.1 from 4.2% to 13.5%, and GPQA Diamond from 43.13% to 54.95%. Over the same interval, IFBench increases from 28.94% to 38.50%, MATH-500 from 60.2% to 75.2%, and AA-LCR from 29.5% to 37.3%. These results show that longer SFT continues to improve coding, agentic, reasoning, and instruction-following performance at a fixed one-loop inference budget. 

4.2. RLVR Environments and rewards. We train across 23 single-step environments covering coding, agentic tasks, reasoning, long-context question answering, instruction following, structured outputs, safety, and other environments. For LLM-based reward, we use Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 3

for scoring the policy responses. 

Reward profiling and data selection. Before RLVR training, we profile candidate prompts using the SFT checkpoint at iteration 32,400 with one-loop execution. For each prompt, eight responses are collected and graded with the corresponding environment’s reward function. We retain prompts whose mean reward is strictly between 0 and 1, excluding extremes of consistently zero reward and consistently full reward. Each prompt’s profiled pass rate (or mean profiling reward) is preserved for the difficulty-aware loop curriculum introduced later. We then re-balance the profiled data by oversampling targeted environments and filter prompts to fit the RLVR training context. The final RLVR data mixture comprises ∼47% agentic tasks, ∼27.5% reasoning and coding, ∼8.1% long-context question answering, ∼17.4% instruction following, safety, and other domains. 

RLVR training setup. Starting from the SFT checkpoint, we train for 200 steps using asyn-chronous group relative policy optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) in NeMo RL (NVIDIA, 2025a). Each step samples 384 prompts and generates 16 responses per prompt, yielding a global batch size of 6,144 rollouts. We train in BF16 with distributed Adam, using ( 𝛽 1, 𝛽 2) = (0 .9, 0.999), a constant learning rate of 4 × 10 −6 without warmup, no weight decay, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Rollout workers use temperature 1.0 and top-𝑝 = 1 .0 within the 65,536-token context window. We launch separate vLLM rollout workers each with a fixed loop count 𝑘 from {1, 2, 3, 4}. Within each global batch, prompt quotas follow weights of 4:1:1:1 for 𝑘 = 1 , 2, 3, 4, respectively. Each prompt is assigned a loop count before rollout expansion, and all 16 rollouts in its GRPO group use that same count.

4.4. Token Efficiency 

Additional loops not only improve accuracy on agentic tasks but also reduce the number of tokens the model generates to complete them. Table 3 decomposes the output tokens per attempt into the number of turns per attempt and the output tokens per turn. Going from one to four loops, output tokens per attempt fall by 1.3–2.5 ×, and both factors contribute, in proportions that depend on the task. On the long-horizon tasks, most of the savings come from completing the task in fewer turns: on GDPval-AA v2, turns per attempt drop from 116 to 49 while output tokens per turn change by only 5%, and on Terminal-Bench v2.1, turns drop by 39% and tokens per turn by 20%. On SWE-Bench Verified, the two factors contribute comparably, with 17% fewer turns and 11% fewer tokens per turn. On 𝜏 3-Banking, where the conversation length is largely set by the simulated user, the number of turns changes by only 7%, and most of the savings come from 18% shorter turns. Thus, with additional loops, Dory1.0-4B both takes fewer actions to complete a task and writes less per action. These savings offset much of the higher compute per token. Approximating generation compute as output tokens times layer applications per token (72, 112, and 192 at 𝑘 = 1 , 2, 4), two loops cost only 1.03–1.25 × the compute of one loop across the four tasks, yet recover most of the accuracy gain of four loops: 86% of the GDPval-AA v2 Elo gain, 80% on SWE-Bench Verified, 58% on Terminal-Bench v2.1, and 42% on 𝜏 3-Banking. Four loops, by contrast, cost 1.07–2.04 × the compute of one loop. The second loop therefore gives the largest accuracy gain per unit of added compute, making 𝑘 = 2 a good default, with 𝑘 = 4 for tasks where accuracy matters most.

## 6. Inference Optimization 

We explore two ways to improve inference efficiency in Dory1.0-4B. Last-loop KV caching makes attention KV memory independent of loop count, while self-speculative decoding uses shallow drafting and deeper verification to accelerate generation. We evaluate these optimizations separately, using per-loop KV caches for self-speculative decoding. 

6.1. Last-Loop KV Caching 

With standard per-loop caching, each recurrent attention layer maintains a separate KV cache for every loop: a loop-𝑖 query attends to earlier tokens’ loop-𝑖 keys and values. The recurrent block’s KV memory therefore grows linearly with the loop count 𝑘 , while the input and output blocks each retain one cache. Last-loop KV caching (Ladhak et al., 2026) instead maintains a single cache per recurrent attention layer, overwriting its entries at each loop. After each forward step, the cache retains the final-loop keys and values. Queries attend to final-loop entries for previously cached tokens and current-loop entries for tokens processed in the same step. This keeps attention KV memory at its one-loop footprint for every 𝑘 , without retraining, but exposes earlier-loop queries to final-loop keys and values rather than the same-loop entries used during training. We compare both cache policies on the final post-trained checkpoint at 𝑘 = 4, with 𝑘 = 1 as a reference, where the policies coincide (Table 6). Serving uses prefix caching and chunked prefill. Last-loop caching retains gains from additional loops, with score changes of −2.12 to +0 .42 points relative to per-loop caching at 𝑘 = 4. 6.2. Self-Speculative Decoding 

Dory1.0-4B uses its variable execution depth for self-speculative decoding, with the same checkpoint drafting at 𝑘 = 1 and verifying at 𝑘 = 4. The drafter proposes 𝛾 = 4 tokens autoregressively, which the verifier evaluates in a batched forward pass. Rejection sampling uses both models’ probabilities to accept proposals and correct rejections, preserving the verifier’s sampling distribution in exact arithmetic without a separate draft model. With per-loop caching, drafting and verification share the KV caches of the input block and first recurrent loop. We also reuse the corresponding draft activations during verification, avoiding redundant computation for token positions already processed by the drafter. Drafting and verification execute sequentially, so the acceleration comes from accepting multiple tokens per verification step and reusing computation. We first measure steady-state decode throughput across batch sizes and prompt lengths (Figure 6). With KV and activation reuse, self-speculation achieves 1 .37–1 .71 × the throughput of autoregressive decoding at 𝑘 = 4 across all measured configurations. Gains generally decrease at larger batch sizes but persist through batch size 256 for shorter prompts and batch size 64 for 16K-token prompts. Self-speculative decoding reduces mean prefill-plus-decode request duration relative to autoregressive decoding at 𝑘 = 4, using the same checkpoint, benchmark sampling settings, and serving configuration (Table 7). Across six benchmarks, self-speculative decoding achieves 1 .11–1 .53 × speedups in mean prefill-plus-decode request duration, with gains depending on the workload and serving conditions.
