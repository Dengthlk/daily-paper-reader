## 1. Introduction 

Learning vision-language-action (VLA) models requires demonstrations that connect language goals, visual obser-vations, and executable actions [2, 11, 12, 16]. Collecting such data at scale in the physical world is costly, requir-ing robotic hardware, human supervision, and repeated en-vironment setup [10]. Video games offer a practical set-ting for collecting large-scale interaction data: human play-ers can perform diverse tasks in rich virtual environments, while visual observations and keyboard-and-mouse actions can be recorded together [20]. Paired with language anno-tations, these trajectories support learning to translate task instructions into actions grounded in visual observations. In particular, 3D games demand close coordination between high-level decision-making and low-level control, requiring agents to navigate, scout, engage opponents, and interact with the environment through rapid and precise keyboard-and-mouse actions. Under partial observability and contin-uously changing conditions, agents must continually adapt their decisions and actions to environmental feedback. Tasks in 3D games often require agents to combine mul-tiple skills across successive stages. For example, an agent asked to approach the building, scout the entrance, engage the enemy, and fall back to cover to recover may fight effec-tively yet continue chasing an opponent when it should re-treat. Successful task completion therefore requires agents to understand how each skill serves the current subgoal and to select, compose, and switch skills as the task pro-gresses. This motivates two complementary data require-ments. First, hierarchical annotations linking tasks, sub-goals, and skill-level instructions : demonstrations should connect overall objectives to local goals and concrete in-structions, making explicit what to achieve and how to act, with temporal boundaries indicating subgoal transi-tions. Second, large-scale action data covering diverse goals and tasks : extensive keyboard-and-mouse trajecto-ries should span varied scenarios and skill combinations, capturing how navigation, reconnaissance, combat, interac-tion, and recovery are executed and composed for different objectives. Together, these properties support learning both task structure and the behaviors needed to realize it. Existing game-interaction datasets exhibit limitations in both behavioral coverage and supervision for task struc-ture. EgoCS-400K [7] provides detailed replay-grounded actions and multi-granularity captions, but remains confined to Counter-Strike and primarily describes local clips and ac-tion chains. Open-P2P [23] broadens game coverage, yet its sparse, goal-oriented instructions provide limited super-vision for connecting fine-grained skill execution with in-termediate and overall objectives. GameHorizon [22] intro-duces multi-horizon instructions aligned with action record-ings, but constructs its instruction hierarchy under prede-fined duration ranges for operations, goals, and strategies. These constraints may split a sustained subgoal or combine several brief subgoals, making temporal hierarchy an imper-fect proxy for task-stage structure. These limitations moti-vate demonstrations with broader behavioral coverage and explicit, objective-driven stage annotations, linking each task to its constituent skills and marking when execution transitions between subgoals. To bridge this gap, we introduce AceAction-5M , a dataset that unifies multi-game tasks, fine-grained hierar-chical instructions, and temporally aligned keyboard-and-mouse trajectories. Each episode pairs a global task de-scription with temporally bounded stage-level subgoals, and aligns local action instructions frame by frame with the keyboard-and-mouse actions that realize them. The resulting trajectories capture how navigation, reconnais-sance, combat, interaction, and recovery are composed and switched, providing a data foundation for learning to map complex task instructions to continuous, fine-grained con-trol. Our contributions are as follows: • A hierarchically annotated, multi-game action dataset. AceAction covers 42 games and 5,027.87 hours of play, with 5,476,222 episodes in which task descrip-tions, stage-level subgoals, and local action instructions are jointly aligned with frame-level keyboard-and-mouse trajectories. • An annotation scheme that makes stage structure ex-plicit. We define 43 skill categories spanning navigation, reconnaissance, combat, interaction, and recovery, and mark the start and end of every stage, turning when to switch from an implicit property of the data into an ex-plicit, learnable signal. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Game Data and Benchmarks for World Mod-eling 

Gameplay data supports action-conditioned world model-ing by aligning visual observations, actions, and environ-ment states. GameNGen [21] learns video prediction from DOOM trajectories collected by a reinforcement learn-ing agent, while DIAMOND [1] demonstrates interactive world modeling using recorded CS:GO gameplay. Wild-World [13] provides frame-level annotations of skeletons, camera poses, and depth for evaluating action following and state consistency. 2EgoCS-400K [7] synchronizes player states, actions, and events extracted from CS:GO/CS2 replays with over 10,000 hours of first-person video. Its multi-granularity captions and video generation prompts link visual changes to re-play information, providing language supervision for world modeling. GameVerse [9] provides minute-scale gameplay videos with temporally coherent camera trajectories and event-aligned structured captions, supporting long-horizon interactive world modeling. GameHorizon [22] combines 5,000 hours of gameplay from 21 games with synchronized keyboard-and-mouse ac-tions and three levels of instructions: operations, goals, and strategies. Its offline benchmark evaluates action un-derstanding, goal decomposition, and cross-horizon consis-tency through multiple-choice questions, with predicted ac-tion sequences matched to answer options for scoring. Our work links subtask instructions to waypoints and tempo-rally aligned keyboard-and-mouse trajectories within multi-stage tasks, providing supervision for subgoal transitions and continuous control. 

2.2. Game Action Data for Instruction Following 

Large-scale gameplay demonstrations support action policy learning [24, 25]. MineRL [8] provides human demonstra-tions in Minecraft with automatically annotated states and actions. VPT [3] uses inverse dynamics to infer keyboard-and-mouse actions from Minecraft videos, while Nitro-Gen [15] extracts gamepad actions from online videos for cross-game behavior cloning. Language-based supervision further connects gameplay behaviors to task goals. MineDojo [5] combines an open-ended task suite specified in natural language with an internet-scale multimodal knowledge base of videos, tuto-rials, wiki pages, and forum discussions. Open-P2P [23] provides cross-game keyboard-and-mouse trajectories with sparse goal-oriented instructions. SIMA [18] learns to fol-low language instructions across 3D environments, focus-ing primarily on short-horizon tasks, while SIMA 2 [4] extends this setting to complex instructions and instruc-tion chaining. STEVE-1 [14] supports text- and visual-goal control in Minecraft and composes multi-step behav-iors through sequential subgoal prompts. AceAction organizes demonstrations around complete multi-stage tasks, with annotations spanning overall goals, stage-level subgoals, and local skill instructions. Each sub-task is associated with a waypoint, explicit temporal bound-aries, and aligned keyboard-and-mouse trajectories. This structure connects individual skill execution to task pro-gression, providing supervision for action execution and the timing of subgoal transitions. 

## 3. Dataset 

3.1. Data Source 

Our dataset comprises two complementary components: directly collected gameplay and publicly sourced game-play. The directly collected gameplay was recorded from full-time in-house staff who held a Gold rank and con-sented to participate. These recordings were transcribed in anonymized form during data collection. Together, these components provide approximately 5,180 hours of game-play recordings paired with keyboard-and-mouse actions. 

Directly collected gameplay. We collected gameplay recordings and corresponding keyboard-and-mouse actions from human players. This component includes approxi-mately 1,891 hours of Game for Peace, including combat scenarios, and 261.17 hours of Valorant. These recordings pair gameplay observations with the actions performed by human players. 

Publicly sourced gameplay. To broaden coverage across games and tasks, we incorporate 2,103.05 hours of CS:GO and CS2 gameplay from match recordings collected online, 811.50 hours of annotated 3D gameplay from Open-P2P, and 113.25 hours of Minecraft gameplay from VPT. These durations refer to the portions included in our dataset rather than the complete upstream datasets. 

Action representation and sampling rates. Across both components, we use a common action representation that encodes keyboard and mouse-button states, together with horizontal and vertical mouse displacement. Our directly collected gameplay is recorded at 16 Hz, corresponding to one action step every 62.5 ms. For publicly sourced game-play, we CS:GO and CS2 action sequences are 16 Hz, while retaining the original 20 Hz sampling rate for Open-P2P and VPT. 

3.2. Construction Pipeline 

We construct hierarchical annotations from gameplay ob-servations and actions recorded from human players (Fig-ure 2). The pipeline first identifies skill-based subgoals and uses visually recognizable waypoints to construct naviga-tion subgoals. Adjacent subgoals are then composed into overall instructions. Throughout this process, source in-dices preserve the correspondence between semantic anno-tations and the original image–action sequences. 

Skill-based subgoal annotation. We group every four con-secutive actions into an annotation unit with a correspond-ing visual observation. Each trajectory is processed in con-secutive windows of up to 32 units. Given the ordered ob-servations and actions, a vision–language model jointly pre-dicts a skill category, start and end indices, and a subgoal description for each segment. The skill category identifies the type of behavior, while the subgoal description specifies its concrete objective. A new segment begins when the primary objec-tive or interaction target changes. To preserve this conti-nuity across window boundaries, each subsequent window receives the previous window’s final observation, skill cat-egory, and subgoal description. The model also predicts a continuation flag indicating whether its first segment ex-tends the preceding subgoal. We merge the two segments only when this flag is positive, their skill categories match, and their sequence indices are adjacent. This allows a sub-goal to span multiple annotation windows. After normal-izing category labels, descriptions, and boundaries, we re-move residual airborne segments and map the retained sub-goals back to their original image–action sequences using source indices. 

Waypoint-based subgoal annotation. Navigation sub-goals are constructed around arrival events at visually rec-ognizable locations. We identify these events using a slid-ing window containing up to eight recent observations. The window advances by one observation at each step, and the model evaluates whether the newest frame shows arrival at a specific location. Its input includes the observations, recorded actions, associated skill annotations, previously accepted waypoint names, and the most recent waypoint. Visual observations provide the evidence for arrival, while actions and skill annotations provide behavioral context. For each accepted arrival event, the model assigns a de-scriptive waypoint name and associates it with the source frame. Previously accepted names are reused when the same location is recognized again. Continued activity within an already reached location does not produce re-peated arrival annotations. Each waypoint thus identifies a destination and the observation at which it is reached. We then sample clips of varying lengths that end at the waypoint frame. This creates navigation examples with a shared destination but different starting points and inter-vening actions. For each clip, the model receives sampled keyframes, including the first and arrival frames, together with the complete action sequence, skill context, and target waypoint name. It generates a navigation subgoal that in-cludes the waypoint name and describes the observed route or movement toward that destination. Although annotation uses sampled keyframes, the resulting subgoal retains the complete image–action sequence. 

Instruction composition. We combine adjacent subgoals from the same trajectory in execution order to form an over-all instruction. The overall instruction summarizes the task defined by this sequence, while the constituent subgoals re-tain its intermediate objectives. A composed task can in-clude successive subgoals of the same skill or transitions between different skills. For example, navigation subgoals may lead to a destination where the player subsequently per-forms an interaction. For each composed sample, we retain the overall in-struction, ordered subgoal descriptions, skill categories, segment boundaries, and corresponding image–action se-quence. These boundaries identify the observations and ac-tions associated with each intermediate objective, preserv-ing the relationship between the complete task and its exe-cution stages. Before export, we check that descriptions are non-empty, skill categories are valid, and segment boundaries are or-dered and within the source sequence. We also verify image–action correspondence and confirm that each navi-gation clip ends at its designated waypoint frame. The re-sulting samples explicitly connect overall instructions, in-termediate subgoals, and executable actions.

Model-based verification. We use Qwen3.8-27B [17] and GLM-5.3-FLASH [6] to review each annotated sample against its gameplay clip and recorded actions. Both mod-els perform bottom-up verification across three annotation levels. First, they check whether each action description matches the visible behavior and recorded controls. Sec-ond, they assess whether each subgoal accurately describes the objective supported by its associated observations and actions. Finally, they verify that the overall instruction is consistent with the constituent subgoals and the complete trajectory. At every level, the review checks both visual grounding and action consistency. A sample is retained only when both models approve all annotation levels. A rejection at any level by either model leads to its exclusion. 

Human inspection. Following model-based verification, eight human reviewers inspect 500 trajectories, with each trajectory assessed by at least two reviewers. The inspec-tion checks whether action descriptions match the observed operations, subgoals reflect the corresponding execution stages, and overall instructions accurately describe the com-plete tasks. Reviewers assess these annotations against both the gameplay clips and recorded actions. The pass rate across independent review records is 95.8% , where each record represents one reviewer’s assessment of one trajec-tory.

Our dataset emphasizes two aspects for training and evaluating game agents, including vision–language–action (VLA) models. First, trajectory segmentation is grounded in both player-view videos and recorded keyboard–mouse actions. Each annotated segment preserves the temporal correspondence between visual observations, language instructions, and control inputs, providing aligned supervision for instruction following and action prediction. Second, our dataset pro-vides extensive subgoal descriptions and second-scale skill annotations within an explicit task–subgoal–skill hierarchy. Overall task instructions are linked to ordered subgoals and action description, with temporal boundaries marking sub-goal transitions. These annotations provide structured tex-tual supervision for reasoning about task decomposition, skill composition, and subgoal transitions, supporting both training and evaluation on multi-stage tasks. 

## 4. Conclusion 

We introduce AceAction-5M, a dataset of human game-play across multiple 3D games for training and evaluat-ing game agents and vision–language–action (VLA) mod-els. Combining directly collected and publicly sourced gameplay, AceAction links player-view observations and aligned keyboard–mouse actions through a task–subgoal– skill instruction hierarchy. Our annotation pipeline iden-tifies subgoals from changes in objectives or interaction targets, grounds navigation subgoals in visually recogniz-able waypoints, and composes adjacent subgoals into multi-stage task instructions. Explicit temporal boundaries con-nect overall task objectives to local skill execution and mark subgoal transitions. Model-based verification and human inspection assess consistency among language annotations, visual observations, and actions.
