Harness evolution from a common base exhibits Locality , Complementarity , and Nonstationarity : variants repair different failures, and their utility changes as the policy learns. Rather than seeking a universally superior harness, we introduce Harness-S to selectively rescue failures of a base execution system through 

sibling harnesses and transfer the resulting capabilities into a single policy that remains effective under the original harness . Directional Harness Evolution develops specialized sibling harnesses along distinct repair directions, forming a reusable Sibling Harness Pool that remains fixed during policy training. Com-plementary Harness Rescue Training invokes this pool only when all initial attempts under the base harness fail. The same policy generates fresh rollouts under selected auxiliary harnesses, and these trajectories re-place the failed group for reinforcement learning. An Adaptive Harness Router selects the Top-k variants using Harness Behavior Profiles and voting across multiple LLMs. Policy-aware profile re-anchoring refreshes this evidence as the policy learns. Across AppWorld, SWE-Gym, and DBBench, the trained policy substan-tially outperforms policy-only training when returned to the original base harness. Behavioral analyses and held-out cases support the transfer of harness-elicited behaviors into the policy itself. Reusing the router for Rank-1 inference provides additional gains at the same executor rollout count.

# 1 Introduction 

An agent’s behavior depends jointly on its model policy and its harness : the prompts, tool interfaces, context and memory mechanisms, and execution rules that govern interaction with the environment. Even with model weights held fixed, the harness shapes the information the policy receives and how its decisions become actions. ReAct demonstrates the value of interleaving reasoning and action (Yao et al., 2023b), while SWE-agent shows how the design of the agent–computer interface affects task execution (Yang et al., 2024b). Recent methods automate the optimization of prompts, workflows, and harness code from execution feedback (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Agrawal et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026). A central question is whether specialized execution support can improve the policy itself, so that its benefits persist under the original harness. We study harness evolution from a common base H0 within a single execution system, a setting motivated by recent work such as Meta-Harness (Lee et al., 2026), HarnessX (Chen et al., 2026b), and Life-Harness (Xu et al., 2026). We call the resulting variants sibling harnesses : they share the execution foundation of a complete system, such as DeepSeek Harness (DeepSeek-AI, 2026), while modifying how it supports the policy. Within this setting, we observe three properties: • Locality. Harness edits often repair a subset of failures while introducing regressions on previously solved tasks (Figure 1a). Their effects depend on the failures being targeted, so successive variants develop distinct strengths. • Complementarity. Sibling harnesses can recover one another’s errors. Their oracle union covers more tasks than individual variants or repeated sampling with H0 at the same rollout count (Figure 1b), showing the benefit of diverse execution behaviors. 1arXiv:2611.04277v1 [cs.AI] 3 Nov 2026 20 10 0 10 20 30

• Nonstationarity. A harness’s utility changes as the executing policy learns. Its advantage over H0 can become a disadvantage, and the ranking of variants can reverse across policy checkpoints (Figure 1c). These properties expose two limitations of harness optimization. (i) Combining local repairs into a universal harness is extremely difficult. Repairs may impose incompatible execution requirements, and even compatible edits can interfere with one another. A single accumulated design can therefore lose the strengths of its constituent variants. 

(ii) Coupled harness–policy evolution incurs substantial evolution cost and limits harness reuse. Alternating harness and policy updates (Chen et al., 2026b,c) requires repeated generation and validation of variants as earlier repairs become less useful to the changing policy. Gains achieved by the evolved pair may also fail to transfer when the policy returns to the base harness H0. We introduce Harness-S to selectively rescue failures of the base system with specialized sibling harnesses and transfer the resulting capabilities into a single policy that remains effective under the original H0.

Directional Harness Evolution first develops variants along distinct repair directions. Starting from H0, we diag-nose failures in execution trajectories and let each child harness target a limited set of related causes. Joint validation coverage selects the next parent set; all executable, protocol-compliant variants are retained. The resulting large, reusable Sibling Harness Pool preserves distinct repair specializations and remains fixed throughout policy train-ing. 

Complementary Harness Rescue Training uses this pool to expand exploration on tasks the policy fails to solve with H0. When all initial rollouts for a task fail, the Adaptive Harness Router selects the Top-k auxiliary har-nesses, and the same policy generates fresh rollouts under them from the task’s initial state. These rescue rollouts replace the failed group for reinforcement learning, allowing the shared policy to learn from its own actions under complementary execution support. The objective is to retain the acquired behaviors when the policy returns to H0.The Adaptive Harness Router matches tasks to sibling harnesses using behavioral evidence and independent votes from multiple LLMs. Harness Behavior Profiles describe the task conditions under which each variant helps or hin-ders the current policy relative to H0. To account for nonstationarity, policy-aware profile re-anchoring periodically refreshes the profiles from current-policy validation trajectories, keeping routing aligned with the policy as it learns. # 2 Related Work 

## 2.1 Harness Evolution 

Execution design shapes the behavior expressed by a language model, from reasoning and action representations (Yao et al., 2023b; Xu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b) to interaction interfaces and search (Yang et al., 2024b; Zhou et al., 2024a). Automatic optimization extends this design space through instruction refinement (Yang et al., 2024a; Yuk-sekgonul et al., 2024), evolutionary prompt search (Fernando et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2025), and program compilation or reflective selection (Khattab et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2026). Memory, skill libraries, and context adaptation provide another route to improving execution from experience (Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023, 2024c; Zhang et al., 2026f). At the system level, agent design and workflow search optimize executable structures (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), while code evolution, end-to-end harness optimization, and runtime adaptation develop broader interventions (Zhang et al., 2026a; Lee et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). Harness-S uses directional evolution to retain complementary local repairs in a reusable sibling pool. The implementations remain fixed during policy training; their behavioral profiles are refreshed to track changes in utility as the shared policy learns. 

## 2.2 Harness–Policy Optimization 

Reinforcement learning improves agent behavior through outcome feedback (Shao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025), supported by executable environments and training interfaces (Pan et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). Interactive benchmarks cover web services (Yao et al., 2023a; Zhou et al., 2024b; Yao et al., 2024), applications and databases (Trivedi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025), and software engineering (Jimenez et al., 2024). Joint methods alternate harness and policy updates (Kim et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026c), evolve matched pairs (Chen et al., 2026a), or learn runtime coordination (Ning et al., 2026). Model routing (Chen et al., 2023; Ong et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024) and response ensembles (Jiang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a) provide related approaches to allocating execution 3resources. HarnessBandit schedules one harness per optimizer step using learnability and gradient-based cross-harness transfer-ability signals (Wei et al., 2026). HarnessX couples harness evolution with cross-harness GRPO over a shared replay buffer, grouping trajectories by task across harness and policy versions (Chen et al., 2026b). Harness-S instead asks whether specialized harnesses can selectively rescue failures of a base execution system and transfer the resulting capabilities into a single policy that remains effective under the original harness. Its allocation unit is a failed task under H0: only all-failure groups trigger fresh rollouts from the current policy across selected siblings in a fixed pool. Evaluation under H0 tests whether the resulting improvement survives removal of auxiliary execution support. 

## 2.3 Recursive Self-Improvement 

Recent surveys distinguish self-improvement by whether updates modify model parameters or the surrounding scaf-fold (Ren et al., 2026). Program-level approaches improve optimization code or agent logic that supports subse-quent improvements (Zelikman et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a). AIDE 2 applies this loop to research agents (Srikanth et al., 2026), while RRSI regularizes harness edits to favor transferable mechanisms (Xia et al., 2026). SpecForge refines behavioral specifications from execution feedback (Cheng et al., 2026); self-rewarding models and SEAL instead update parameters using self-generated rewards or adaptation data (Yuan et al., 2024; Zweiger et al., 2025). Harness-S uses a fixed sibling pool to rescue base-system failures and transfer the resulting behaviors into one policy; policy updates refresh the evidence used for later rescue allocation. Its self-improvement loop operates over policy parameters and experience selection, with a fixed optimizer, verifier, and training-time harness pool, rather than recursively redesigning the improvement algorithm. Appendix H further discusses agent RL training, knowledge transfer, harness editing, and reusable skills. 

# 3 Harness-S 

Harness-S uses selective rescue of H0 failures to transfer useful behaviors from sibling harnesses into a shared policy (Figure 2). Directional evolution builds a reusable pool of specialized variants; rescue training uses them to explore tasks that defeat the base harness. The Adaptive Harness Router selects variants using behavioral evidence that is refreshed as the policy learns, and can be reused at inference. 

## 3.1 Policy, Harness, and Task Outcome 

A policy and its harness jointly determine how an agent executes a task. For task x, the policy πθ generates a trajectory τ ∼ pθ,H (· | x) under harness H, with verifier-defined outcome Rx(τ ) ∈ { 0, 1}. The harness controls the context and execution rules through which the policy interacts with the environment. We define its utility for that policy and task as the expected success rate, 

uθ (H, x ) = Eτ ∼pθ,H (·| x)[Rx(τ )] . (1) The Sibling Harness Pool H = {H0, H 1, . . . , H M } contains variants descended from a common base H0. All variants share the execution foundation, environment interfaces, and task verifier, and use the same policy parameters. Their relative utility varies across tasks and policy checkpoints, motivating task-specific selection that adapts as the policy learns.

## 4.2 Overall Performance (RQ1) 

Harness-S outperforms policy-only training, frozen-policy harness optimization, and joint adaptation under both H0 and Hrank1 (Table 1). The routed 4B policy exceeds 9B GRPO with H0 rescue on every reported metric; the routed 9B policy also exceeds the frontier-model references on DBBench. 

The gains combine retained policy improvement with better execution support. Harness-S outperforms plain GRPO and GRPO with H0 rescue across all benchmark–capacity settings under H0, indicating a benefit beyond additional sampling. With the policy frozen, Harness-S (HE only) outperforms Meta-Harness, supporting the value of the sibling pool and task-specific selection. Routing the trained policy further improves both metrics in every setting without increasing the executor rollout count. On AppWorld 4B, Harness-S outperforms H0 rescue with fewer rollouts and less training GPU time (Appendix G). Its full-pipeline GPU hours and external-model token use fall within the range of the joint-adaptation baselines. 

## 4.3 Learning Mechanism (RQ2) 

Selective rescue supplies more within-task reward contrast than additional sampling or fixed harness mixtures. Figure 3 compares Qwen3.5-4B on AppWorld under GRPO, H0 rescue, the two fixed mixtures, and Harness-S. The Effective Group (EG) rate measures the fraction of retained groups containing both successes and failures. Harness-S leads in every stage, reaching 79.38% over the full run versus 70.94% for GRPO with H0 rescue and 64.69% for standard GRPO. All-failure groups provide no within-task reward contrast; recovering some of these tasks supplies alternatives that GRPO can reinforce. Both fixed mixtures remain close to standard GRPO, suggesting that harness diversity is more useful when directed toward tasks on which the base group fails. 

Effective groups also account for a larger share of the retained training tokens. ## 4.4 Rescue Strategies (RQ3) 

Task-specific allocation and profile refresh improve rescue at a fixed rollout budget (AppWorld, Qwen3.5-4B; Table 2). Random Harness samples pool members, while Rule Router (without Profiles) ranks them by success on similar validation tasks. LLM Router ( 4 × 2) allocates two rollouts to each of four selected harnesses; Top-8 allocates one to each of eight, keeping the rescue rollout budget fixed. The fixed-profile Top-8 variant keeps its profiles unchanged, while Harness-S re-anchors them during training. Rescue success measures the fraction of rescue-triggering tasks with at least one successful rollout (Appendix C). 

Task-specific selection improves rescue success across training stages. Both rule-based and LLM-guided strate-gies outperform random selection throughout training. Top-8 Adaptive also exceeds LLM Router ( 4 × 2), suggesting that broader harness exploration recovers more failures than repeated sampling from fewer harnesses. Under the same final AppWorld policy, individual siblings repair 11–15 base-harness failures, while the nine analyzed siblings repair 29 in union (Appendix E). This is coverage obtained by executing each sibling once; routing must select candi-dates before observing their outcomes. 

Adaptive profiles improve routing over a fixed-profile Top-8 variant. With the rollout allocation held at one attempt per selected harness, re-anchoring yields higher rescue success in all four stages and improves final Avg@3 by 4.71 percentage points. This comparison supports updating the router’s behavioral evidence as the policy learns. 

All evaluated rescue strategies improve final test performance over GRPO, with Top-8 Adaptive achieving the highest Pass@3 and Avg@3. The remaining strategies show a tradeoff: Rule Router achieves higher Pass@3 than LLM Router ( 4 × 2), but lower Avg@3. ## 4.5 Policy Behavior (RQ4) 

Harness-S changes how the policy gathers evidence and completes actions under H0. Table 3 compares concrete workflows of the base, GRPO, and Harness-S policies. On AppWorld, Harness-S consults API schemas before first use more often than GRPO and repeats execute code less often, while also increasing pagination, multi-call execution, and read-back after writes. These shifts suggest greater use of interface information and state verification, rather than a uniform increase in tool activity. The frequencies describe behavior, not correctness: more calls or checks are useful only when they serve the task. 

The SWE-Gym trajectories show more explicit verification and recovery from blocked tests. Harness-S more often writes assertion checks, revisits a failed target after editing, and inspects the final diff. When tests are blocked, switching to assertions occurs in 48.5% of Harness-S cases versus 13.3% for GRPO. These patterns describe alternative ways to validate a patch when the standard test workflow is unavailable. 

Successful rescue behaviors also reappear on untrained instances after auxiliary support is removed. Ta-ble 4 traces two AppWorld cases from successful training rescues to held-out execution under H0. The trained policy preserves a set-intersection constraint and completes a feedback-dependent account-recovery workflow where GRPO fails. The first case concerns applying the same selection rule to new task instances; the second carries a recovery procedure from Venmo to Amazon, including password reset and verification. They illustrate transfer of both a task constraint and a multi-step procedure across distinct executions. Both successful rescues entered a policy update, and the corresponding behavior later appears on a different instance under H0. These cases connect auxiliary execution experience to behavior retained by the policy after the specialized support is removed.

Harness-S retains its performance advantage under external execution harnesses without further policy updates. # 5 Conclusion and Future Directions 

Harness-S trains a shared policy to retain capabilities elicited by complementary sibling harnesses under the original 

H0. Directional evolution preserves distinct repair strategies, while selective rescue recovers learning signals from base-harness failures. Across AppWorld, SWE-Gym, and DBBench, gains persist under H0; behavioral analyses sup-port transfer, and Rank-1 routing improves inference. Future work will study lasting transfer and efficient pool reuse across policies. We will study how independently developed harnesses exchange task state across execution inter-faces. The adaptations start from the size-matched Qwen3.5 policy and common base harness. Environment interfaces and task verifiers remain unchanged; each method retains its own search organization and policy-update objective. Hbest

Evaluation protocol. Within each run, each test task receives three executor rollouts. Pass@3 measures the fraction of tasks with at least one successful rollout, while Avg@3 averages success over the three attempts. Main-table scores are means over three independent randomized runs; the reported Harness-S standard deviations are computed across runs. Plain GRPO retains each initial group; the rescue control replaces an all-failure group with fresh H0 rollouts using the same per-task allocation as Harness-S. Total sampling depends on rescue frequency, which differs between policies, and includes both discarded base attempts and rescue rollouts. 

Rescue allocation variants. All rescue groups contain eight rollouts. Random Harness samples pool members. Rule Router ranks candidates by success on similar validation tasks, without using profiles. LLM Router ( 4 × 2)allocates two rollouts to each of four selected harnesses; Top-8 allocates one to each of eight. The fixed-profile Top-8 variant keeps its profiles unchanged, while Harness-S refreshes them at calibration checkpoints. Rescue success is the fraction of rescue-triggering tasks for which at least one fresh attempt succeeds. 

Fixed mixed-harness GRPO. The Claude Code mixture allocates four rollouts to DSH H0 and four to Claude Code. The Top-4 mixture combines DSH H0 with four selected siblings throughout training. The same mixture is used across training tasks, without waiting for an all-failure base group. These controls expose one shared policy to diverse harness contexts throughout training. Only policy parameters are updated, and both policies are evaluated under H0.

Meta-Harness. With the policy frozen, the proposer uses previous implementations, scores, and trajectories to revise harness code (Lee et al., 2026). The adaptation searches within the common execution framework, preserving environment interfaces and the task verifier. Candidates are checked for compatibility and evaluated on training tasks. A single selected Hbest is used across test tasks. 

WHALE. The adaptation alternates Meta-Harness-style search with rejection-sampling fine-tuning on successful tra-jectories (Kim et al., 2026). Successful trajectories supply the policy update, after which harness search proceeds with the updated policy. This preserves the alternation between model adaptation and execution-support optimization. Final evaluation uses the selected policy and its fixed Hbest .

HarnessForge. Failure analysis guides harness changes, and successful search trajectories train the corresponding branch policy (Chen et al., 2026a). Branches retain separate policy adaptations and the provenance of their training trajectories. As recorded in Table 10, training reuses successful search trajectories, so zero additional training rollouts does not mean that the policy remains frozen. The final selected pair executes with a fixed Hbest .

Pool and evaluation. The pool contains H0 and 19 auxiliary harnesses; rescue selects eight auxiliaries and inference ranks all 20. The HE-only variant freezes the starting policy; the two trained variants share a checkpoint and differ only in test-harness selection. The nine siblings analyzed in Appendix E define the scope of that calibration analysis, not the size of the routing pool. 

Profile refresh. For AppWorld 4B, profiles are built before training, refreshed every ten updates, and re-anchored with the final policy. Each calibration freezes the current policy for paired validation execution. Routing uses the latest profiles between checkpoints, with all harness implementations fixed. 

Inference voting. Multiple LLMs independently rank candidates using the task, current profiles, validation success rates, and outcomes on similar validation tasks. Each ballot ranks four distinct candidates with weights 1, 3/4, 1/2,and 1/4. Unlisted candidates receive zero weight. Weights are summed across ballots; ties use validation success, then harness ID. Candidate order varies across calls. The base harness uses its execution description and validation evidence. Each selected harness is fixed across the three evaluation rollouts for its task. The ranking is completed before any task outcome is observed, and failed attempts do not trigger a new inference-time selection.

# H Extended Related Work 

## H.1 Agent RL Training 

Interactive environments make action selection and observation handling part of the training problem. Toolformer learns API use through self-supervised examples, while AgentTuning initializes agent capabilities through instruc-tion tuning on interaction trajectories (Schick et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2023). Such supervision can initialize tool-use behavior before reinforcement learning from task outcomes. WebGPT combines browsing demonstrations with human-preference feedback (Nakano et al., 2022). ReTool learns to interleave reasoning with code execution, while Search-R1 learns multi-turn search interactions and masks retrieved content during optimization (Feng et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025). SWE-RL develops software-engineering reasoning from software evolution data with rule-based so-lution rewards (Wei et al., 2025). The supervision source and action interface jointly determine which behaviors can be learned. Long interactions introduce credit-assignment and rollout-quality problems beyond optimizing individual responses. RAGEN identifies instability associated with collapsing reward variation and studies how rollout diversity and feed-back shape multi-turn learning (Wang et al., 2025). LOOP trains agents directly in stateful environments such as AppWorld using a memory-efficient PPO variant (Chen et al., 2025). AgentGym-RL explores a complementary cur-riculum, gradually increasing interaction horizons to balance early exploitation with broader exploration (Xi et al., 2025). Agent Lightning separates execution from optimization and decomposes trajectories into transitions with assigned credit (Luo et al., 2025). These directions distinguish the learning objective, interaction curriculum, and runtime interface through which training experience is collected. Classical RL offers additional ways to learn when successful outcomes are rare or delayed. Hindsight Experience Replay reuses unsuccessful attempts by relabeling them with goals that were achieved (Andrychowicz et al., 2018). RUDDER redistributes delayed rewards through return decomposition (Arjona-Medina et al., 2019), whereas Go-Explore separates returning to promising states from exploring beyond them (Ecoffet et al., 2021). These methods intervene in goal assignment, credit distribution, and state coverage, respectively. The distinction matters for lan-guage agents: changing how experience is generated and changing what an existing trajectory teaches are different interventions. Uniformly rewarded groups limit the signal available to group-relative updates. PPO uses a surrogate objective to control policy updates, while GRPO estimates advantages from rewards within a sampled group (Schulman et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024). DAPO uses dynamic sampling to retain groups with reward variation, filtering groups whose samples are all correct or all incorrect (Yu et al., 2025). Model-aware tool-use data evolution in LoopTool and hint-conditioned generative recommendation in HCGRec address related data and exploration bottlenecks (Zhang et al., 2026d,b). Harness-S changes execution support before generating fresh attempts on the same failed task. It keeps the task and verifier fixed while seeking the successful alternatives needed for policy updates. 

## H.2 Knowledge Transfer 

The form of supervision determines what a student can inherit from a teacher. Classical knowledge distillation trans-fers predictive distributions (Hinton et al., 2015), while Sequence-Level Knowledge Distillation trains autoregressive 22 students with teacher-generated sequences (Kim and Rush, 2016). These approaches transfer information at different granularities: relations among candidate outputs versus complete generated solutions. The distinction is relevant when successful behavior consists of a sequence of decisions rather than a single prediction. Multiple specialists can also be consolidated into a shared decision-making model. Policy Distillation combines task-specific reinforcement-learning policies into a single network (Rusu et al., 2016). Kickstarting combines guidance from previously trained agents with continued reinforcement learning, allowing a student to benefit from several experts and improve beyond them (Schmitt et al., 2018). The resulting learner can act without querying every original expert, separating the resources used to acquire a capability from those required to deploy it. Additional computation and reasoning traces provide another source of transferable knowledge. Expert Iteration alternates tree-search planning with training a policy to generalize the resulting decisions (Anthony et al., 2017). STaR trains on successful self-generated rationales, including rationales regenerated with access to the correct answer after failure (Zelikman et al., 2022). Distilling Step-by-Step uses teacher-generated rationales as additional supervision alongside task labels (Hsieh et al., 2023). These approaches differ in how they obtain useful traces, but all use richer training-time computation to shape a model’s subsequent behavior. Transfer must also account for the distribution induced by the learner. DAgger aggregates expert-labeled states visited by successive learner policies to address distribution shift in sequential imitation (Ross et al., 2011). Generalized Knowledge Distillation similarly obtains teacher feedback on student-generated sequences (Agarwal et al., 2024). MiniLLM studies on-policy optimization of reverse KL divergence to limit student probability mass on outputs that the teacher assigns low likelihood (Gu et al., 2026). Together, these works show why the training distribution and the matching objective are separate design choices. Teachers may additionally have information that the deployed learner will not receive. Generalized distillation con-nects this setting to learning with privileged information (Lopez-Paz et al., 2016); Learning by Cheating applies it to driving by training a vision-based policy from an agent with privileged environmental state (Chen et al., 2019). VAD examines the source of multimodal teacher feedback through controlled visual-evidence interventions (Zhang et al., 2026c). Harness-S instead obtains behavioral diversity by executing the same current policy under different harness contexts. Task outcomes supervise its trajectories, and evaluation under H0 tests whether useful behaviors persist when auxiliary execution support is removed. 

## H.3 Harness Editing and Reusable Skills 

The execution interface determines how a language model’s outputs become actions. ReAct interleaves reasoning and environment interaction (Yao et al., 2023b); SWE-agent and CodeAct study dedicated computer interfaces and executable code actions, respectively (Yang et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2024b). AutoGen extends the design space to configurable conversations among agents, tools, and human participants (Wu et al., 2023). These systems establish different control structures and action representations that later optimization procedures can modify. Execution support can be optimized at several levels of granularity. OPRO uses evaluated solutions to guide new prompt proposals (Yang et al., 2024a). EvoPrompt applies evolutionary operators to prompt populations, while Promptbreeder also evolves the mutation prompts that generate candidates (Guo et al., 2025; Fernando et al., 2023). DSPy compiles modular language-model pipelines against task metrics (Khattab et al., 2023). TextGrad propagates textual feedback through computation graphs, and AFlow searches code-represented workflows with execution feed-back (Yuksekgonul et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). AgentOptimizer treats an agent’s functions as adjustable parame-ters while keeping the underlying language model fixed (Zhang et al., 2024). Thus, prompt editing, workflow search, and function revision expose different parts of an execution system to optimization. Maintaining an archive supports exploration of multiple improvement directions. ADAS searches code-defined agents using previous discoveries, and the Darwin Gödel Machine branches from an archive of executable agents whose mod-ifications are evaluated empirically (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a). GEPA combines trajectory-based reflection with complementary prompt candidates on a Pareto frontier (Agrawal et al., 2026). Meta-Harness gives its proposer access to earlier implementations, scores, and execution traces (Lee et al., 2026). Harness-R1 makes the editing policy itself learnable from the outcomes of its runtime changes (Shao et al., 2026). Archive design and proposal learning therefore address distinct parts of search: retaining alternative behaviors and improving how candidates are gener-ated. 23 Reusable tools and skills reduce the need to rediscover useful procedures. LLMs As Tool Makers separates a model that constructs reusable tools from one that applies them, allowing construction costs to be shared across later re-quests (Cai et al., 2024). Voyager accumulates executable skills through interaction, while Agent Workflow Memory induces reusable routines from past experience (Wang et al., 2023, 2024c). The reusable object can consequently range from a callable function to a workflow supplied as context, with different requirements for retrieval and execution. Natural-language memory offers another way to preserve experience without immediately updating model weights. Reflexion stores linguistic feedback for subsequent trials, ExpeL extracts insights from past tasks, and ACE incre-mentally curates strategies in context playbooks (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026f). MMSkills extends reusable packages to multimodal evidence; SkillRise and SkillGate learn skill curation and selection, respec-tively (Zhang et al., 2026e; Yao et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). Representing a procedure, deciding when to retrieve it, and retaining its benefits in policy parameters remain separate questions. Execution resources can evolve jointly with the policy or be allocated during its training. Co-Harness and WHALE alternate harness and model optimization, while HarnessForge evolves coupled harness–policy systems (Chen et al., 2026c; Kim et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a). HarnessBandit instead schedules one harness per optimizer step using learnability and cross-harness transferability signals (Wei et al., 2026). Harness-S allocates a fixed sibling pool at the task level, invoking auxiliary harnesses after all initial attempts under H0 fail. Policy-aware profiles track which variants remain useful, while rescue trajectories update one shared policy. This links harness search to policy learning through complementary exploration, with base-harness evaluation separating retained policy gains from additional inference-time support. 24
