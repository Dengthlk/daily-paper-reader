Density-Aware Reward Scaling: A Reinforcement Learning Framework for
Reward-Proportional Sampling
Ria Shekhawat∗, Abhijeet Sinha∗, Yingtao Zhu, Dianbo Liu
National University of Singapore
Abstract
Reinforcement learning objectives that maximize expected
return can concentrate probability mass on a small subset
of high-reward outcomes, limiting diversity when multiple
distinct solutions are desirable. We introduce Density-Aware
Reward Scaling (DARS), a simple reward-processing mecha-
nism that promotes reward-proportional sampling in continu-
ous outcome spaces while retaining standard policy-gradient
training. DARS estimates the local density of outcomes within
each on-policy rollout group and inversely scales rewards by
this density. The method requires no learned density model,
auxiliary objective, critic, or modification to the underlying
policy-gradient objective. In a controlled multimodal navi-
gation task, DARS-GRPO avoids the single-mode collapse
exhibited by GRPO and closely recovers the target mode allo-
cation. Applied to text-to-image prompt adaptation, DARS-
GRPO consistently improves image and prompt diversity
across datasets, Policy LMs, and reward settings while main-
taining good semantic alignment. These results show that
density-aware reward scaling provides a lightweight way to
improve coverage of diverse high-reward outcomes within ex-
isting reinforcement-learning pipelines.
1
Introduction
Reinforcement learning is typically formulated to maximize
expected return, which can drive outcome-level probability
mass toward a small subset of high-reward outcomes (GX-
Chen et al. 2025). While desirable for conventional control
problems, this behavior is limiting in domains where multiple
distinct outcomes are simultaneously valuable. In generative
modeling, scientific discovery, and structured prediction, the
objective is often not merely to identify a high-reward solu-
tion, but to learn a policy that maintains broad coverage over
the high-reward outcome space.
Several approaches have been proposed to promote
such diversity. Entropy-regularized RL encourages stochas-
tic action selection, but action-level stochasticity does not
in general specify a desired distribution over terminal
outcomes (Haarnoja et al. 2018). Generative Flow Net-
works (GFlowNets) instead target reward-proportional sam-
pling, providing a principled framework for generating di-
verse high-reward samples (Bengio et al. 2023). Applying
∗Equal Contribution
GFlowNet-style objectives to autoregressive pretrained mod-
els generally requires formulating generation through flow-
or trajectory-based objectives rather than retaining an un-
changed policy-gradient training pipeline (Yun et al. 2025).
Inverse Probability Scaling (IPS) offers another route by in-
versely scaling reward according to terminal-outcome vis-
itation probability (Sinha, Elango, and Liu 2026). In con-
tinuous or high-dimensional outcome spaces, however, ex-
act repeated outcomes may be rare or may not provide a
meaningful notion of semantic equivalence. We introduce
Density-Aware Reward Scaling (DARS), a simple framework
for encouraging a policy that has reward-proportional behav-
ior within standard policy-gradient reinforcement learning.
DARS is motivated by the limitations of IPS and replaces dis-
crete visitation frequency with an estimate of local outcome
density in continuous outcome spaces. For each on-policy
rollout group, outcomes that lie in densely populated regions
receive proportionally reduced rewards, while high-reward
outcomes in underrepresented regions receive greater rela-
tive rewards. This density scaling is applied before advantage
estimation and requires no modification to the underlying
policy-gradient objective. Importantly, DARS introduces no
learned density model, critic, auxiliary loss, or specialized
policy architecture; instead it simply modifies reward pro-
cessing within the existing RL pipeline.
Our central contribution is a simple mechanism for miti-
gating outcome collapse and promoting coverage of diverse,
high-reward regions within standard policy-gradient training.
We validate DARS through controlled synthetic experiments
that establish its ability to approximate reward-proportional
sampling, followed by text-to-image prompt adaptation ex-
periments demonstrating improved diversity while preserv-
ing generation quality, and kernel-width ablations that pro-
vide mechanistic evidence for its density-aware reward scal-
ing.

Related Work
Mode collapse in RL and loss of diversity: Mode col-
lapse is widely associated with generative models that rep-
resent only a small subset of a multimodal data distribution.
An analogous failure occurs in reinforcement learning at
the level of terminal outcomes: even when multiple trajec-
tories or solutions receive similar rewards, expected-return
optimization concentrates the learned policy on a narrow
arXiv:2611.04792v1  [cs.LG]  3 Nov 2026


subset of them, reducing the diversity of generated solu-
tions in language and image generation (Kirk et al. 2024;
Chen et al. 2026). Existing approaches mitigate this be-
havior through entropy regularization, KL constraints, re-
ward shaping, or diversity-aware objectives (Schulman et al.
2017; Liu et al. 2026), yet they do not directly control the
distribution of terminal outcomes. Recent work instead at-
tributes outcome collapse to expected-return optimization
itself, where an outcome’s aggregate learning signal is am-
plified by its current sampling probability, creating a rich-get-
richer feedback loop (Sinha, Elango, and Liu 2026). Reward-
proportional methods address this issue by sampling out-
comes in proportion to their rewards: GFlowNets achieve this
through specialized flow-conservation objectives and have
been extended to multi-objective optimization, continuous
state spaces, prompt adaptation, and language-model reason-
ing (Bengio et al. 2021, 2023; Jain et al. 2023; Lahlou et al.
2023; Zhu et al. 2026), while IPS instead rescales rewards us-
ing inverse visitation probabilities estimated from empirical
outcome frequencies (Sinha, Elango, and Liu 2026). How-
ever, empirical visitation frequencies become ineffective in
continuous outcome spaces, where exact outcome repetitions
are exceedingly rare. Concurrent work (Chen et al. 2026),
also adjusts GRPO rewards using within-group semantic re-
dundancy for mathematical reasoning in LLMs. In contrast,
we study density-aware reward scaling as a mechanism for
reward-proportional sampling in continuous outcome spaces
and directly evaluate recovery of the target reward distribu-
tion.
Prompt Adaptation and Image Generation: Loss of
output diversity has become increasingly visible in rein-
forcement learning from human feedback and other forms
of large-language-model post-training (Ouyang et al. 2022;
Kirk et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2026). Reward optimization can
favor a narrow set of response styles or semantic patterns even
when many responses are valid. In practice, this behavior
is often moderated with KL regularization, reward mixing,
early stopping, or stochastic decoding. Prompt-optimization
methods learn rewrites that improve text-to-image quality or
alignment (Hao et al. 2023), while PAG learns diverse ef-
fective prompts with a GFlowNet formulation over prompt-
generation trajectories (Yun et al. 2025). CADS acts at dif-
fusion inference time by annealing the conditioning signal
during sampling.

Density-Aware Reward Scaling (DARS)
IPS shows that reward-proportional samplingcan beachieved
by inversely scaling rewards according to outcome probabil-
ities under the current policy (Sinha, Elango, and Liu 2026),
but estimating these probabilities through exact visitation
frequencies does not extend naturally to continuous outcome
spaces, where repeated outcomes are unlikely. DARS instead
represents outcomes in a continuous feature space where
distances capture meaningful similarity between outcomes.
This representation allows visitation to be defined over local
regions rather than exact outcome identities. DARS estimates
the local density of each sampled outcome within its on-
policy rollout group and inversely scales its reward by this
density, reducing reinforcement of densely sampled regions
while relatively favoring underrepresented high-reward re-
gions.

3.3
Behavior
At either kernel width extremes, as h →0, ρi ≈1 and the
rewards remain unchanged; as h →∞, ρi ≈G and group-
relative advantage normalization cancels the shared reward
scale. Intermediate bandwidths instead produce non-uniform
reward scaling across the given rollout group, thereby mod-
ifying the resulting policy-gradient signal.
For d-dimensional outcome representations, pairwise ker-
nel evaluation costs O(G2d) time and O(G2) memory.
DARS introduces no learned density model, critic, or auxil-
iary optimization objective.

Navigation Task: Controlled
Distribution-Recovery Experiment
This experiment tests whether DARS can make a policy-
gradient RL method act as an approximate reward-
proportional sampler in a continuous outcome space. The
primary comparison is against GRPO: both methods use the
same policy, environment, and terminal reward, but only
DARS corrects for local outcome collapse. GFlowNet pro-
vides a reward-proportional reference with a different train-
ing objective. The experiment is a controlled empirical test
of whether DARS approaches the normalized reward distri-
bution.
Environment and Target: We evaluate how closely a
policy approaches a known normalized reward density on the
unit square X = [0, 1]2. Each episode starts at s0 = (0, 0).
At state st, the policy either terminates or takes a nonnegative
displacement satisfying
0 ≤at,i ≤min(δ, 1 −st,i),
i ∈{1, 2},
with deterministic transition st+1 = st + at. Trajectories
therefore move monotonically up and to the right. The step
bound δ makes distant terminal states require several deci-
sions to reach for the policy. The policy may stop at any
visited state except the origin, and it is forced to stop at the
boundary or when the rollout horizon is reached. Only the
terminal state receives rewards.
The movement policy uses polar coordinates. A neural
network predicts a Bernoulli stopping probability and sep-
arate mixtures of Beta distributions for a normalized angle
and radius. The angle is mapped to θt ∈[0, π/2], and the
radius is rescaled by both δ and the distance to the boundary.
The resulting action is
at = (rt cos θt, rt sin θt).
This guarantees valid first-quadrant movements and lets the
policy jointly learn where to move, how far to move, and
when to terminate. Since the radial parameterization bounds
the Euclidean step length, it imposes the slightly stricter
constraint ∥at∥2 ≤δ.
The reward is an equally weighted mixture of four isotropic
Gaussians, as shown in Figure 1. Each component has stan-
dard deviation σ = 0.075.

4.2
Results and Interpretation
The primary result is the comparison with GRPO. In the
reported run, GRPO concentrates on a single high-reward
mode, whereas DARS-GRPO assigns substantial mass to
all four modes and approaches their target weights. The
DARS-GRPO point estimates are closer to the target than
GRPO across the metrics reported in Table 1. GRPO’s higher
mean raw reward reflects tight concentration near one of four
equally high peaks, rather than agreement with the normal-
ized reward distribution. Thus, within this controlled envi-
ronment, the reported run provides evidence that density
reweighting prevents the evaluated policy from concentrat-
ing on one mode, recovers the correct mode weights, and
improves global geometric agreement with the known target
relative to GRPO. This is the central controlled contribution
of the experiment.
DARS-GRPO closely matches, and on several key met-
rics surpasses, GFlowNet despite requiring only a sim-
ple reward-scaling modification within a standard policy-
gradient pipeline. Both methods achieve identical precision,
indicating equally accurate recovery of high-reward out-
comes. DARS consistently improves metrics that capture the
global reward distribution, including sliced Wasserstein, en-
ergy distance, mode total variation, minimum mode mass,
hierarchical Wasserstein error, HDR outside mass, and most
larger-bandwidth MMD and Sinkhorn metrics, demonstrat-
ing more faithful recovery of target mode proportions and
global geometry.
GFlowNet performs slightly better on metrics emphasiz-
ing fine-scale local density, such as Jensen–Shannon di-
vergence, histogram total variation, within-mode Wasser-
stein error, recall, and small-bandwidth MMD, reflecting its
specialized flow-based objective. Nevertheless, these results
show that a lightweight modification to conventional policy-
gradient RL is sufficient to achieve reward-proportional be-
havior that is competitive with a substantially more sophisti-
cated algorithmic framework.
Having established in a controlled setting that DARS
approximates reward-proportional sampling, we next ask
whether this property is useful in a real continuous-output
RL problem where the target reward distribution is unknown.
5
Text-to-Image Prompt Adaptation
5.1
Motivation
Prompt adaptation for image generation refers to the set-
ting in which a language model rewrites the input prompt
to match a desired set of outcomes before image gener-
ation. Text-to-image prompt adaptation provides a natural
real-world benchmark for evaluating reward-proportional re-
inforcement learning. Unlike the synthetic experiments in
Section 4, where the target reward distribution is explicitly
known, the reward-proportional distribution over generated
images is intractable and cannot be directly characterized. A
single text prompt typically admits many semantically faith-
ful realizations that differ in composition, viewpoint, light-
ing, object arrangement, or artistic style while remaining
equally valid.
Since the underlying reward distribution is unavailable,
directly measuring reward-proportional sampling is infeasi-
ble. Instead, we evaluate its consequences through observ-
able proxies. If DARS successfully distributes probability
mass across multiple high-reward regions, it should produce
a more diverse set of images while preserving prompt align-
ment and image quality. We therefore measure visual diver-
sity together with alignment and preference metrics as an
indirect estimate of improved coverage of the latent reward
landscape. Consequently, the objective is not merely to iden-
tify a single high-reward image, but to maintain coverage
over the diverse set of high-quality outcomes consistent with
the prompt.
Since input prompts are the conditioning signal in diffu-
sion models, semantically faithful prompt rewrites help steer
the generation in desired directions. Learning a distribution
over such rewrites therefore provides an effective mecha-
nism for exploring multiple high-reward image modes while
keeping the underlying image generator fixed.
Following this formulation, we fine-tune a language model
using reinforcement learning to rewrite input prompts. Im-
ages generated from the rewritten prompts are rewarded as
defined in section 5.2. Since multiple visually distinct images

5.4
Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate each prompt-rewriting policy along three com-
plementary dimensions: image diversity, rewrite diversity,
and image fidelity or preference. Image diversity is measured
in DINOv2 CLS representation space using the Vendi Score
(Friedman and Dieng 2023) of the cosine-similarity matrix
of L2-normalized CLS embeddings, and the mean pairwise
Euclidean distance between CLS embeddings. Rewrite diver-
sity is measured analogously in CLIP text-embedding space
using Prompt Vendi and the mean pairwise cosine distance
among rewritten prompts. Finally, CLIP-align, HPSv2 (Wu
et al. 2023), and PickScore (Kirstain et al. 2023) assess the
semantic compatibility and human-preference-style quality
of each generated image relative to the input prompt. CLIP-
align is the nonnegative cosine similarity between the L2-
normalized CLIP embeddings of a generated image and its
original prompt. Diversity metrics are computed within each
group of rewrites or images generated for the same original
prompt and then averaged across the evaluation set, follow-
ing within-prompt conditional evaluation. For all metrics,
higher values indicate greater diversity or stronger fidelity
and preference, respectively.
5.5
Experiment Details
We use a frozen Stable Diffusion v1.4 generator (Rombach
et al. 2022) and optimize only the prompt-rewriting pol-
icy. For all baselines and reward functions, both Qwen3-
0.6B(base) (Yang et al. 2025) and GPT-2 (124M) (Radford
et al. 2019) are already supervised fine-tuned on a shared set
of curated prompt rewrites. We then train the models on 1,024
randomly sampled MS-COCO prompts (Lin et al. 2014) and
evaluate them on disjoint MS-COCO and Lexica prompts
(Shen et al. 2023). DARS uses h = 20 for Gaussian kernels,
and the alignment reward uses a CLIP-similarity threshold
of 0.2.
We compare DARS-GRPO with vanilla GRPO, PAG, and
GFlowNet prompt optimization, together with Stable Diffu-
sion v1.4 with random seed initialization and CADS (Sadat
et al. 2024) as control baselines. All methods are evaluated
using the same held-out prompts, generation budget, diffu-
sion model, and sampling procedure. Diffusion seeds are
sampled independently across methods, so image-diversity
metrics also capture variation introduced by stochastic diffu-
sion sampling while prompt-diversity metrics are unaffected
by this confound.
5.6
Results and Interpretation
Across both datasets, Policy LMs, and reward settings,
DARS-GRPO consistently improves upon vanilla GRPO
across all three diversity metrics (Table 2) without significant
loss of image fidelity. Further, among all prompt-adaptation
methods, it achieves the highest DINO pairwise distances
and Image Vendi scores, indicating that density-aware re-
ward scaling broadens the learned rewrite distribution and
increases coverage in DINOv2 image-representation space.
The high Prompt Vendi and diversity scores further indicate
that this image diversity arises from diverse prompt rewrites,
while CLIP-align, HPSv2, and PickScore imply close image
fidelity.
Changing the reward function affects performance across
all metrics, showing that DARS responds to the reward land-
scape rather than producing diversity gains randomly. Con-
sistent with our hypothesis, the binary gated alignment-only
reward yields broader sampling from uniformly scored re-
ward zones, whereas the combined reward forces the policy
to learn a more dynamic reward landscape characterized by
aesthetically-driven peaks, thus, sampling from a more varied
distribution as reflected by the higher average Image Vendi
scores.

6
Ablation Study
As discussed in 3.3, kernel width h affects reward-scaling in
DARS and hence needs to be calibrated for accurate density
estimation. For the experiments in Section 5, we demonstrate
the direct effects of varying kernel widths on image-diversity
produced by DARS (Table 3). Diversity metrics consistently
peak at intermediate kernel widths and degrade when the
bandwidth is either too small or too large, while image qual-
ity remains largely unchanged. This behavior reflects how
DARS estimates local outcome density. With a very small
kernel width, nearly every generated image appears isolated,
causing density estimates to be dominated by self-similarity
and resulting in almost uniform reward scaling. Conversely,
an excessively large kernel width treats distant images as
belonging to the same neighborhood, oversmoothing density
estimates and diminishing the relative reward differences that
encourage exploration. Intermediate kernel widths instead re-
solve meaningful local neighborhoods allowing for effective
density estimation in DARS.
7
Limitations
DARS relies on a meaningful representation space and ker-
nel bandwidth for estimating local outcome density. Its ef-
fectiveness may therefore degrade when embedding dis-
tances poorly reflect semantic similarity or when no sin-
gle bandwidth captures the relevant neighborhood structure.
The pairwise density computation also scales quadratically
with rollout-group size, potentially limiting its use with large
groups. Our experiments focus on a controlled navigation
task and text-to-image prompt adaptation with fixed image

8
Conclusion
We introduced Density-Aware Reward Scaling (DARS), a
lightweight mechanism for promoting diverse, high-reward
outcomes within standard policy-gradient training. By in-
versely scaling rewards using local outcome density, DARS
counteracts repeated reinforcement of densely sampled re-
gions without requiring a critic, auxiliary objective, learned
density model, or modified policy-gradient loss. In a con-
trolled multimodal navigation task, DARS-GRPO prevents
the mode collapse observed with GRPO and closely recovers
target mode allocation. In text-to-image prompt adaptation,
it consistently increases prompt and image diversity across
datasets, Policy LMs, and reward settings while retaining se-
mantic fidelity. These results position density-aware reward
scaling as a simple yet effective approach to improving out-
come coverage.

Reward choice: Combined vs Alignment-only
Figures 9–12 compare the two reward functions using the
same Qwen3-0.6B policy LM. The combined reward com-
bines semantic fidelity with improvement under the frozen
aesthetic scorer, whereas the alignment-only reward applies
only the minimum CLIP-alignment constraint. Each embed-
ded grid compares GRPO and DARS under one reward, al-
lowing the effect of reward design to be separated from the
effect of density scaling.
The alignment-only objective permits DARS to explore
a substantially broader visual region since it is free of aes-
thetic bounds that affect the combined reward. Across the
examples, it produces larger changes in setting, illumination,
camera distance, and composition. This is particularly visi-

range of architectural and natural backgrounds. The quanti-
tative results show the same pattern with higher Image Vendi
Scores and DINO pairwise distance for alignment-only re-
wards.

Implementation Details
Experimental Configuration
Unless otherwise specified,
all experiments use the same training, generation, and eval-
uation settings. Table 4 summarizes the policy optimization
hyperparameters shared by DARS and the GRPO baseline,
while Table 5 lists the hyperparameters specific to density-
aware reward scaling. The generation pipeline, reward formu-
lation, and datasets are held fixed across experiments unless
explicitly varied in an ablation study.
DARS and GRPO are trained under identical optimiza-
tion settings to ensure a fair comparison. Both methods use
the same policy LM, optimizer hyperparameters, decoding
configuration, and rollout schedule. They differ only in the
reward scaling mechanism. Unless otherwise stated, these
hyperparameters are used for all reported experiments.
Table 5 lists the hyperparameters specific to DARS. Den-
sity is estimated in the DINOv2 image embedding space
using a Gaussian kernel with bandwidth h = 20. To study
the effect of density estimation, all kernel-width ablations
retain the same training configuration while varying only the
kernel bandwidth.
Kernel-width ablations use the same experimental setup
while varying only the Gaussian kernel width,
h ∈{10, 15, 20, 25, 30},
with all other hyperparameters kept fixed.
Computing Environment
All experiments were con-
ducted on a server equipped with four NVIDIA A100-SXM4
GPUs using the software environment summarized in Ta-
ble 6. Mixed-precision training uses bf16, while Stable
Diffusion inference is performed in fp16 to improve com-
putational efficiency without affecting evaluation.
Training Configuration
Tables 4 and 5 summarize the
optimization hyperparameters and DARS-specific settings
used during training. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all
experiments employ these default configurations. DARS and
GRPO differ only in the computation of policy rewards, while
sharing identical optimization settings.
Generation Configuration
All generated images are pro-
duced using the Stable Diffusion v1.4 pipeline with identical
sampling settings across methods. Each rewritten prompt
is decoded independently using 20 diffusion steps with a
classifier-free guidance scale of 7.5. The same CLIP encoder,
DINOv2 encoder, and aesthetic predictor are used through-
out both training and evaluation to ensure consistency across
reported metrics.
Reward Configuration
Two reward variants are consid-
ered in this work. The primary configuration combines aes-
thetic improvement with CLIP-based semantic alignment,
while the alignment-only variant removes the aesthetic com-
ponent to isolate the contribution of density-aware reward
scaling. Apart from the reward definition, all experimental
settings remain unchanged.
Evaluation Protocol
Evaluation follows the same protocol
for all methods. For each evaluation prompt, the policy gen-
erates N = 64 rewritten prompts, each of which is used to
