## ReSI-Math: Exploring Recoverable Capability Frontiers for Efficient Reasoning Improvement

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become an effective approach for improving mathematical reasoning in large lan-guage models. However, a fundamental ques-tion remains: when a model fails to solve a problem, does it truly lack the underlying capability? In this work, we reveal that di-rect failure does not necessarily imply inabil-ity. Some reasoning failures can be recov-ered by exploring the model’s own trajectories, exposing a recoverable capability frontier : a policy-dependent region where solutions are not reliably generated through direct decod-ing but can be elicited through trajectory-level search. Based on this observation, we introduce 

ReSI-Math , a framework that explores recov-erable capability frontiers and converts recov-ered capabilities into data-efficient RLVR su-pervision. ReSI-Math uses Self-Judge-guided evolutionary search to identify recoverable fail-ures under a fixed policy and adaptively selects frontier problems for sequential RLVR as the policy evolves. On Qwen3-VL-8B, correct– incorrect trajectory crossover produces 272 verifier-confirmed correct offspring from 621 attempts (43.8%). After three sequential one-shot RLVR stages, the MATH500 and We-Math average improves from 78.71 to 80.16. Under matched single-problem RLVR, ran-dom selection reaches only 53.31 versus 54.16 for frontier-guided Q1. Project link: https: //cnu-bot-group.github.io/ReSI-Math/ .

1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become an effective approach to im-proving mathematical reasoning in large language models, using verifiable outcomes to optimize rea-soning policies (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). Despite these advances, interpreting a model’s fail-ures remains important for understanding and im-proving its reasoning. An incorrect response leaves open whether the model cannot produce a valid solution or whether the particular generation pro-cess failed to elicit one. This raises a fundamental question: when a model fails to solve a problem, does it truly lack the underlying capability? 

Evidence from inference-time reasoning sug-gests that a model’s observed performance depends on how its reasoning is elicited. Repeated sam-pling and self-refinement can produce successful solutions after unsuccessful attempts (Cobbe et al., 2021; Madaan et al., 2023), while CoTEvol shows how reasoning trajectories can be evolved through crossover and mutation (Wang et al., 2026). These findings motivate examining recoverability under fixed model parameters: whether structured explo-ration of the current model’s own trajectories can produce a verifier-confirmed solution when direct generation remains unreliable. This perspective makes the conditions under which reasoning suc-ceeds part of the object of study. We characterize a recoverable capability fron-tier as a policy-dependent set of problems whose solutions are not reliably generated through di-rect decoding but can be elicited through bounded trajectory-level search. Direct sampling describes how consistently a problem is solved, while search probes whether a correct solution can be obtained by examining and recombining generated trajecto-ries. Throughout this exploration, the model param-eters remain fixed and an external verifier checks the resulting answers. The frontier is thus defined operationally with respect to the current policy and a specified search procedure and budget. Recoverability also provides a possible connec-tion between capability exploration and subsequent learning. A problem whose solution can be elicited through search, but is not yet reliably generated directly, may offer a useful target for policy opti-mization. This possibility is particularly relevant to data-efficient RLVR. These results mo-tivate investigating whether search-confirmed re-coverability can help identify problems from which the current policy can learn effectively. Based on this perspective, we introduce ReSI-Math , a framework that explores recoverable ca-pability frontiers and uses the resulting signals for reasoning improvement. Under a fixed policy, the model first samples multiple reasoning trajectories and then acts as a Self-Judge to analyze their useful components, potential errors, and complementary information. Evolutionary crossover generates new trajectories from these candidates, and a rule-based verifier determines whether the resulting answers are correct. Problems exhibiting successful recov-ery become candidates for Solver-RLVR, which optimizes fresh rollouts on the original questions using verifiable outcome rewards. The same ex-ploration also produces verifier-labeled trajectories and exposes self-evaluation errors, providing super-vision for separately trained Judge-RLVR. The learning process must also account for changes in the policy. Figure 1 shows how ob-served rollout profiles change across training stages over 28 candidate problems, each evaluated with 16 rollouts. From the base policy to the third stage, the number of all-fail problems decreases from 18 to 12, while the number of all-pass problems in-creases from 0 to 4; total correct rollouts rise from 37/448 to 128/448. These changes motivate re-assessing candidate problems under the updated policy. Building on this motivation, we propose 

policy-adaptive sequential one-shot RLVR . After each training stage, ReSI-Math reprofiles the up-dated policy and selects the next frontier problem, yielding a local recursive self-improvement loop. We evaluate ReSI-Math using Qwen3-VL back-bones with 2B, 4B, and 8B parameters. On Qwen3-VL-8B, crossover between a correct and an incor-rect trajectory produces 272 verifier-confirmed cor-rect offspring from 621 attempts (43.8%). Three sequential one-shot RLVR stages improve mean ac-curacy on MATH500 and WE-MATH from 78.71 to 80.16. In a separate single-problem comparison with matched training budgets, frontier-guided se-lection achieves an average of 54.16 across four mathematical reasoning benchmarks, compared with 53.31 for a random-selection baseline. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce an operational characterization of recoverable capability frontiers, connect-ing unreliable direct generation with verifier-confirmed recovery through bounded search under fixed model parameters. • We develop ReSI-Math, a Self-Judge-guided evolutionary framework that identifies candi-date problems for Solver-RLVR and produces trajectory-level supervision for Judge-RLVR. • We propose policy-adaptive sequential one-shot RLVR and evaluate frontier-guided learn-ing across three model scales, with analyses of search outcomes, training schedules, and changes in problem solvability. 

2 Related Work 

Recent studies on reinforcement learning with ver-ifiable rewards (RLVR) increasingly suggest that mathematical reasoning can improve with surpris-ingly limited training data. LIMR (Li et al., 2025) studies data selection for RL scaling and shows that carefully selected subsets can retain strong perfor-mance with substantially fewer training examples. One-Shot RLVR (Wang et al., 2025) pushes this regime to the extreme, demonstrating that a single training example can sometimes induce substantial reasoning gains, while DoPR (Zhang et al., 2026) dynamically selects informative examples for pol-icy refinement. Related evidence also appears beyond RLVR: One-Shot OPD (Fu et al., 2026) shows that a single query can recover a large frac-tion of full-data on-policy distillation gains. More broadly, data-efficient instruction tuning has shown that carefully selected subsets can outperform or approach much larger training sets (Chen et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024; Ivison et al., 2025), and the ordering of training examples can itself affect generalization (He et al., 2025). These results mo-tivate studying not only how much data is required, but also which examples are most valuable. Our work further treats this value as policy-dependent: the most informative problem may change as the policy itself evolves. Another line of work improves reasoning through repeated sampling, self-refinement, or evo-lutionary search. Best-of-N(Cobbe et al., 2021) and Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) obtain better solutions through sampling or iterative feedback, while COTEVOL (Wang et al., 2026) formulates CoT synthesis as population-based evolutionary search. In contrast, we use evolution as a capability-probing mechanism : rather than focusing only on the final improved CoT, we study whether failed reasoning can be recovered under a fixed policy through trajectory recombination. Prior work has explored using model-generated feedback to improve subsequent reasoning or generation, such as Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023). Related LLM-as-a-Judge methods use language models to evaluate model-generated re-sponses (Zheng et al., 2023). More recent self-evolving systems further organize such feedback into iterative improvement loops; for example, R-Zero (Huang et al., 2026) improves Challenger and Solver roles through repeated interaction. ReSI-Math instead realizes a local, policy-adaptive recur-sion in which Self-Judge guides trajectory search, RLVR updates the policy, and the updated policy is reprofiled to determine the next learning target.

The core idea of ReSI-Math is that a model’s capability boundary need not be observed only after param-eter updates through downstream evaluation; in-stead, it can be actively probed under a fixed policy by conducting structured search over the model’s own reasoning trajectories. 3.2 Repair: Self-Judge-Guided Evolutionary Search 

Given candidate regions near the recoverable capa-bility frontier, Repair evaluates whether failed rea-soning can be transformed into verifier-confirmed correct solutions through Self-Judge-guided evolu-tionary search. The model parameters remain fixed throughout this stage, and search is restricted to the trajectory space induced by the current policy. Self-Judge provides structured analysis of candi-date trajectories for evolutionary search. Two com-plementary judgment modes are considered: the 

Blind Judge evaluates reasoning without access to verifier outcomes or reference answers, whereas the Guided Judge additionally receives verifier-confirmed outcome feedback while remaining iso-lated from the reference solution. Self-Judge and the verifier therefore serve distinct functions: Self-Judge provides search guidance, whereas the veri-fier supplies the external correctness criterion used to determine whether repair succeeds. Given parent τi, τj , and their Self-Judge feed-back, an offspring trajectory is generated: 

τchild ∼ πθ(· | x, τ i, τ j , J (τi), J (τj )) . (2) The crossover operator is adapted from the reflective global crossover formulation of CoTEvol (Wang et al., 2026) and is used to recombine complementary reasoning structures rather than reproduce a parent trajectory verbatim. Each offspring is subsequently evaluated by the rule-based verifier. A verifier-confirmed transition from at least one failed parent to a correct offspring constitutes a realized failure-to-success transition, including both C −F → C and F −F → C. Such transitions provide operational evidence of recov-erability under the fixed policy. The effectiveness of C–F repair is quantified by the offspring-level recovery rate: 

RRate C-F = #{correct C–F offspring }

#{C–F offspring } . (3) This metric measures the fraction of generated C–

F offspring that are verified as correct and is dis-tinct from the fraction of source problems for which at least one successful repair is obtained. Detailed population construction, Self-Judge protocols, evo-lutionary updates, and search configurations are provided in Appendix B.2, while the crossover prompt is provided in Appendix D.

Solver Consolidation. For the Solver, a problem that exhibits a verifier-confirmed failure-to-success transition is treated as a recoverable training exam-ple. Rather than imitating the evolved correct tra-jectory itself, Solver-RLVR trains the current pol-icy directly on the original problem using verifier-based rewards. The objective is to transform ca-pability that can only be elicited through search into behavior that can be expressed more reliably through direct generation. 

Judge Consolidation. Evolution also exposes self-evaluation errors by comparing Self-Judge pre-dictions with verifier-confirmed outcomes. These disagreements provide trajectory-level supervision for Judge-RLVR, where the model is trained to better discriminate whether a candidate reasoning trajectory reaches a correct outcome. The two learning signals are therefore comple-mentary: recoverable problems provide supervi-sion for Solver-RLVR, while judgment errors pro-vide trajectory-level supervision for Judge-RLVR. Thus, Consolidate converts search-time behavior into parameterized capability: recoverable reason-ing becomes more stable direct problem-solving behavior, while self-evaluation errors become su-pervision for more reliable judgment. 

3.4 Reprofile: Local Recursive Self-Improvement 

The recoverable capability frontier is policy-dependent rather than a fixed property of the can-didate set. After Solver-RLVR updates the policy from πt to πt+1 , the rollout distribution over the same candidate problems may change: previously frontier-adjacent problems may become reliably solved or less informative, while other problems may move closer to the updated capability bound-ary. Candidate problems are therefore re-evaluated after each policy update rather than following a fixed ranking obtained before training. Let the candidate pool at stage t be 

Qt = {q1, . . . , q M }. (8) At stage t, candidate problems are re-sampled under the current policy πt, and the rollout statistics required by the frontier-selection score are recom-puted using verifier-confirmed outcomes. The next training target is selected using the same top-1 rule defined in Consolidate:

Training on q⋆t produces the updated policy πt+1 ,under which the candidate pool is reprofiled before the subsequent selection. Repeating this profile– select–update cycle yields the policy-adaptive se-quential RLVR process in ReSI-Math, where each policy update changes the evidence used to deter-mine the next training target.

4.1 Experimental Setup Models. We use Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct as the primary backbone throughout our experiments, as well as Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct and Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct. Qwen3-VL is an open-weight vision-language model family with strong text understand-ing, visual perception, and multimodal reasoning capabilities. 

Datasets. We construct our evolutionary training pool from MathBook-Pro in WE-MATH 2.0 (Qiao et al., 2026). We construct the training data for both Solver-RLVR and Judge-RLVR from a common source of 243 seed mathematical problems. For Solver-RLVR, the evolutionary search produces a 28-problem candidate pool, from which one prob-lem is selected at each sequential stage for single-problem RLVR. For Judge-RLVR, trajectory-level supervision is derived from the same evolutionary source using verifier-labeled solver trajectories. De-tailed data construction, filtering criteria, and statis-tics are provided in Appendix A. For Solver check-point selection, we construct DEV192 by sampling 96 problems from MATH500 and 96 problems from WE-MATH testmini. Since DEV192 is con-tained in the full evaluation benchmarks reported in the main tables, we additionally evaluate on sub-sets that exclude these development examples. The corresponding results are reported in Appendix C. 

Benchmarks. For Solver evaluation, we use four mathematical reasoning benchmarks: WE-MATH testmini(Qiao et al., 2025), MATH500(Hendrycks et al., 2021), AIME 2025(Zhang and Team, 2025), and OlympiadBench(He et al., 2024). For Judge evaluation, we use JudgeBench (Tan et al., 2025), a pairwise LLM-as-a-Judge benchmark with ob-jectively verifiable preference labels across Knowl-edge, Reasoning, Math, and Coding. We use the GPT-4o split containing 350 response pairs. 

Implementation Details. Both Solver-RLVR and Judge-RLVR use Group Relative Policy Opti-mization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) with LoRA adaptation. Solver-RLVR uses 16 online rollouts per training problem with batch size 1, while Judge-RLVR uses 8 rollouts per candidate prompt. Full optimization hyperparameters are provided in Ap-pendix B.3. 

4.2 Main Results Recoverability under Fixed-Policy Search. Un-der the evolutionary search protocol, 621 correct– failed ( C–F ) crossover offspring are generated from the 243-problem source set, of which 272 are verified as correct, corresponding to an offspring-level recovery rate of 43.8%. This result provides direct empirical evidence that failed trajectories can contain complementary reasoning information that remains recoverable under a fixed policy.

5 Conclusion 

We introduce ReSI-Math , a framework for re-cursive self-improvement in mathematical rea-soning. Self-Judge-guided evolutionary search probes the recoverable capability frontier and uses crossover to repair failed reasoning into verifier-confirmed correct trajectories. Recoverable prob-lems and judgment–verification discrepancies pro-vide complementary supervision for Solver-RLVR and Judge-RLVR, respectively. Policy-adaptive sequential RLVR re-profiles the problem pool af-ter each policy update to guide subsequent train-ing. Experiments show that evolutionary search uncovers capabilities not yet reliably expressed by the model, while dynamic re-profiling outper-forms static schedules in the extreme low-data regime. Thus, ReSI-Math extends evolutionary search beyond data synthesis to capability probing and learning-signal discovery, translating search-time recoverability into more reliable reasoning. Limitations 

This work has two main limitations. First, our dy-namic capability frontier is an operational, empiri-cally defined notion under the current experimental setting. While sufficient for our analysis, it does not provide a strict characterization of the model’s un-derlying capability boundary relative to the training data. Second, our experiments focus primarily on the Qwen3-VL family and mathematical reasoning, and we optimize the Solver and Judge separately rather than jointly. Generalization to other model families and reasoning domains, as well as the po-tential benefits of joint Solver–Judge optimization, remain important directions for future work.

A.1 Solver-RLVR Data Construction 

The Solver receives a text problem together with its associated image when available; text-only bench-marks contain no image input. The model gener-ates a reasoning trajectory followed by a final an-swer formatted as \boxed{...} . A deterministic rule-based verifier parses the final answer and com-pares it with the official reference answer. The ver-ifier supports multiple answer formats, including multiple-choice options, integers, decimals, frac-tions, radicals, coordinates, and symbolic expres-sions. A rollout receives reward 1 if the final an-swer is verified as correct and 0 otherwise. 

A.2 Judge-RLVR Data Construction 

The Judge receives the original problem, the as-sociated image when available, and a candidate Solver trajectory. Instead of generating a new so-lution, it predicts whether the candidate reaches a verifier-confirmed correct outcome. The final judg-ment is represented as \boxed{1} for correct and 

\boxed{0} for incorrect. The underlying verifier label is hidden from the model during RLVR. The Judge receives reward 1 if its prediction matches the hidden label and 0 otherwise. Therefore, Judge-RLVR trains outcome discrimination rather than direct answer generation. 

A.3 Dataset Statistics 

We select 243 problems organized into such fami-lies as the seed pool for evolutionary search. This family structure allows us to study complementary reasoning behaviors among closely related multi-modal problems and to perform crossover between trajectories that share common semantic structure. In our preprocessing, we group each base in-stance and its progressive variants into a common problem family, represented using the internal iden-tifiers base/x/y/z/xy/xz/yz/xyz .The resulting evolutionary trajectories provide two types of downstream supervision. At the prob-lem level , a failure-to-success transition identifies a problem that lies near the model’s recoverable capa-bility frontier and can therefore serve as a candidate for Solver-RLVR. At the trajectory level , the evolu-tionary process produces verifier-confirmed correct and incorrect candidate solutions, which can be converted into Judge-RLVR examples. Thus, the same evolutionary source provides complementary supervision for solving and judging, although the corresponding training units are different: prob-lems for Solver-RLVR and candidate trajectories for Judge-RLVR. 

B Implementation Details 

Table 6 summarizes the functional differences among evolutionary search, Solver-RLVR, and Judge-RLVR. Detailed configurations are provided in the following subsections. 

B.1 Rule-Based Verifier 

We use a deterministic rule-based outcome verifier as the external correctness anchor. During blind crossover and blind self-judgment, the model is not given the reference answer, verifier label, or in-formation about which parent trajectory is correct. After generation, the verifier checks the final out-come and assigns a correctness label. The verifier-confirmed outcome can subsequently be used by the guided Judge branch and as the trusted binary supervision for RLVR, while the reference answer itself remains hidden from the Judge. Conceptually, the Self-Judge determines where to search , whereas the rule-based verifier confirms 

whether the resulting trajectory succeeds .

B.2 Evolutionary Search Configuration 

We use the v7-era Self-Judge-guided evolutionary framework throughout the main experiments. For each seed problem, the Solver first generates 16 independent trajectories in Round 0. A rule-based verifier then determines their outcomes, after which we maintain a fixed population of four trajectories consisting of two verifier-confirmed correct trajec-tories and two failed trajectories. The population is evolved for three rounds. At each round, candidate parent trajectories are se-lected based on the current population and Self-Judge feedback, and crossover is used to generate new candidate trajectories. The offspring are ver-ified and the population is restored to the fixed 

2C + 2F composition. A Blind Judge and a Guided Judge then analyze trajectory correctness, failure modes, and complementary information to support the subsequent round. The Blind Judge does not ob-serve verifier outcomes, whereas the Guided Judge may use verifier-confirmed outcome feedback with-out observing the reference solution. Checkpoint-wise rollout profiles in Tables 10–12 reveal that single-problem RLVR does not induce uniformly monotonic capability improvement. These re-sults indicate that frontier training is directionally plastic: a single problem can move the policy ei-ther toward or away from reliable expression of a targeted capability. 

Re-learning Can Induce Reverse Interference. 

Table 13 further shows that capability recovery is not necessarily globally beneficial. Starting from the selected Q3@650 policy on Qwen3-VL-8B, re-training Q1 increases its rollout success from 11/16 to 16/16, but simultaneously reduces the pre-viously mastered Q2/Q3-related family from 64/64 to 23/64 and decreases DEV192 from 78.12 to 75.00. The corresponding full-test average also drops from 80.16 to 78.47. Thus, local reacquisi-tion of a target capability can suppress previously consolidated capabilities, providing direct evidence that sequential single-problem RLVR exhibits non-monotonic and bidirectional interference. In more severe cases, this interference manifests as partial or temporary forgetting of previously acquired ca-pabilities.
