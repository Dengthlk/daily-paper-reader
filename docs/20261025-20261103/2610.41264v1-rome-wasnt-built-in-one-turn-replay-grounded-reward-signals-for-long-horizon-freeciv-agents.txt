## ABSTRACT 

Training language-model agents to play long-horizon strategy games is hard be-cause deterministic feedback is sparse and often arrives only at the end of a game. In-game scores can offer dense signals, but they do not reliably predict future out-comes: in 8–68% of position pairs across eight strategy games, the higher-scoring position leads to the worse outcome. We term this phenomenon scoreboard traps .In Freeciv, an open-source turn-based empire-building game where several players manage units, cities, and diplomacy over hundreds of turns, we look into better reward signals for gameplay agents. To facilitate this, we build C IV HARNESS , an open system that saves current game states, branches on different decisions, and replays the game to future turns to provide measurable long-horizon feedback from saved positions. We use C IV HARNESS to train C IV TELESCOPE , an LLM Judge that predicts which one of two game positions will yield the better outcome. On scoreboard-trap pairs, C IV TELESCOPE is 1.3–2.0 times more accurate than off-the-shelf language models, and it generalizes to new game starts, later turns, and unseen rules. We then train a Freeciv gameplay agent with C IV TELESCOPE as a dense reward, and call this recipe C IV MARSHMALLOW . When used in the middle stage of the game where scoreboard traps are common, C IV MARSHMALLOW reaches an average training phase gain of 8.94 points, 77% more than the best baseline, the end-of-game reward. Over the full 120-turn game, C IV MARSHMALLOW uses a hybrid reward that starts from the scoreboard or the end-of-game reward and shifts to C IV TELESCOPE as the game advances; this hybrid reward scores highest among the rewards we compare. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large Language Model (LLM) agents have made substantial progress in decision-making in settings that require exploration and planning, like everyday web use (Zhou et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025), mobile device control (Bai et al., 2025), and strategy game playing (Schultz et al., 2025; Belle et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2026). In strategy games, agents must learn to plan carefully, manage resources, and make tactical decisions. Reinforcement learning (RL) with game-based feedback offers a way to train these LLM agents (Zhou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026). However, strategy games often involve long horizons , with the consequences of decisions unfolding over many turns. Agents must therefore learn from sparse, delayed feedback at the end of the game and connect these eventual outcomes to intermediate decisions.

Outside games, process rewards score the intermediate steps of math solutions and agent trajecto-ries (Lightman et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2025); in games, one readily available source of dense feedback is the current in-game score, shown in the game’s scoreboard (Mnih et al., 2015; K¨ uttler et al., 2020; Klissarov et al., 2024). However, this score may not reflect the long-term value of a current game position: the benefits of strategic investments often emerge later, while changing game conditions and other players’ actions can undermine an apparent lead. We call a pair of positions a scoreboard trap 

when the position with the higher current score compared to the other leads to a worse future outcome (See Figure 1(a)). Across eight strategy games with varying gameplay complexity, we demonstrate that such traps affect 8–68% of position pairs (Figure 2). To study how to provide better reward signal for strategic gameplay agents, we focus our experiments on Freeciv, an open-source turn-based empire-building game (Freeciv developers, 2026). We introduce CIV HARNESS (Section 4), a reusable harness for agentic reinforcement learning that saves game states, branches on alternative decisions, and replays their continuations to provide training feedback. With C IV HARNESS , we find that frontier LLMs rarely detect scoreboard traps: when asked which of two positions leads to the better future outcome, five frontier models reach only 26%–34% accuracy on held-out trap pairs, below chance (Figure 1(b)). We instead train C IV TELESCOPE (Section 5.2), a pairwise language-model judge, on future outcomes averaged over replays from each position, which reaches 77.5% accuracy on the same pairs. With 80 turns remaining, where scoreboard traps are common and C IV TELESCOPE can already rank positions, C IV MARSHMALLOW improves the phase gain by 77% over the best baseline (the end-of-game reward). For the full 120-turn game, C IV MARSHMALLOW shifts from the scoreboard or the end-of-game outcome to C IV TELESCOPE as the game advances, which keeps the early-game signal stable; this hybrid reward scores highest among the rewards we compare. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Turn-based strategy-game agents. CivRealm provides a Freeciv environment for learning and language-based agents (Qi et al., 2024); SAGA and Vox Deorum add planning structure (Jin et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025), and Civilization benchmarks report sparse terminal feedback over hundreds of turns (Chen et al., 2026; Andrews et al., 2026). These environments start each run from a new game or a designed scenario, whereas C IV HARNESS branches and replays any saved position, so policies and rewards can be compared against future outcomes from shared positions. Unlike the value estimator of Chen et al. (2026) which learned from played games to evaluate agents, C IV TELESCOPE 

learns from replayed branches of the same position and serves as a training reward. 

Model feedback and proxy rewards. Language- and vision-model feedback can be distilled into rewards (Klissarov et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024c; Cao et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024), but it depends on the model recognizing progress; optimizing a proxy can diverge from the intended objective (Gao et al., 2022; Razin et al., 2025), and game benchmarks document gaps between visible scores and progress (K¨ uttler et al., 2020; Paglieri et al., 2025). We measure these gaps against replayed outcomes and show that replay labels, unlike preference labels, do not inherit the labeler’s trap errors. 

Rollout-grounded feedback and curricula. Outside games, process supervision and value esti-mation use step labels, Monte-Carlo rollouts, or progress measures (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b; Yuan et al., 2025; Setlur et al., 2025; Kazemnejad et al., 2025), and agent methods learn rewards or values from interaction (Xia et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025). We instead train a pairwise judge from rollout outcomes in a multiplayer strategy game and calibrate it with reference positions of known value. Following reverse curricula (Florensa et al., 2017; Salimans & Chen, 2018), we increase the remaining turns from phase to phase and compare reward signals within each phase. 

## 3 BACKGROUND 

Freeciv. Freeciv (Freeciv developers, 2026) is an open-source turn-based strategy game modeled on Sid Meier’s Civilization 2. Each player leads a civilization from its first settlers: it founds and grows cities, researches technologies, builds units and wonders, and negotiates or fights with the other players. In our games, the agent controls one of four players on a 1000-tile map; the other three are Freeciv’s built-in AI players at a fixed bot difficulty (Section 4). The agent sees only what its units and cities have explored. Freeciv keeps a running score for every player, computed from quantities such as citizens, technologies, wonders, and culture, and shows it on the in-game scoreboard; we call the agent’s score at the end of the game its terminal score . Scoreboard traps across strategy games. Strategy games often suffer from scoreboard traps :when the higher current position can lead to a worse future outcome. To measure the traps, we build pairs of positions from recorded games and count the fraction of pairs in which the scoreboard order opposes the order of their future outcomes. Scoreboard traps occur in 8–68% of decidable position pairs across eight games (Figure 2(a)), including 26% in Freeciv under CIV 2CIV 3 and 27% under 

CLASSIC (Appendix Table 5). Trap rates also vary with game progress (Figure 2(b)): in CIV 2CIV 3, the rate falls from 55% in the first tenth of the game to 8% in the last (Appendix A.2). In Freeciv, the scoreboard also becomes a worse predictor as games get longer: from 60-turn to 80-turn games, its correlation with the terminal score falls from 0.81 to 0.12 and the trap rate rises from 30% to 54% (Appendix Table 9). 

Off-the-shelf LLM judges. On held-out Freeciv trap pairs, all six LLMs, from frontier closed models to a small open model, score below chance (0.26–0.34), even though they reach 0.77–0.82 on non-trap pairs (Figure 1(b); Appendix Table 11). Warning them in the prompt that visible scores can mislead does not help: the frontier models still rank only 0.32–0.38 of trap pairs correctly. The failure also appears in simpler games: four of five frontier models rank below chance in Chess, and two of five in Othello (Appendix Table 6). 

## 4 DESIGNING AND BUILDING CIV HARNESS 

To facilitate our training and evaluation, we introduce C IV HARNESS , a reusable harness for agentic reinforcement learning in Freeciv. From a saved state, C IV HARNESS runs K deterministically seeded branches on the real Freeciv game engine, either to the end of the game or for a chosen number of turns, and records their observations, actions, and outcomes; replay checks verify that the same save, seed, and action trace reproduce the same state. A gameplaying agent interacts through configurable observations and actions, including city produc-tion, research, and unit orders executed by the game engine. The agent sees only its own player’s view of the game, not the full game state. We also speed gameplay rollouts by implementing batch execution, which manages independent games, retries on failed gameplay runs, and records completed work so collection can resume. CIV HARNESS records the agent’s ordinary rollouts, and restoring a saved position lets it collect sibling rollouts that take different decisions from the same state, for group-based training and for comparing reward signals from shared starts. Game logs report observations, issued actions, branch seeds, and bot difficulty. Researchers using C IV HARNESS can therefore reuse position banks, replace a policy or reward, and reproduce the resulting comparisons with the same replay checks. Figure 3 shows how C IV HARNESS connects to slime, the reinforcement-learning training engine used in our experiments. The rollout interface exchanges player-visible observations and actions between the policy and the game engine, converts C IV TELESCOPE ’s pairwise preferences into state values with a reference set for judge-based rewards (Equation (2)), and passes the resulting samples to slime, which updates the policy. In this section, we describe using C IV HARNESS to first create trap pairs to train an LLM judge, CIV TELESCOPE , that can escape the scoreboard trap. We then detail C IV MARSHMALLOW , our recipe for training a Freeciv gameplay agent with C IV TELESCOPE rewards, used alone or combined with other rewards over the course of a game. 5.1 REPLAY ORACLE AND TRAP PAIRS 

We define a replay oracle as a mechanism that takes the current game state and uses C IV HARNESS to observe future game states, possible till the end of the game. For a game state s, the replay oracle creates K branches forward from s, all the way to the end of the game with Freeciv’s built-in bots at a fixed bot difficulty. We define Y (s) as the player’s mean terminal score over these branches from s.For positions taken from policy rollouts, Y (s) is the realized terminal score of that rollout. CIV TELESCOPE is a pairwise language-model-based judge, fine-tuned from Qwen2.5-7B. It predicts which position has the higher terminal score under the replay oracle. The input of C IV TELESCOPE 

is a pair of game positions, each rendered as text from the evaluated player’s point of view: every player’s score together with that player’s gold, technologies, cities, and units. The output is the position expected to reach the higher terminal score; because the answer can depend on which position is listed first, we query both orders and average the two answers. The training labels are the terminal scores of two kinds of games: branches played to the end by Freeciv’s bots from saved positions, which give Y (s), and the agent’s own rollouts. A training pair holds two positions from different games at most ten turns apart whose labels differ by more than twice their pooled standard error, and we fine-tune the model with LoRA (rank 16) on 10,800 such pairs. Section 5.3 turns its pairwise preferences into a reward. 5.3 CIV MARSHMALLOW : T RAINING RECIPE FOR FREECIV GAMEPLAY AGENTS 

We now provide the full training recipe, C IV MARSHMALLOW , for training a Freeciv gameplaying agent. The recipe has three parts: a phased training schedule that moves from late-game to full-game starts, the C IV TELESCOPE reward, which turns the judge’s preferences into a dense reward, and, for full games, a hybrid reward that combines C IV TELESCOPE with a sparse or scoreboard reward. We train the baseline rewards, SPARSE and SCOREBOARD , with the same schedule, so that every reward is compared under the same conditions. 

Phased training approach. Every gameplay agent, whether trained with C IV MARSHMALLOW 

or with a baseline reward, is trained on late-game positions first and on earlier positions later. We denote the number of game turns remaining to play by rem , and the maximum number of turns in our gameplay is 120. The gameplay agent is trained sequentially in four training phases , rem20 ,

rem40 , rem80 , and rem120 , each with more turns remaining than the one before. Each training phase starts from the policy checkpoint of the preceding phase and a fixed position bank , a set of saved game states from which training games start (turn 100, 80, 40, and 1). Training thus follows a reverse curriculum, which starts near the end of a task and moves the start earlier as training proceeds (Florensa et al., 2017; Salimans & Chen, 2018). 5Rewards. We show that current game scores frequently disagree with eventual outcomes, creating scoreboard traps that are also difficult for off-the-shelf LLM judges to recognize. We introduce C IV HARNESS ,which branches and replays saved game states to obtain grounded long-horizon feedback, and use these replay outcomes to train C IV TELESCOPE , a judge that more reliably distinguishes positions by their future value. We then use C IV TELESCOPE as a dense reward within C IV MARSHMALLOW ,a phased training recipe for Freeciv agents. We show that replay-grounded rewards are especially useful in the middle of the game and C IV TELESCOPE can already rank positions, while combining them with simpler signals yields stronger full-game training. More broadly, our findings suggest that for long-horizon agents, useful reward signals need not come directly from what looks good now: grounding feedback in realized future outcomes can provide a better basis for learning gameplay.

## A BACKGROUND 

A.1 FREECIV RULESETS 

A Freeciv ruleset is a set of data files that defines the units, buildings, technologies, governments, terrain, and game parameters, so the same server plays different games under different rulesets. We use two rulesets shipped with Freeciv 3.2.5. CIV 2CIV 3, the default ruleset of the Freeciv 3 server, keeps the rules of Civilization II as its reference and moves the game toward Civilization III, aiming at slower and more linear city development; CLASSIC is the older standard ruleset from which it departs. Relative to CLASSIC , CIV 2CIV 3 removes rapture growth, so celebrating cities no longer grow every turn; makes Settlers cost two citizens instead of one; adds food upkeep for most units; enables plague in cities larger than four; makes a technology cheaper when a nation the player has an embassy with already knows it, and charges a per-city research upkeep late in the game; and requires at least three tiles between cities. Scoreboard traps occur under both rulesets, in 26% of decidable position pairs under CIV 2CIV 3 and 27% under CLASSIC (Figure 2(a)). The main experiments, the judge’s training data, and its held-out evaluation use CIV 2CIV 3; CLASSIC is used only to test the judge on unseen rules (Appendix D.4). A.2 CROSS -GAME TRAP PROBE 

Every Freeciv trap rate and trap pair in the paper uses the terminal target of Section 5.1, the mean terminal score of the evaluated player; Table 5 lists the continuation policy, branch count, game length, and pair rule behind each. The chess bank holds 541 positions from 80 played games, seven snapshots per game, each valued by 20 stochastic Stockfish replays to the end with the mean result for the side to move as the oracle and material balance in pawns as the visible proxy. The Othello bank holds 420 positions from 70 games, six snapshots per game, each valued by 24 stochastic replays with the final disc differential as the oracle and the current disc differential as the proxy. Pairs are built exactly as for Freeciv: across games, bounded move gap, decidable when the oracle gap exceeds twice the pooled standard error. The zero-shot evaluation uses 300 trap and 300 non-trap pairs per game, both presentation orders, using the Freeciv prompt with only the game name changed. Calls that time out are excluded rather than scored. Table 6 reports the per-judge results. Five further banks (Table 7) apply the same pipeline to the OpenSpiel implementations of backgam-mon, 9 ×9 Go, Hearts, and 2048, and to the Catanatron implementation of Settlers of Catan. Positions are snapshots of self-play by a deliberately weak stochastic engine, Monte Carlo tree search with 30 simulations of one random rollout each and 10% uniformly random moves, which plays the role of the skill-level noise of Stockfish in chess. 2048 uses a one-ply greedy policy with the same random fraction and Hearts uniform random play, because tree search with terminal rewards applies neither to a single-player game with intermediate rewards nor to an imperfect-information game. Each position is replayed K=100 times by the same engine ( K=48 for Catan, where every snapshot yields four positions, one per seat, sharing the same continuations). ## C TRAINING A FREECIV GAMEPLAYING AGENT 

C.1 JUDGE TRAINING AND CALIBRATION 

The judge is evaluated on 600 non-trap and 600 trap pairs from games and rollouts excluded from training. For each sampled game turn of the phase being trained, the reference set of Equation 2 is drawn from eight positions with known terminal scores: six from policy rollouts, evenly spaced in rank by terminal score, and the two highest-scoring bot-played positions. During training B holds four of the eight, chosen to span the score range ( M =4 ), and the judge is queried in one presentation order. In the JUDGE and hybrid arms a candidate set is rebuilt every N =4 policy updates from 40 recent training rollouts and validated on the 40 rollouts that follow them. The candidate replaces the set in use only if the Spearman correlation between its potential and the terminal score is at least 

ρmin =0 .2 both at turn 40 and at the last decision (Equation 3); the set never changes within a policy update. The potential is sensitive to the score range of the reference set. In the rem120 phase of seed 44, replacing only the reference set, from one whose four scores ranged from 7 to 33 to one ranging from 2 to 36, turned the training-score slope from −0.06 to +0 .06 points per iteration; on seed 43 the slope was positive with both sets ( 0.10 and 0.16 ). On the terminal-score label set, full-parameter SFT memorizes the training set and lands at chance on held-out pairs while dismantling the base model’s score-reading prior; LoRA with rank 16 on the same data, labels, and prompt generalizes, and it is stable across three training seeds. ## D EXPERIMENTS 

D.1 AGGREGATION 

For each starting position, the eight games are averaged before computing a phase gain, and a game in which the agent’s player is eliminated counts as a score of zero. Paired win rates compare these start-level gains. D.2 JUDGE EVALUATION AND ZERO -SHOT JUDGES 

Off-the-shelf models are scored on a seeded subsample of 300 trap and 300 non-trap pairs of the judge’s held-out set, with the same fog renderings and prompt as the judge; intervals are cluster bootstraps over source games, and ratios are paired over the same pairs (Table 11). The judge’s accuracies on the full 600-pair slices are 0.778 and 0.866. Every pair is presented in both orders and accuracy is content-level. D.3 REGRESSION VALUE HEAD PROTOCOL 

The head shares C IV TELESCOPE ’s base, adapter, and recipe (Qwen2.5-7B, LoRA rank 16, two epochs, one seed) and replaces the pairwise objective with a scalar output trained to predict a position’s replay outcome, the mean terminal score of its K=8 branches, under a Huber loss on standardized targets. Per-position replay outcomes are available for 2,900 positions of the judge’s training bank; the head is trained on the 2,583 of them from games outside the held-out set and read after the two epochs, without snapshot selection. On a pair it picks the position with the higher predicted score. It is scored on the 200 held-out trap pairs and 197 non-trap pairs whose positions carry a per-position label, and C IV TELESCOPE and the zero-shot backbone are re-read on the identical pairs from their existing answers: the head scores 0.520 [0 .317 , 0.688] on trap pairs and 0.548 on non-trap pairs, C IV TELESCOPE 0.688 and 0.777, and the backbone 0.219 and 0.612. The head fits its training positions (Pearson r = 0 .77 with the replay outcome) but not held-out positions (r = 0 .16 ). D.6 PROMPTING -SCAFFOLD PANEL 

The five prompting-based agents in Table 3 are direct prompting; BaseLang, which writes a chain of reasoning before the action, and Mastaba, a hierarchical design with one summarizing call over several advisor calls, both from CivRealm (Qi et al., 2024); the scene-graph planner SAGA (Jin et al., 2026); and the self-reflection loop Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023). We re-implement the four published designs on our menu interface with our own prompts. Of the 1,800 prompting games, 1,684 completed and the rest failed on infrastructure. All use the same Qwen3-8B base, the same menu interface, and the same opponents as the trained policies, on the rem120 evaluation set Erem120 .The trained policies are the checkpoints produced by the rem120 phase, the last of the four training phases, on that base. Prompt cost dominates the per-call cost, so the trained policy and the scaffolds with about one call per decision cost the same; only the hierarchical advisor scaffold, with four calls per decision, is materially more expensive. D.7 LABEL -SOURCE ABLATION PROTOCOL

The labeler agrees with the replay outcome on 0.678 of the pairs overall and on 0.489 of the trap pairs.
