# Human-Feedback-Guided Multi-Agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning for Interactive Driving Behavior Modeling 

Longhao Yan, Jinhao Liang, Kaidi Yang 

Abstract —Modeling human driving behavior is fundamental to understanding driver decision-making and supporting au-tonomous vehicles (AVs) in mixed traffic. Reward learning offers a principled way to infer the latent objectives behind driving behaviors, yet most existing methods are formulated in single-agent settings and treat surrounding vehicles as dynamic obsta-cles. Directly extending reward learning to multi-agent scenarios is difficult because the joint action space grows exponentially with the number of interacting vehicles. In addition, existing approaches mainly rely on physical demonstrations and rarely exploit human feedback during reward recovery. To address these limitations, this paper proposes a Human-Feedback-Guided Multi-Agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning (HFG-MAIRL) framework for interactive driving behavior modeling. First, we develop a multi-agent maximum-entropy inverse reinforcement learning module that explicitly models each vehicle and its directly interacting neighbors in a localized Markov game, while using aggregated traffic states to capture the influence of the broader traffic environment. The resulting coupled multi-vehicle responses are approximated through finite marginal-response iterations over factorized vehicle-level policies, avoiding explicit enumeration of the full joint action space. Second, we incorporate human feedback as ordinal preference supervision through a Bradley–Terry likelihood. In our experiments, driver emotion labels are used as one instantiation of such feedback and converted into pairwise ordinal preference constraints. Experi-ments on the AIDE dataset show that HFG-MAIRL outperforms representative IRL baselines in decision modeling and produces interpretable feedback-aligned reward patterns. 

Index Terms —Multi-Agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning (MAIRL), Human Feedback, Reward Learning, Driving Behavior Modeling, Driver Emotion.

Most existing approaches to human driving behavior mod-eling focus on predicting the short-term intent of surrounding agents (e.g., drivers or human traffic participants) based on historical trajectories [9]–[17]. For instance, Ref. However, these approaches do not explicitly capture the underlying decision-making process and may therefore generalize poorly to scenarios beyond their training data, such as behavioral shifts in response to AVs. To uncover the underlying decision-making process behind driving behaviors, recent studies have attempted to infer, from expert demonstrations, the objectives or rewards that human drivers seek to optimize [18]–[24]. For example, Ref. [19] applied inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) to infer risk-averse reward functions for human drivers at unsignalized intersections, explicitly modeling their trade-offs between safety and efficiency. Ref. [20] utilized deep IRL to extract implicit reward maps from expert demonstrations, enabling agents to learn nuanced social navigation rules based on scene geometry. Ref. [23] employed IRL to model human car-following dynamics, specifically quantifying how drivers adapt their underlying reward preferences when influenced by AVs. However, these approaches often consider single-agent settings, where each human driver is modeled in isolation, and surrounding agents are treated as part of the environment rather than strategic participants whose decisions mutually influence one another. As a result, the learned reward func-tions may not fully capture the strategic interactions that shape driving decisions in real-world traffic. Only a few recent studies have explicitly modeled multi-driver interactions through multi-agent IRL (MAIRL) [25]–[30]. For example, Ref. [26] framed driver interactions as a decentralized multi-agent problem, utilizing adversarial IRL to simultaneously arXiv:2610.33726v1 [cs.AI] 24 Oct 2026 2

[27] developed a game-theoretic IRL approach to quantify diverse social patterns and coupled decision-making during complex driving interactions. Ref. [28] proposed a hierarchical MAIRL framework that incorporates the concept of driving responsibility to capture socially compatible interactive behaviors. These studies high-light the need to extend driving reward learning from isolated agents to interaction-aware multi-agent settings. Nevertheless, this extension remains computationally challenging, as the joint action space grows exponentially with the number of interacting vehicles, making direct joint-policy inference in-tractable in realistic traffic scenarios. Moreover, rewards learned solely from trajectory demon-strations may not necessarily reflect the true objectives of human drivers. Even when the learned rewards generate be-haviorally plausible trajectories, they may primarily reflect sta-tistical regularities in observed data rather than the subjective considerations behind human driving decisions. One potential solution is to incorporate human feedback signals, which are increasingly available in various forms (e.g., emotion, verbal comments, satisfaction ratings), as an additional source of information for interpreting and constraining the learned rewards. Such signals can reveal drivers’ relative preferences across different traffic states, actions, or interaction outcomes, thereby providing qualitative guidance on which situations should be assigned higher or lower rewards. However, little attention has been paid to incorporating human feedback signals into the learning of human driving rewards. 

Statement of Contributions: To address the aforementioned gaps, we propose a Human-Feedback-Guided Multi-Agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning (HFG-MAIRL) framework for interactive driving behavior modeling. Our contribution is two-fold. First, we develop a MAIRL framework that recovers interaction-aware reward functions from coupled multi-vehicle driving demonstrations. Unlike single-agent formulations that treat surrounding vehicles as part of the environment, our framework models vehicles as strategic agents whose decisions are mutually influenced through their interactions. To enable tractable reward learning, we explicitly model each vehicle and its directly interacting neighbors while representing broader traffic conditions through aggregated traffic states. Second, to enhance the behavioral relevance of the learned rewards, we develop a human-feedback-guided component that incorpo-rates available human feedback as ordinal preference super-vision through a Bradley–Terry likelihood, thereby mitigating the ambiguity of trajectory-only reward learning and better aligning the learned rewards with drivers’ subjective responses to traffic interactions. In this paper, we use driver emotion as an example instantiation of such human feedback. To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first attempts to incorporate human feedback signals into driving reward learning. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Sec-tion II presents the problem formulation. Section III details the methodology of the proposed HFG-MAIRL framework. Section IV evaluates the proposed framework on a multimodal driving dataset. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

As illustrated in Fig. 1, we propose a Human-Feedback-Guided Multi-Agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning (HFG-MAIRL) framework for interactive driving behavior modeling through reward learning from multi-vehicle demonstrations and human feedback. Section III-A formulates the multi-vehicle driving process as a finite-horizon Markov game with interaction-aware reward functions. Building on this formulation, Section III-B develops a maximum-entropy-based MAIRL approach that learns context-dependent reward weights from demonstrated multi-vehicle decisions through iterative marginal-response inference. To make MAIRL tractable, we localize the interaction graph to each focal vehi-cle and its directly interacting neighbors, while the influence of vehicles outside the local graph is retained from aggregated traffic states. Section III-C incorporates available human feed-back as ordinal preference supervision to constrain the relative rewards of the corresponding demonstrated decisions.

B. Localized Multi-Agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning 

With the Markov game formulated above, this subsection leverages MAIRL to infer the parameters θint ,i,κ and θind ,i,κ 5

in Eq. (1) from interactive driving demonstrations, which char-acterize driver preferences over different performance criteria. However, directly applying MAIRL in typical traffic scenarios may require a large interaction graph. In particular, while a vehicle’s decision depends on its neighboring vehicles, those neighbors’ decisions are also influenced by their own neigh-bors. Accounting for such dependencies therefore requires the interaction graph to extend beyond the immediate neighbor-hood and, recursively, across a potentially large portion of the traffic system. Explicitly modeling such a large interaction graph poses two challenges. First, it requires demonstration datasets covering a correspondingly large portion of the traffic system, which are typically unavailable. Second, it substan-tially increases the computational complexity of solving the Markov game. To address these challenges, we construct a local interaction graph for each vehicle that explicitly includes only the vehicle itself and its immediate neighbors. This confines both the Markov game computation and the required demonstration data to a small local graph. To retain the influence of vehicles outside this graph, we represent their collective effects on each neighboring vehicle through aggregated traffic states, analogous to mean-field states. This localized representation therefore captures the influence of the broader traffic environ-ment while substantially reducing the required data coverage and computational complexity. Accordingly, the presentation of our methodology is divided into two parts. We first present the MAIRL formulation for the general Markov game, and then describe how it is applied to traffic scenarios through a localized Markov game with aggregated traffic states.

2) Localized Markov Game with Aggregated Traffic States: 

To make the Markov game tractable with locally available driving demonstrations, we localize the interaction graph by explicitly retaining only each focal vehicle and its directly interacting neighbors. Simply truncating the graph, however, would neglect the influence of vehicles outside the local graph, which can still affect the decisions of the explicitly represented vehicles through the surrounding traffic condi-tions. We therefore summarize these external vehicles using fixed-dimensional aggregated traffic states within the localized Markov game. Overall, such localization affects two compo-nents of the original Markov-game formulation: (i) the ob-servation of each explicitly represented vehicle is augmented with the aggregated traffic state to retain the influence of the surrounding traffic, and (ii) the forward-safety interaction of a frontmost vehicle is evaluated against a virtual proxy constructed from the aggregated traffic state when no real leading vehicle is explicitly represented.

Each cell covers approximately one lane in width and 20–30 m longitudinally, and ρdi,t denotes the traffic density within cell

The cells are oriented according to the local direction of the lane occupied by vehicle i, and their densities are collected as ρi,t = [ ρFi,t , ρ F L i,t , ρ Li,t , ρ BL i,t , ρ Bi,t , ρ BR i,t ,ρRi,t , ρ F R i,t ]⊤.This representation retains spatial coverage of the surround-ing traffic while avoiding explicit modeling of every vehicle. Directly interacting neighbors are represented at the micro-scopic level, whereas vehicles beyond the local interaction graph are summarized at the macroscopic level through traffic density. The latter retain the aggregate traffic information within each local cell while sacrificing vehicle-level resolu-tion, thereby providing complementary information about the broader traffic environment. Such local traffic densities can be obtained or estimated from onboard perception or other available traffic measurements.

C. Human-Feedback-Guided Preference Alignment 

The MAIRL approach developed above aims to infer reward weights from interactive driving demonstrations, but demon-strations alone only provide behavioral evidence and may not fully reveal the subjective preferences underlying the observed decisions. For example, similar driving behaviors may lead to different subjective evaluations depending on the driver’s perceived comfort, safety, or satisfaction. To reduce this ambi-guity, we incorporate human feedback, represented by interval-level labels characterizing the driver’s subjective response to the experienced driving behavior (e.g., emotional responses), which is assumed to be available for a subset of vehicles 

Vfb ⊆ V . Such feedback can be readily obtained from increas-ingly common in-cabin driver monitoring modalities, such as video recordings of facial expressions, and subsequently processed or annotated into feedback labels. By providing sub-jective evaluations associated with demonstrated state-action 7

pairs, human feedback offers complementary supervision for aligning the learned reward with human preferences. B. Experiment Setup and Implementation Details 

Fig. 2: Illustration of the multimodal driver emotion dataset used in this work. AIDE captures naturalistic on-road driving with synchronized multi-view videos annotated with five emo-tion categories. In this study, we instantiate the general human-feedback formulation using driver emotion annotations. The proposed framework itself is not restricted to emotion feedback and can accommodate other categorical or ordinal feedback signals from which pairwise preferences can be constructed. 

1) Dataset: We utilize the AIDE dataset [36] to eval-uate the proposed framework in naturalistic on-road driv-ing scenarios. Naturalistic datasets containing both traffic-scene observations and synchronized driver-state annota-tions are scarce, and AIDE provides paired observations of driver states and surrounding traffic through synchronized in-cabin, front, left, and right camera recordings. Each sam-ple consists of a 3-second driving clip with 45 frames per view and is annotated with an emotion category e ∈{Anxiety , Peace , Weariness , Happiness , Anger }. Vehicle trajec-tories are obtained through YOLO-based detection and multi-object tracking [37] and projected into a bird’s-eye-view coor-dinate system. Only lane-changing and forward-driving scenes are included, as these scenarios contain explicit multi-vehicle interactions while providing sufficient trajectory observations for reward inference. The dataset is split at the clip level into training, validation, and test sets with an approximate ratio of 70%/15%/15%. The validation set is used exclusively for model selection and configuration determination. After all model configurations and default hyperparameters are fixed, the test set is used for benchmark comparison, ablation studies, and sensitivity analysis. Fig. 2 presents representative examples from the AIDE dataset. 

2) Implementation Details: We implement all benchmark methods using the hyperparameter settings reported in their original publications, with minor adaptations to ensure com-patibility with the action representation used in our evaluation. For a fair comparison, all methods use the same trajectory can-9

didate generation procedure, terminal-anchor sets, observation horizons, and traffic observations. HFG-MAIRL uses an observation horizon and decision interval of Tobs = Tdec = 1 .0 s . Candidate terminal anchors are uniformly discretized over longitudinal and lateral dis-placement ranges [smin , s max ] and [dmin , d max ], whose bounds are estimated from the 5th and 95th percentiles of the training trajectories. The same anchor specification is used across vehicles, and the default action space contains K = 20 

trajectory candidates. Each candidate action corresponds to a 16-point trajectory segment over the subsequent decision interval. For reward-feature construction, the reference speed is set to 

vref = 15 .0 m /s. The acceleration and yaw-rate normalization constants are amax = 3.0 m /s2 and ωmax = 0.5 rad /s,respectively. The anisotropic social-distance scales are set to 

σx = 10 .0 m and σy = 2 .0 m , where σy < σ x increases sen-sitivity to lateral proximity during adjacent-lane interactions. The context encoder fctx is implemented as a two-layer multilayer perceptron with a hidden dimension of 32 and outputs context-dependent weights for the individual and in-teraction reward features. Multi-vehicle decision coupling is approximated using Nresp = 3 marginal-response iterations at each decision epoch. Each episode contains Tm = 2 decision intervals, with the discount factor and inverse-temperature parameter set to γ = 1 .0 and β = 1 .0, respectively. The emotion scoring function S(e) maps Happiness to 2, 

Peace to 1, and Anxiety , Weariness , and Anger to 0. These scores are used only to construct ordinal preference relations Happiness ≻ Peace ≻ { Anxiety , Weariness , Anger } and are not treated as numerical reward targets. Any alternative numer-ical values that preserve this ordering would yield exactly the same set of preference pairs. Although the general formulation permits feedback for any subset Vfb ⊆ V , AIDE provides emotion annotations only for the ego driver. We therefore set 

Vfb = {ego } and apply the preference loss only to the reward of the feedback-annotated ego vehicle. All learnable models are trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 and a batch size of 8. The default preference-loss and regularization weights are set to 

λpref = 1 .0 and λreg = 10 −4, respectively. All benchmark, ablation, and sensitivity experiments follow the same training and evaluation protocol. All models are implemented in Py-Torch and trained on a high-performance computing platform equipped with NVIDIA H100 and A40 GPUs. 

C. Performance Analysis 

We evaluate HFG-MAIRL on the test set by measuring how accurately the learned policy reconstructs demonstrated vehicle behaviors. At each decision interval, the policy selects the highest-probability trajectory candidate for each vehicle based on vehicle states and traffic history updated using the observed trajectories. The selected trajectories are compared with expert trajectories for all vehicles. We report Average Displacement Error (ADE) and Final Displacement Error (FDE) for spatial accuracy, Speed Deviation Error (SDE) and Acceleration Deviation Error (AccDE) for kinematic accuracy, and Heading Deviation Error (HDE) for heading consistency. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation over five random seeds. Table I compares HFG-MAIRL with representative IRL benchmarks. HFG-MAIRL achieves the lowest reconstruction errors across all five metrics, with an ADE of 0.32 m, FDE of 0.95 m, SDE of 1.06 m/s, AccDE of 1.99 m/s 2,and HDE of 0.10 rad. Compared with AIRL, HFG-MAIRL reduces ADE, FDE, SDE, AccDE, and HDE by 85.5%, 83.2%, 68.3%, 56.6%, and 44.4%, respectively. The other three IRL baselines achieve substantially lower errors than AIRL but remain considerably less accurate than HFG-MAIRL. In particular, compared with MaxEnt IRL, which achieves the strongest overall reconstruction performance among these baselines, HFG-MAIRL further reduces ADE by 76.5%, FDE by 68.0%, SDE by 36.1%, AccDE by 24.3%, and HDE by 28.6%. The comparatively small performance differences among Margin IRL, Variational Bayesian IRL, and MaxEnt IRL, together with their larger gap from HFG-MAIRL, suggest that changing the conventional IRL learning objective alone is insufficient to achieve the reconstruction accuracy obtained by the proposed interaction-aware framework. The largest relative improvements of HFG-MAIRL are observed in ADE and FDE, indicating that its advantage is particularly pronounced in recovering the spatial outcome of demonstrated decisions. The simultaneous reductions in SDE, AccDE, and HDE further show that the improvement extends to the associated motion dynamics.

D. Ablation Study 

We evaluate the contributions of four key components in HFG-MAIRL through ablation experiments: the aggregated traffic state, virtual forward-safety proxy, marginal-response inference, and human feedback supervision. Each component is removed individually, with the remaining model structure and evaluation settings kept unchanged. Table II summarizes the resulting performance. 

Value of the aggregated traffic state. We evaluate the contribu-tion of the aggregated traffic state to vehicle observations by removing it from the context representation while retaining its use for constructing the virtual forward-safety proxy. Without this component, ADE increases from 0.32 m to 0.34 m, FDE 

Fig. 4: Learned reward distributions grouped by emotion label. from 0.95 m to 0.97 m, SDE from 1.06 m/s to 1.09 m/s, AccDE from 1.99 m/s 2 to 2.03 m/s 2, and HDE from 0.10 rad to 0.11 rad. The degradation is modest but consistent across all five metrics. This pattern is consistent with the role of the aggregated traffic state as a complementary representation of broader traffic conditions: directly interacting neighbors already provide detailed vehicle-level interaction information, while the aggregated state supplies additional context from traffic beyond the explicitly modeled local interactions. Its removal therefore does not eliminate the primary interaction information, but reduces the broader contextual information available for context-dependent reward inference. 

Value of the virtual forward-safety proxy. We evaluate the contribution of the virtual proxy by removing it from HFG-MAIRL. The virtual proxy provides a forward-safety refer-ence for a lane-frontmost vehicle when no physical leader is explicitly represented in the localized interaction graph. Removing this component increases ADE from 0.32 m to 0.33 m, SDE from 1.06 m/s to 1.09 m/s, AccDE from 1.99 m/s 2 to 2.03 m/s 2, and HDE from 0.10 rad to 0.11 rad, while FDE remains unchanged at 0.95 m at the reported precision. The relatively small overall effect is consistent with the specialized role of the virtual proxy: it is activated only when an explicitly represented physical leader is unavailable and contributes only to the forward-safety components of the interaction reward. The modest degradation therefore suggests that the proxy supplements the localized interaction model with useful forward-traffic constraints without becoming a dominant source of decision information. 

Value of marginal-response inference. We evaluate the contri-11 

bution of marginal-response inference by disabling the itera-tive updates of neighboring-vehicle responses while retaining the remaining interaction components. Removing marginal-response inference increases ADE from 0.32 m to 0.47 m, FDE from 0.95 m to 1.40 m, SDE from 1.06 m/s to 1.61 m/s, AccDE from 1.99 m/s 2 to 2.90 m/s 2, and HDE from 0.10 rad to 0.15 rad. The substantial and consistent degradation across spatial, kinematic, and heading metrics indicates that iterative response modeling affects the overall evaluation of decisions. In HFG-MAIRL, each vehicle evaluates its candidate actions under the current response distributions of the other interacting vehicles, and the resulting policies are iteratively updated. Disabling this process removes the propagation of mutual decision responses across vehicles, leading to less accurate reconstruction of the demonstrated interactive decisions. These results provide empirical support for explicitly accounting for coupled multi-vehicle responses during reward inference. 

Value of human feedback preference supervision. We evaluate the contribution of human feedback supervision by remov-ing the preference loss from HFG-MAIRL. Without human feedback supervision, ADE increases from 0.32 m to 0.46 m, FDE from 0.95 m to 1.36 m, SDE from 1.06 m/s to 1.56 m/s, AccDE from 1.99 m/s 2 to 2.81 m/s 2, and HDE from 0.10 rad to 0.13 rad. Notably, the human feedback does not directly supervise trajectory positions or kinematic quantities, and it provides only ordinal constraints on the rewards associated with demonstrated decisions. The broad degradation observed after removing this supervision indicates that these additional reward-ordering constraints help distinguish among behav-iorally plausible reward structures and improve the resulting evaluation of trajectory candidates. Together with the emotion-grouped reward structures shown in Figs. 3 and 4, the results show that human feedback is a complementary source of supervision that contributes both to reward interpretation and to behavioral reconstruction beyond trajectory demonstrations alone. 

E. Sensitivity Analysis 

We further evaluate the effects of two key parameters in HFG-MAIRL through sensitivity analysis: the number of trajectory candidates K and the preference-loss weight λpref .Each parameter is varied individually, with the remaining model structure and evaluation settings kept unchanged. Ta-ble III summarizes the resulting performance. † indicates the default setting. 

Sensitivity analysis on trajectory candidate number. We eval-uate the effect of the number of trajectory candidates K by varying the size of the discrete action space. Increasing K

from 10 to 20 improves all five reconstruction metrics, with ADE and FDE decreasing from 0.42 m and 1.18 m to 0.32 m and 0.95 m, respectively. This improvement is consistent with the action representation of HFG-MAIRL: a larger candidate set provides finer coverage of feasible terminal displacements and reduces the mismatch between demonstrated trajectories and the available discrete actions. Increasing K further to 30 reduces ADE, FDE, SDE, and AccDE to 0.30 m, 0.85 m, 0.93 m/s, and 1.77 m/s 2, respectively, while HDE increases slightly from 0.10 rad to 0.12 rad. Thus, increasing candidate resolution generally improves spatial and kinematic recon-struction, although the benefit is not uniform across all motion characteristics. The default K = 20 therefore provides strong reconstruction performance with a smaller action space, while 

K = 30 offers additional accuracy on several metrics at the cost of evaluating more candidate actions during marginal-response inference. 

Sensitivity analysis on preference-loss weight. We evaluate the effect of the preference-loss weight λpref by varying the contribution of human-feedback-derived ordinal preference su-pervision during reward learning. Reconstruction performance remains relatively stable across λpref ∈ 0.5, 1.0, 2.0. The default λpref = 1 .0 achieves the lowest ADE, FDE, SDE, and AccDE of 0.32 m, 0.95 m, 1.06 m/s, and 1.99 m/s 2,respectively, while HDE remains 0.10 rad across the three evaluated settings at the reported precision. The limited varia-tion across this range indicates that the learned behavior is not highly sensitive to the exact weighting of the preference term. This stability is consistent with the joint learning objective, in which the MAIRL likelihood continues to ground the reward in demonstrated driving decisions while the preference term provides complementary ordinal supervision. The default 

λpref = 1 .0 achieves the lowest or tied-lowest reconstruction errors across the evaluated test-set settings, while the limited variation across the three settings indicates robustness to mod-erate changes in the strength of human feedback supervision. V. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we propose HFG-MAIRL, a human-feedback-guided multi-agent inverse reinforcement learning framework for interactive driving behavior modeling. HFG-MAIRL for-mulates interactive driving as a finite-horizon Markov game and learns context-dependent reward functions from multi-vehicle demonstrations. It models coupled vehicle interac-tions through iterative marginal-response inference, represents broader traffic influences using aggregated traffic states, in-troduces a virtual forward-safety proxy when no physical leader is explicitly represented, and incorporates available human feedback as complementary ordinal preference su-pervision beyond trajectory demonstrations. Experimental re-sults on the AIDE dataset show that HFG-MAIRL consis-tently improves reconstruction accuracy over representative IRL baselines across spatial, kinematic, and heading metrics. The learned reward-feature compositions and emotion-grouped reward distributions further reveal interpretable variations in driving preferences across interaction contexts associated with different human responses. This work suggests several directions for future research. First, we plan to integrate the learned human reward model into large-scale mixed-traffic simulations to investigate how human-aligned driving preferences influence traffic-level ef-ficiency, safety, and interaction dynamics. Second, we plan to incorporate continuous multimodal human-state signals, such as physiological and behavioral responses, to provide more detailed preference information and better reflect how human responses change over time during complex driving interactions. 12
