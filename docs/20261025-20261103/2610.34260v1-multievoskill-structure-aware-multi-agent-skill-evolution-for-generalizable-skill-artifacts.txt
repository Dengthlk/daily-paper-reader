# MultiEvoSkill: Structure-Aware Multi-Agent Skill Evolution for Generalizable Skill Artifacts 

Kounianhua Du* 1, Jianxing Liu* 1, Ruoshui Shan* 1, Yucheng Zhang* 3,Ke Li 2, Xing Sun 2, Weinan Zhang 11Shanghai Jiao Tong University, 2 Tencent, 3 Purdue Shanghai, China

Abstract 

Skill documents are becoming the default adap-tation surface for frozen large language model (LLM) agents, but current skill generators face two key limitations. First, humans and different LLMs differ in how they organize procedural knowledge and approach task solving. Skills authored from a human or single-model per-spective may therefore fail to align with the rea-soning behaviors of other backbones, limiting cross-model generalization. Second, sequen-tial skill descriptions inadequately represent procedures with parallel branches and explicit dependencies, while existing skill optimizers primarily operate on prose and lack explicit sup-port for graph-structured skill evolution. We introduce MultiEvoSkill, a skill-optimization framework comprising three core components: 1) Skill compiler casts the skill as a typed graph with tailored operators, producing a compact, informative representation with explicit proce-dural topology; 2) Heterogeneous agent sys-tem proposes revision advices using collective intelligence, where minimal and informative issues are identified based on multiple sets of experiences. 3) Structure-aware debate and evolution mechanism organizes candidate ed-its into a heterogeneous graph, resolves them under dependency and importance constraints, and distills the collective proposal into the ar-tifact. Multi-dimensional gating ensures the information gain and topological correctness of each evolution. Optimal skills are then ob-tained when the system converges at a consis-tent state. Various experiments and ablations against SOTA methods validate the effective-ness and generalization of MultiEvoSkill. 

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents externalise procedural knowledge into skills : reusable natural-language policies that can be loaded by a frozen backbone at run time. Anthropic’s A GENT SKILLS 

standard (Anthropic, 2025a,b) formalizes this inter-face, while recent work treats the skill document it-self as an optimizable artifact, improving it through execution feedback, trajectory distillation, or evo-lutionary search (Yang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Ni et al., 2026; Agrawal et al., 2025; Tan-jim et al., 2026). Unlike parameter updates, a skill is lightweight to edit, easy to inspect, and can in principle be reused by different frozen models with-out changing their weights or execution harnesses. This makes skill evolution a promising route to persistent agent improvement. Despite this promise, current skill optimization pipelines face two recurring limitations. First, most methods represent a skill as a linear prose docu-ment. Prose is convenient for LLMs to read and write, but its procedural relationships are not ex-posed as explicit, mechanically addressable objects. In high-reasoning-demand domains, such as mathe-matical reasoning, spreadsheet manipulation, work-flow generation, or tool-grounded question answer-ing, successful task solving often requires explicit dependencies among intermediate artifacts, con-straint checks, conditional decisions, and branch-specific recovery steps. Flattening such structure into a sequential paragraph obscures which steps are prerequisites, which branches are mutually ex-clusive, and which constraints must be preserved. The limitation is even more evident when cross-sample generalization is required within a single domain: different samples may require different procedural branches, even though they share the same high-level task family. A single prose trajec-tory tends to over-specify one path or ambiguously mix several paths, making it difficult to reuse the skill across heterogeneous samples. As illustrated in Figure 1a, the graph form makes procedural dependencies and task-conditioned branches ex-plicit, enabling more flexible reuse across heteroge-neous samples. Although recent work has explored structured skill libraries, pseudocode skills, and graph-based skill composition (Li et al., 2026a; 1arXiv:2610.34260v1 [cs.AI] 25 Oct 2026 Xia et al., 2026b; Bai et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026a; Huang and Huang, 2025), existing graph-oriented approaches primarily focus on inference-time composition, library organization, or program-matic skill networks. Our focus is the joint design of training-time graph editing, heterogeneous evi-dence aggregation, and validation-gated updates to one persistent artifact, rather than inference-local composition or library-level organization. Second, existing skill evolution is often driven by experience from a single backbone or a homo-geneous family of models. However, humans and LLMs do not organize procedural knowledge in the same way, and different LLM families exhibit different planning habits, abstraction levels, tool-use strategies, and failure modes. A skill authored by a human or optimized from one model’s tra-jectories may therefore encode model-specific rea-soning conventions that do not transfer to other backbones. Simply adding more models is not suf-ficient: a naive ensemble that sends every task to every model increases cost and may still fail to dis-till their complementary evidence into a reusable artifact. What is needed is an evolution mechanism that lets heterogeneous models collect distinct ex-perience, critique and refine each other’s diagnoses, and consolidate their collective intelligence into a single skill that remains useful when deployed without an inference-time ensemble. Figure 1b con-trasts single-model skill evolution with collective evolution, where heterogeneous agents exchange complementary evidence and consolidate it into a shared, reusable skill artifact. We introduce MultiEvoSkill, a structure-aware multi-agent framework for evolving generalizable skill artifacts. MultiEvoSkill represents a skill as a typed attributed directed multigraph whose nodes encode actions, decisions, artifacts, and constraints, and whose edges encode ordering, branching, pro-duction, consumption, and semantic dependency relations. This representation makes the topology of a task-solving procedure explicit. It allows one skill to express complex reasoning structures and to activate different branches for different samples within the same domain. On top of this representation, MultiEvoSkill evolves the skill through a heterogeneous multi-agent pipeline. 2 Related Work 

Skill representations and adaptation surfaces. 

File-based natural-language skills externalise pro-cedural knowledge without modifying model pa-rameters, providing a persistent adaptation sur-face formalised by Anthropic’s A GENT SKILLS 

standard (Anthropic, 2025a,b) and adopted by systems that accumulate reusable experience in skill libraries (Wang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2026a). More explicit representations compile prose into typed programs or task-level graphs: Skill-as-Pseudocode introduces typed contracts (Li et al., 2026a), while GraSP constructs and locally repairs inference-time DAGs (Xia et al., 2026b). At a broader level, SkillDAG, PSN, and ASG-SI organise skills into persistent graphs or program-matic networks (Bai et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026a; Huang and Huang, 2025). 

Skill evolution mechanisms. Skill evolution con-tinuously improves persistent, reusable skill arte-facts by incorporating execution experience and retaining validated revisions for future reuse (Ding et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026b). Trajectory-distillation methods compare multiple complete trajectories to isolate reusable sequence-level pat-terns from run-specific noise. Execution-feedback methods instead revise the current skill directly from step-level signals within individual runs, such as errors, unmet requirements, or rewards (Ding et al., 2026). Multi-agent systems divide this process among executors, reflection analysts, pro-posers, and verifiers, while population-based meth-ods explore multiple candidates through muta-tion, recombination, or fitness-guided selection. Reinforcement-learning methods optimise skill generation, selection, or refinement policies using task- or reuse-level rewards accumulated across rollouts. Across these routes, text-space updates com-monly employ bounded insertion, replacement, deletion, or targeted rewriting; structured updates modify the corresponding nodes and relations. Can-didate acceptance is typically controlled by empir-ical improvement or verifier-based validity, while evolution terminates upon verifier satisfaction, lack of further gain, budget exhaustion, or population stability. 

Multi-agent methods for skill evolution. Indi-vidual LLMs are limited in exploring diverse skill updates, motivating multi-agent methods that ex-change information and refine candidate skills col-lectively. Within this framework, proposers gen-erate candidate answers, plans, skills, or edits; re-viewers provide critiques; verifiers assess candi-dates using execution evidence, rules, or scores; revisers modify candidates following critique or evaluation; executors run skills or workflows to produce trajectories, rewards, and errors; and spe-cialist agents contribute distinct domain or task-specific capabilities (Zhang et al., 2026b; Ma et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b). These roles define functional responsibilities, whereas multi-agent debate (MAD) provides an im-portant interaction paradigm in which peer agents iteratively critique and revise competing proposals (Du et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2024; Khan et al., 2024; Motger et al., 2026). Ef-fective debate benefits from diverse perspectives and calibrated evidence (Zhu et al., 2026), and commonly terminates through consensus or stabil-ity criteria (Yazici et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2025). Agent contributions are subsequently reconciled through response fusion, patch consolidation, or candidate selection (Jiang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Ni et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Hwang et al., 2026).

3.2 Skill Compiler 

The compiler represents procedural knowledge with explicit graph objects rather than leaving all relationships implicit in prose. Natural language still supplies node instructions and edge annota-tions; graph structure makes these relationships addressable by the optimizer. 

Definition 3.1 (Typed attributed skill graph) . A skill is a directed multigraph 

G = ( V, E, A, R, ψ, Φ, α ), where ψ : V → A 

and Φ : E → R assign node and edge types, and α associates attributes with graph elements. Nodes represent actions, decisions, artifacts, or constraints; edges represent ordering, branching, production, consumption, or constraint relations. Node attributes encode local instructions and applicability conditions. Edge attributes encode guards and dependency explanations. The executor consumes a rendered representation of the skill. In our implementation, a candidate graph is accepted only if all node and edge references resolve, the graph is acyclic, every executable node is reachable from START and can reach END, and all node and edge types satisfy the declared schema. Table 1 illustrates the correspondence. A para-graph can describe a subgraph, so this is not an invertible prose-to-graph mapping or a span-level source map.

4.3 Main Results (RQ1) 

MultiEvoSkill obtains the highest reported score on every column. These results demonstrate the exceptional generalization capacity of our frame-work. The superior performance is primarily driven by two core methodological designs: 1) Multi-EvoSkill utilizes topological representation via typed skill graphs instead of traditional linear prose or trajectory-based methods. The explicit topolog-ical structure enables executors to strictly follow prerequisite constraints and branch guards, while allowing for localized, graph-grounded edits to oc-cur during the evolution process without introduc-ing non-local side effects or corrupting orthogonal execution branches. 2) MultiEvoSkill overcomes the biases and blind spots of a single model fam-ily through its multi-agent and -role pipeline. By leveraging contrasting execution trajectories and cross-family peer debates, cognitive weaknesses or missing parameter constraints in one model are systematically compensated by the complementary strengths of other models, ultimately crystallizing robust, high-quality skill artifacts into a unified skill graph. 

4.3.1 (RQ2) Case Study on Skill Forms 

Consider a table-manipulation task family (Table 3) in which samples share an initial inspection step but require different procedures: exact lookup, ag-gregation, formula preservation, or conditional for-matting. A prose-form skill can describe all four branches, yet the executor must infer their con-trol flow and dependencies from text, potentially mixing irrelevant operations, computing before re-solving the target range, or interpreting conditions such as “preserve formulas when possible” incon-sistently across backbones. Repairs may also have non-local effects: adding “always preserve for-mulas” to address one failure can interfere with tasks that explicitly require formula replacement. A typed skill graph instead represents actions, de-cisions, intermediate artifacts, constraints, and vali-dation checks as separately addressable nodes, con-nected by control-flow, data-flow, prerequisite, and branch-guard edges. 5 Conclusion 

We introduce MultiEvoSkill, a structure-aware multi-agent framework that distills heterogeneous execution experience into a single reusable skill artifact for frozen LLM agents. Its typed graph rep-resentation makes procedural branches and depen-dencies explicit targets for revision, while evidence-grounded reflection, issue-specific debate, and hi-erarchical patch consolidation integrate comple-mentary corrections. Multi-dimensional gating combines behavioral evaluation with structural and semantic checks to control candidate acceptance. MultiEvoSkill achieves the highest reported scores across the five benchmarks. Leave-one-out ablation validates the effectiveness of the collective intelli-gence and evolution mechanism. Ablation on gat-ings justify the effectiveness of each gating config-uration. Together, these findings support structured collective skill evolution as a promising approach to persistent agent improvement. 

Limitations 

A structural validator checks graph admissibility, not all intended task semantics. An LLM topol-ogy jury can miss unsupported dependencies or reject useful edits. Positive gains on a repeatedly reused selection set can overfit that set, and a state-similarity stopping rule does not imply optimality. The graph is a procedural representation rather than a formally verified execution engine.
