Abstract 

Interactive environments offer a practical way to evaluate large language model (LLM) agents, and competitive games extend this evaluation to decisions shaped by competing goals and other agents’ choices. Yet existing evaluations confined to individual games or execution frameworks leave un-clear which competitive advantages persist across settings. We introduce DECE (Diverse Eurogame 

Competitive Environments), a unified arena spanning ten Eurogames with shared interfaces and evalua-tion protocols. These games combine lasting strategic commitments, competition over shared resources, and multiple routes to victory. Our primary study evaluates 12 model–harness configurations, spanning nine models and four execution harnesses, in 480 three-player matches across ten games. The main and supplementary studies comprise 1,032 completed matches in total. These comparisons reveal broad cross-game ranking agreement alongside game-specific strengths. Matched studies show that the same model can perform differently across execution harnesses, adding another source of variation to these profiles. These performance advantages do not consistently translate into better token or cost efficiency, making resource use an additional consideration when comparing configurations. Together, the findings support evaluating complete agent configurations by both their competitive performance across games and the resources needed to achieve it. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly act through tools in external environments (Yao et al., 2023). Interactive benchmarks evaluate their decisions over extended trajectories (Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). In competition, opponents pursue their own objectives and change an agent’s available oppor-tunities. An action’s value depends on both its future consequences and opponents’ choices. We ask: 

How can competitive environments support reliable evaluation of complete agent systems across diverse strategic demands? 

Strategy board games make this question tractable. They combine explicit rules, programmatically exe-cutable states and actions, and independently verifiable outcomes (Gaina et al., 2020; Balla et al., 2024). Their interactions can be represented as partially observable stochastic games (POSGs) (Hansen et al., 1arXiv:2610.35773v1 [cs.AI] 26 Oct 2026 2004). Agents act on their observation histories; their actions determine state transitions and outcomes. We focus on Eurogames (German-style strategy board games), which combine persistent investments, com-petition over shared opportunities, and multiple routes to victory. In Splendor , development cards reduce later purchase costs; Azul placements constrain future spatial scoring (Space Cowboys, n.d.; Next Move Games, n.d.). These commitments shape both a player’s future options and opponents’ opportunities. Existing multi-game benchmarks and harness studies provide foundations for competitive evaluation (Costarelli et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2026). Turning these games into a common benchmark presents three challenges. Agents need consistent observation–action interfaces across diverse rules and information struc-tures, while retaining their tools and context management. Analysis must distinguish recurring competitive strength from game-specific advantages and account for execution settings and resource use. Reliable comparisons require consistent rule enforcement, a common participant roster, seat-balanced protocols, and verifiable outcomes. # 2 Related Work 

Games provide a natural setting for evaluating LLM agents because they combine explicit rules, sequential interaction, and verifiable outcomes. Recent benchmarks span both digital and tabletop environments: Orak, BALROG, and LMGame-Bench evaluate agents across video games (Park et al., 2026; Paglieri et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026a) , while GameBench and MindGames study competitive and social reasoning in board, card, and strategy games (Costarelli et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026). FinBoardBench studies financial decisions and PTCG-Bench examines card play, harness ablations, self-evolution, and cost (Hu et al., 2026b; Hua et al., 2026). Complementary work such as TAG and From Code to Play provides executable tabletop environments or evaluates components used by search-based players (Gaina et al., 2020; Eberhardinger et al., 2024), while LudoBench studies reasoning from fixed game states (Peper et al., 2026). 

Position of DECE. DECE compares complete agent configurations across ten Eurogames with persistent investments, spatial commitments, and multiple scoring routes. A common roster measures cross-game agreement and local strengths; matched studies assess harness contrasts, and game-specific frontiers relate performance to dollars and tokens. Table 1 compares the benchmark scope. 

# 3 DECE: Benchmark Design and Evaluation Protocol

3.2 Unified game interface and participant abstraction 

All games share an interface for setup, seat-specific observations, legal actions, state transitions, and terminal rankings. The engine enforces rules and scoring. Simultaneous choices use a frozen state and advance only after all required seats submit actions. Public information, private state, and unrevealed randomness remain separate. MCP and native adapters expose this interface; persisted states and execution records support auditing, replay, and resume (Appendix A.3). Each participant specifies a model snapshot, execution harness, prompts, tools, memory scope, inference settings, and resource budget. The shared interface permits different internal histories and orchestration strategies. The primary panel contains 12 configurations spanning nine model identities and four harnesses (Appendix A.2). # 4 Experimental Evaluation 

4.1 Cohorts and uncertainty 

The main comparison contains 480 three-player matches across ten games: 144 from the original Splendor, 7 Wonders, and Brass cohort, plus 336 from the seven-game expansion. Each game contributes 48 matches and 12 appearances per participant, balanced across seats. Another 252 two- or four-player matches support player-count analysis, giving 732 native-system matches in total. All outcomes reconcile with schedules and final records (Appendix D.7). Table 3 reports the three-player cohort; PWS means weight games equally. Its column groups follow Figure 2. Pointwise 95% intervals use 5,000 whole-match bootstrap draws per game, recomputing PWS and refitting Elo while retaining the ten games (Appendix D.3). We completed 180 matches for three non-LLM algorithms—Random, Heuristic, and ISMCTS—in separate 60-match cohorts. Their PWS scores gauge their approximate competitive standing relative to the 12 LLM participants (Table 3). Each cohort contains six three-player matches per game against two LLM opponents. The three cohorts use identical games, rules, setup seeds, opponent configurations, and seat assignments; only the baseline algorithm changes (Appendix D.8). Two independent studies cross OpenCode and Qwen Code in Splendor and 7 Wonders. The 72-match bridge pairs DSV4-Flash with Qwen3.8-Flash across nine scenarios per game; the 48-match within-Qwen-family study pairs Qwen3.8-Flash with Qwen3.8-Max across six. Within each study, those models under OpenCode serve as anchors. Each scenario fixes the setup seed and focal seat across four matches, with balanced seats. Pointwise intervals resample whole scenarios. Each study retains its own anchors and analysis, separate from the native ratings (Appendices G and G.3).

Advantages recur across selected strategic settings. Qwen3.8-Max/Qwen Code ranks first by Elo in Azul, Harmonies, and Carcassonne, and second in Burgundy. All four combine prominent spatial con-straints with public current holdings under the rubric in Figure 2; their equal-game PWS is 84.9%. GPT-5.6-Terra/Codex shows a different concentration: it ranks third, second, and fourth in Century, Brass, and Ark Nova (75.0% mean PWS), but eleventh, tenth, and seventh in Azul, Harmonies, and Carcassonne (36.8%). Its ten-game mean of 50.2% conceals this separation. Together with the Qwen3.8-Max profile, this illus-trates how similar aggregate summaries can arise from markedly different game-level performance patterns. 

A single mechanism does not summarize the profile. Brass and Ark Nova also have prominent spatial constraints, but Qwen3.8-Max/Qwen Code ranks sixth and eighth there. Conversely, GPT-5.6-Terra/Codex’s strong results in several economic systems do not extend to all engine-building games: it ranks tenth in Splendor and sixth in Wingspan. Averaging GPT-5.6-Terra over all spatial games gives 51.0% PWS, versus 49.0% elsewhere, masking substantial differences within that group. These counterexamples motivate ex-amining combinations of commitments, information, and action dependencies rather than assigning a model one domain label.

These frequencies indicate how often a configuration remains a candidate when the ob-served matches are resampled. They distinguish frequently retained options from positions that change with the sampled outcomes. 

Token frontiers separate usage from pricing. Using mean input-plus-output tokens yields one to six frontier configurations per game (Figure 10). GPT-5.6-Luna/Codex appears on three token frontiers, compared with ten cost frontiers; DSV4-Pro/DSH appears on two, compared with ten. Kimi-K2.7-Code/OpenCode shows the reverse pattern, appearing on six token frontiers and no dollar frontiers. The different candidate sets reflect both token consumption and the fixed rates applied to input, cached input, 10 and output. Reporting both measures makes these sources of resource efficiency visible. Token usage is measured in each model’s native tokenizer (Appendix E). 

# 5 Discussion and Conclusion 

DECE finds both cross-game ranking agreement and local differences in competitive performance. The games share persistent investments, contested opportunities, and alternative scoring routes, while differing in rules and information structure. The shared roster reveals where advantages recur and where they reverse. Aggregate rankings summarize broad competitiveness, while game-level profiles identify settings in which a configuration becomes more or less effective. 

Complete systems as the evaluation unit. Matched comparisons connect these profiles to agent exe-cution. The same model can perform differently across harnesses under matched scenarios. Competitive results characterize the complete model–harness configuration. Reporting tools, context management, and execution settings makes that system explicit. Cross-game comparisons show whether configuration gains recur or concentrate in particular strategic settings. 

From profiles to configuration selection. The resource frontiers identify candidate configurations for a game and budget. Future work can select configurations from game rules and results on training games. Evaluation should compare against the best generalist selected on those games, keeping target-game out-comes out of tuning. Applying the same budget to both methods would test whether game-specific selection improves competitive performance at a given resource cost. Token and dollar budgets would test distinct deployment constraints. 

Limitations. The native and matched studies evaluate complete configurations within their respective game and opponent panels. Component interventions, combined with action–outcome analyses, can test how planning and memory contribute to these performance differences. DECE combines executable Eurogames, common tournament protocols, and resource measurements linked to competitive outcomes. Together, these elements support evaluation that reports where complete agent configurations perform well, how execution choices change their results, and what resources they require. 11 References

Figure 2 describes the selected games using six rule-based annotations. The authors assigned ordinal values from publisher rules and the corresponding observation interfaces. RQ1 uses these annotations to organize descriptive comparisons, with group definitions in Appendix D.4. 

Annotation rubric. Each axis uses the following anchors. A value of zero means that the defined mecha-nism is absent, not that a game requires no reasoning. Hidden information concerns current private holdings; uncertainty about future random draws is excluded. Spatial planning concerns geometry and connectivity, not the mere presence of rows in a tableau. 

EC: Engine construction. 0: No persistent production or action modifier; 1: Localized recurring benefit or modifier; 2: Acquired assets repeatedly change action yields, costs, or available conversions. 

SP: Spatial planning. 0: No geometric adjacency or connectivity constraint; 1: Geometry affects a sub-sidiary mechanism; 2: Adjacency, connectivity, or packing is central to placement or scoring. 

AS: Action sequencing. 0: No material ordering dependency; 1: Resource or placement prerequisites cre-ate ordering constraints; 2: Explicit reuse, activation chains, action-strength cycles, or staged produc-tion dependencies. 

OC: Opponent coupling. 0: Independent boards and supplies; only final score comparison; 1: Shared offers, resource pools, or races; 2: Trade or shared infrastructure, contested ownership, neighbor military comparisons, or opponent-shaped compulsory leftovers. 

HI: Hidden information. 0: Current decision-relevant holdings public; future random draws excluded; 1: Limited or optional private holdings; 2: Private hands or goals are a recurrent central part of decisions. 

SC: Scoring coordination. 0: One principal additive scoring conversion; 1: Main scoring route plus a secondary bonus or threshold; 2: Overlapping, nonlinear, comparative, or staged scoring objectives.
